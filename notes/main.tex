\section{General Information}
\label{sec:general-information}

\paragraph{What this course is about.}
These notes accompany the lectures and cover the same ground as the four slide
decks (\texttt{slides/part1.pdf}--\texttt{part4.pdf}), one part per deck. They
are written to be read rather than presented, so within each part the material
follows the argument rather than the order of the slides. The goal is to use
\textbf{tensor networks} to exploit the low-rank structure of both the
solutions and the operators that define partial differential equations. Nothing
here is quantum: the networks are classical data structures, and every
computation runs on a laptop.

\paragraph{The single idea.}
A field sampled on $2^n$ points is a vector in a space of dimension $2^n$, but a
\emph{physical} field is not an arbitrary point in that space. It is smooth, it
is multiscale, and its well-separated scales are weakly coupled. Such a vector
lies close to a low-dimensional manifold, described by $O(n\chi^2)$ numbers
rather than $2^n$, where the bond dimension $\chi$ measures how much the scales
talk to each other. Every part of this course either exploits that fact, tests
it, or maps out where it stops being true.
\margintext{Keep the bet and its price together: exponential memory savings are
bought with a truncation error that must be monitored at every step. The
truncation never goes away.}

\paragraph{The pipeline.}
Solving a PDE with tensor networks is a pipeline of five stages, and each has
choices of its own:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Equation:} the PDE, or, in Part~\partref{4}, the data that stand
    in for it.
  \item \textbf{Problem formulation:} the mesh (uniform or not), the coordinate
    system and the discretization (finite differences, finite elements,
    finite volumes, \ldots).
  \item \textbf{TN encoding:} the network architecture, and how initial
    conditions, boundary conditions and operators are written in it.
  \item \textbf{Time evolution:} the integration scheme, the algebraic routines
    it needs, and the physical constraints and geometries it must respect.
  \item \textbf{Solution retrieval:} reading answers out of a compressed state
    held in classical memory --- no shots, but memory efficiency is still a
    question.
\end{enumerate}
The bond dimension $\chi$ runs through all five stages: it sets the cost of
every step and the accuracy of every answer. Each part opens by naming the
stages it covers, and the conclusion (\cref{sec:conclusion}) walks the pipeline
once more to record where $\chi$ stayed small and where it did not.

\paragraph{How the four parts fit together.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Part~\partref{1} --- Representing fields and operators.}
    Tensors and Penrose notation, matrix product states (tensor trains) built
    by SVD, a short history of tensor networks, quantics tensor trains for
    functions and operators, and a first solver: the heat equation with
    explicit time stepping.
  \item \textbf{Part~\partref{2} --- Linear algebra with tensor networks.}
    Implicit time stepping, which turns each step into a linear solve $Ax=b$
    on the low-rank manifold; the sweeping solvers and the cross interpolation
    that make this possible; encoding boundary and initial conditions; and the
    open problems, ending with nonlinearity.
  \item \textbf{Part~\partref{3} --- The forward problem.} Time evolution of the
    incompressible Navier--Stokes equations: flow initialization, masking
    arbitrary objects, and how far the ranks stretch.
  \item \textbf{Part~\partref{4} --- Learning dynamics from data.} Given
    snapshots rather than an equation, recover the dynamics: dynamic mode
    decomposition and sparse regression, both carried out in tensor-train
    format.
\end{itemize}

Parts~\partref{1} and~\partref{2} are prerequisites for the rest.
Parts~\partref{3} and~\partref{4} can be read in either order: Part~\partref{4}
refers to the Part~\partref{3} solver in two places, as an alternative to its
space--time solve and as the physical prior of hybrid methods, but does not
depend on its details.

\paragraph{Prerequisites.}
Linear algebra --- in particular the singular value decomposition and its
optimality properties --- and a first course in numerical methods for PDEs
(finite differences, explicit and implicit time stepping, stability). No
background in quantum mechanics is assumed anywhere; where a quantum-many-body
term is unavoidable, its numerical-analysis synonym is given alongside it in
\cref{subsec:mps}.

\paragraph{Notation and conventions.}
The same symbols mean the same things in every part.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $n$ is the number of cores (tensors) per encoded coordinate, so the grid
    has $N = 2^n$ points per coordinate.
  \item $\chi$ is the bond dimension of a state and $\chi_A$ that of an operator
    $A$. The numerical-analysis literature calls it the \textsc{tt-rank} and
    writes it $r$; these notes use $\chi$ throughout.
  \item $\varepsilon$ is a truncation tolerance. Time steps are indexed by $k$,
    with $K$ steps in total, and the state is $\mathbf u$.
  \item Physical legs point down in diagrams; virtual legs run horizontally.
  \item \enquote{Tensor train} (TT) is the name of the format; \enquote{matrix
    product state} (MPS) is its name in physics and is used for diagrams.
    \enquote{Quantics tensor train} (QTT) is used only when the binary
    indexing of the grid matters. \enquote{Bond dimension} and \enquote{rank}
    are synonyms.
  \item Terms from quantum many-body physics appear only in margin notes, next
    to their numerical-analysis equivalents.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Margin notes.}
\margintext{Like this one.}
Notes set in the margin carry historical context, dates and attributions, and
warnings that would interrupt the argument if placed in the main text. They are
commentary --- nothing in the main development depends on them.

\paragraph{Materials.}
The companion slides, the notebooks and the shared bibliography all live in the
course repository. Every quantitative claim in these notes is measured there,
on a laptop, and you are encouraged to disagree with any of them by rerunning
the cell.

Each part has one notebook, with exercises --- and their solutions --- placed
where each concept is introduced. Parts~\partref{1} and~\partref{2} build everything from scratch in
about five hundred lines of NumPy (\texttt{qtade\_tn.py}), because the mechanics
are the point; Parts~\partref{3} and~\partref{4} move to \texttt{quimb} for the
representation and compression, and keep the Part~\partref{2} solver, because no
tensor-network library ships a linear solver for $Ax=b$. That seam is itself
worth noticing.

\paragraph{Where the field is now.}
\margintext{Our own route through the subject: the 2024 framework fixed the
encoding and the projection loop; the 2026 turbulence study asked how far the
ranks stretch in 3D; the 2025 space--time paper removed the time loop
altogether. Parts~\partref{3} and~\partref{4} follow that order.}
The subject is moving quickly. Three-dimensional turbulence has been
compressed, simulated and synthesized at $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda = 315$ on a
$1024^3$ grid~\cite{Pisoni2026}; a two-dimensional compressible Euler solver
reached the finals of the Airbus--BMW Quantum Computing Challenge
2024~\cite{Peddinti2025}; space and time have been encoded in a single MPS,
which also underlies MPS-DMD~\cite{Peddinti2025a}; and cross interpolation now
has mature libraries, \texttt{xfac} in C++ and
\texttt{Tensor\-Cross\-Inter\-polation.jl} in Julia~\cite{Nunez2025}.
