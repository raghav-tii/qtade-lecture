Everything in this course rests on a single bet. A field sampled on a fine grid
is an enormous vector, but it is not an \emph{arbitrary} enormous vector: it is
smooth, it is multiscale, and the coupling between well-separated scales is
weak. If that is true, the vector lies on a manifold of far lower dimension than
the space it sits in, and we should compute on the manifold rather than in the
ambient space. We use tensor networks (TNs) to exploit the low-rank structure of
both the solutions and the operators that define a PDE --- classically, with no
quantum hardware involved.

Part~\partref{1} builds the machinery needed to state that bet precisely, in the
order of the slides: tensors and a diagrammatic language for them, together
with the scaling wall they are meant to break
(\cref{subsec:penrose-notation}); the matrix product state, obtained by
repeated SVDs (\cref{subsec:mps}); a short account of where the ideas came from
(\cref{subsec:literature-survey}); the binary re-indexing that makes the ansatz
multiscale, for functions and for operators
(\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}); and --- by the end --- a working PDE
solver whose cost is logarithmic in the number of grid points
(\cref{subsec:heat-explicit-euler}).

\subsection{Tensors and Penrose Notation}
\label{subsec:penrose-notation}
% Keywords: tensor = multidimensional array, curse of dimensionality,
% structured tensors, diagram notation, contraction, trace, contraction cost.

\paragraph{What is a tensor?}
A tensor is a multi-dimensional array, the generalisation of a vector ($v_i$, one
index) and a matrix ($M_{ij}$, two indices) to any number of indices. The
number of indices is the \textbf{order} of the tensor. Tensors occur wherever
data is labelled by several independent attributes: images (pixel $x$, pixel
$y$, colour channel), medical records (age, height, weight, symptom), the
discretisation of a function of several variables, and many-body quantum
wavefunctions.

\paragraph{The curse of dimensionality.}
\label{subsec:numerical-methods-intro}
Tensors beyond a few indices become \emph{exponentially} costly to store and to
manipulate. An order-6 tensor $T_{n_1 n_2 \cdots n_6}$ with $n_j = 1,\dots,10$
already has $10^6$ entries; $N$ indices give a number of entries exponential in
$N$. Grid-based PDE solvers meet the same wall:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Discretize a field on a mesh with $N = 2^n$ points per dimension: in $d$
    dimensions that is $2^{nd}$ unknowns.
  \item A $1024^3$ mesh is $2^{30} \approx 10^9$ unknowns --- \emph{per field,
    per timestep}.
  \item Three-dimensional direct numerical simulation needs
    $\sim\!\mathrm{Re}^{9/4}$ degrees of freedom
    (\cref{subsec:navier-stokes}). An aircraft at cruise sits near
    $\mathrm{Re} \sim 10^{7}$, so $\sim\!10^{16}$ degrees of freedom. Exascale
    hardware does not close a gap of that size; it moves it by a couple of
    orders of magnitude.
\end{itemize}

\margintext{Every classical accelerator --- adaptive meshes, multigrid, spectral
methods, POD and reduced-order models --- exploits structure. The
tensor-network ansatz is another one, not a different kind of thing.}
Finite differences, finite volumes and finite elements all change the constant,
not the scaling. The only way out is \emph{structure}.

\paragraph{Structured tensors.}
Tensor networks are a systematic way of breaking the curse. The smallest
example: a function $f(x,y,z)$ of three binary variables
$x,y,z \in \{0,1\}$ is an order-3 tensor with $8$ entries. If it happens to
factorize, $f(x,y,z) = p(x)\,q(y)\,r(z)$, then $6$ numbers suffice, and any
entry --- say $f(0,1,0) = p(0)\,q(1)\,r(0)$ --- is recovered by contracting the
factors. A general order-$N$ tensor is drawn as one node with $N$ legs; a
tensor network replaces that node by many small nodes joined by bonds, and the
compression comes from the bonds being narrow.

\paragraph{Tensor diagram notation.}
Index expressions with five or six summations are correct and unreadable.
Diagrams (Penrose, 1971~\cite{Penrose1971}) are the working notation of the
field for a concrete reason: they carry exactly the same information, and they
make the \emph{cost} of a computation something you can see~\cite{Bridgeman2017}.
\margintext{Diagrammatic tensor notation is due to Penrose (1971); it entered
numerical practice through the DMRG community in the 1990s. Bridgeman and Chubb
(2017) and \texttt{tensornetwork.org} are readable modern introductions.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A tensor is a \textbf{node}; every index is a \textbf{leg}. A vector
    $v_j$ has one leg, a matrix $M_{ij}$ two, an order-3 tensor $T_{ijk}$ three;
    a scalar has none.
  \item There is no ambiguity, and the names of indices can be omitted: the
    diagram carries precisely what the index expression carries.
  \item Covariant and contravariant indices can be told apart by ingoing and
    outgoing arrows; special tensors get special shapes (isometries are drawn
    as triangles).
\end{itemize}

There are only two moves, and every construction in this course is assembled
from them: \emph{place nodes} and \emph{join legs}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Joined legs} --- sum over the shared index. Examples: the inner
    product $\sum_j v_j w_j$ (two nodes, one bond), the matrix--vector product
    $\sum_j M_{ij} v_j$, and an order-3 tensor contracted with a vector,
    $\sum_j T_{ijk} v_j$.
  \item \textbf{Dangling legs} are the free indices of the result. Counting them
    tells you what object you have built.
  \item \textbf{Closed loop} --- no free legs, so the diagram is a number: a
    trace, $\sum_j M_{jj}$ or $\sum_{ij} A_{ij}B_{ji} = \operatorname{Tr}[AB]$.
\end{itemize}

A small vocabulary of recurring nodes covers most diagrams you will meet.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Identity} --- a bare line.
  \item \textbf{Copy (or $\delta$) tensor} --- a branching node, one index shared
    by three legs.
  \item \textbf{Isometry} --- drawn as a triangle. A tensor $A$ reshaped into a
    matrix is an isometry when $A^{\dagger}A = \mathbb{1}$, so contracting it
    with its conjugate collapses to a bare line.
\end{itemize}

\begin{notation}
  Throughout these notes physical legs point \textbf{down} and virtual (bond)
  legs run \textbf{horizontally}. Conventions differ between papers; a diagram
  only means something once the convention is fixed.
\end{notation}

\begin{remark}[Order is cost]
  The diagram fixes \emph{what} is computed, not \emph{in which order}, and the
  order sets the cost. Contracting the same three-tensor network pairwise in two
  different orders can cost $O(D^4)+O(D^4)$ or $O(D^5)+O(D^4)$ for bond
  dimension $D$; for $A_{ij}B_{jk}C_{kl}v_l$ the order $((AB)C)v$ costs
  $O(D^3)$ while $A(B(Cv))$ costs $O(D^2)$. Take the chain of $n$ order-3
  tensors of \cref{subsec:mps}, each of bond dimension $\chi$ and physical
  dimension $d$: contract it sequentially and the cost is $O(n \chi^3)$;
  contract it by first forming the full $d^{\,n}$-component vector and the cost
  is $O(d^{\,n})$. Same diagram, same numbers out, exponentially different
  bills. Finding the optimal order for a \emph{general} network is
  NP-hard~\cite{Gray2021}; for a chain it is obvious.
\end{remark}

\subsection{MPS or TT via SVD}
\label{subsec:mps}
% Keywords: low-rank structure, SVD, Eckart-Young, truncation by rank or by
% error, recursive SVD (TT-SVD), MPS/tensor train, primitives (sum, product,
% inner product, MPO), canonical form, rounding.

\paragraph{The problem and the ways out.}
We cannot store or manipulate tensors with many indices. There are three ways
out: \emph{sparsity} (when applicable), \emph{sampling}, and \emph{low-rank
structure}. Tensor networks take the third, and for matrices uncovering low-rank
structure is straightforward: it is the singular value decomposition.

\paragraph{The SVD.}
Any matrix factorizes as
\begin{equation}
  M = U S V^{\dagger}, \qquad U^{\dagger}U = \mathbb{1}_r, \quad
  V^{\dagger}V = \mathbb{1}_r,
  \label{eq:svd}
\end{equation}%
where $S$ is diagonal and holds the singular values $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2
\ge \dots \ge \lambda_r > 0$: real, non-negative and decreasingly ordered.
Grouping $A = U\sqrt{S}$ and $B = \sqrt{S}V^{\dagger}$ writes $M$ as two nodes
joined by a bond that runs over $r = \operatorname{rank}(M)$ values.
\textbf{Truncating} the smallest singular values, $M \approx U\tilde{S}V^{\dagger}
= \tilde{A}\tilde{B}$, gives the optimal approximation of that rank in the
$\ell_2$ (and Frobenius) norm --- the Eckart--Young theorem.

\begin{example}[A $4\times 3$ matrix]
  The matrix
  \begin{equation*}
    M = \begin{pmatrix}
      0.435839 & 0.223707 & \phantom{-}0.10\\
      0.435839 & 0.223707 & -0.10\\
      0.223707 & 0.435839 & \phantom{-}0.10\\
      0.223707 & 0.435839 & -0.10
    \end{pmatrix}
  \end{equation*}
  has singular values $(0.933,\ 0.300,\ 0.200)$, with $U$ built from
  $\pm\tfrac12$ entries and $V$ from $(1,\pm1)/\sqrt2$ and $(0,0,1)$. Dropping
  the smallest singular value gives a rank-2 approximation with
  $\|M_{\chi=2} - M\|^2 = 0.2^2$; dropping two gives
  $\|M_{\chi=1}-M\|^2 = 0.2^2 + 0.3^2$. The squared error is exactly the sum of
  the squared discarded singular values.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Two compression strategies.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Fix a \textbf{maximum allowed rank} $\chi$. This defines the
    approximation error
    \begin{equation}
      \epsilon = \Big(\sum_{j > \chi} \lambda_j^2\Big)^{1/2}.
      \label{eq:trunc-error}
    \end{equation}
  \item Fix a \textbf{maximum allowed error} $\epsilon$. This defines the
    smallest $\chi$ such that
    $\epsilon \ge \big(\sum_{j > \chi} \lambda_j^2\big)^{1/2}$.
\end{itemize}
Both are used in practice, and we write a truncation as $(\chi,\epsilon)$.

\paragraph{Recursive SVD on a tensor: TT-SVD.}
To compress a tensor with $N$ legs of dimension $d$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item reshape the tensor as a matrix (first leg against all the others);
  \item apply the SVD to the matrix, and keep $U_1$ as the first node;
  \item glue together (multiply) $S_1 V_1^{T}$ and reshape it (bond and second
    leg against the rest);
  \item sweep through the tensor, repeating 2--3 until the last leg.
\end{enumerate}
The result is a chain of order-3 tensors.

\begin{definition}[Matrix product state / tensor train]
  A tensor with $N$ legs is written as a chain of order-3 tensors,
  \begin{equation}
    v_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_N}
      = A^{(i_1)}_1 A^{(i_2)}_2 \cdots A^{(i_N)}_N ,
    \label{eq:mps}
  \end{equation}%
  where each $A^{(i_k)}_k$ is a matrix of size at most $\chi \times \chi$, with
  $A_1$ a row vector and $A_N$ a column vector so the product is a scalar. The
  \textbf{bond dimension} $\chi$ is the size of the virtual legs. Physicists
  call~\eqref{eq:mps} a matrix product state (MPS), numerical analysts a tensor
  train (TT).
\end{definition}

The parameter count of~\eqref{eq:mps} is at most $N d\chi^2$ rather than $d^N$.
That is exponential compression --- \emph{provided $\chi$ stays bounded as $N$
grows}. Almost everything that follows is about when that proviso holds.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Truncating small singular values at each of the $N-1$ cuts is
    \textbf{quasi-optimal}: the result is within a factor $\sqrt{N-1}$ of the
    best approximation with the same ranks in the Frobenius
    norm~\cite{Oseledets2011}. The $\sqrt{N-1}$ comes from accumulating $N-1$
    independent truncation errors in quadrature.
  \item TT-SVD is exact and fully controlled --- but it needs the dense tensor
    in memory, so it is practical only for modest sizes.
    \Cref{subsec:finding-qtt} returns to this with methods that build an MPS
    \emph{without} ever forming the full array.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why an MPS can work at all.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Cut the chain at bond $k$. The bond dimension needed there equals the
    rank of the tensor reshaped into a matrix across that cut.
  \item That rank is governed by the \textbf{decay of the singular values} of
    the reshaped matrix. Physicists read the same quantity as the entanglement
    between the two halves.
  \item For ground states of gapped one-dimensional local Hamiltonians an
    \textbf{area law} bounds that entanglement independently of system
    size~\cite{Hastings2007}, hence $\chi$ independent of $N$.
  \item Smooth, multiscale functions behave the same way. Noise and random data
    do not: their singular values decay slowly or not at all.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Primitives.}
An MPS is useful because it can be manipulated efficiently without ever being
expanded:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{sum} --- the cores are stacked block-diagonally, so $\chi$ is
    sub-additive: $\chi_{a+b} \le \chi_a + \chi_b$;
  \item \textbf{element-wise multiplication} --- $\chi$ multiplies,
    $\chi_{a\odot b} \le \chi_a\chi_b$ (recursive sketching algorithms avoid
    forming the product in full);
  \item \textbf{perfect sampling} of configurations with probability
    $\propto |v_{i_1\cdots i_N}|^2$;
  \item \textbf{linear system solvers} (Part~\partref{2});
  \item \textbf{inner product} --- zip the two chains together from one end,
    absorbing one pair of cores at a time into a $\chi\times\chi$ environment,
    at cost $O(N d\chi^3)$. The inner product of two vectors with a
    \emph{trillion} entries each ($N=40$ cores with $d=2$) takes about
    $10\,$ms at $\chi = 100$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{definition}[Matrix product operator]
  The operator analogue of an MPS (a TT operator): a chain in which each tensor
  carries two physical legs, one incoming and one outgoing, in addition to its
  two virtual bonds.
\end{definition}

Contracting an MPO with an MPS leg by leg (the \textbf{MPO--MPS contraction})
returns an MPS whose bond dimension is the product $\chi\,\chi_{\mathrm{MPO}}$;
the cost is $O(N d^2 \chi^2 \chi_{\mathrm{MPO}}^2)$.

More advanced algorithms, met later in the course or beyond it:
canonicalization (fixing the gauge freedom), belief-propagation gauging, tensor
cross interpolation (TCI: query the tensor to sketch it; \cref{subsec:finding-qtt}),
the multiscale interpolative construction~\cite{Lindsey2023}, and time
evolution (TEBD, TDVP; Part~\partref{3}).

\paragraph{Canonical forms and rounding.}
\margintext{Gauge freedom is not a nuisance to tolerate; fixing it is what makes
truncation optimal and inner products cheap. In practice you carry the
orthogonality centre along with the state.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The factorization~\eqref{eq:mps} is not unique: insert $G G^{-1}$ on any
    bond and nothing changes. Fixing this \textbf{gauge freedom} gives the
    canonical forms.
  \item A tensor is \textbf{left-orthogonal} when
    $\sum_{i} A^{(i)\dagger} A^{(i)} = \mathbb{1}$, and right-orthogonal under
    the mirror condition. Sweeping QR from the left and from the right produces
    left, right and \textbf{mixed} canonical form, the last carrying a single
    non-orthogonal tensor called the orthogonality centre. The $U_k$ produced by
    TT-SVD are exactly left-orthogonal.
  \item In mixed canonical form the environment on either side of the centre is
    the identity. Truncating the centre by SVD is then optimal for that bond.
  \item \textbf{Rounding is the workhorse of the subject.} Every operation
    inflates ranks --- a sum to $\chi_1 + \chi_2$, an element-wise product to
    $\chi_1\chi_2$, an MPO application to $\chi\chi_{\mathrm{MPO}}$ --- so
    recompress back to $(\chi,\epsilon)$ once per step, every step, at cost
    $O(N\chi^3)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Historical Perspective on Tensor Networks}
\label{subsec:literature-survey}
% Keywords: transfer matrices, finitely correlated states, DMRG, MPS
% formalism, tensor-train rediscovery, other TNs (TTN, PEPS, MERA),
% applications, libraries, TNs for fluids.

A short orientation, partly because the subject has an unusual history. MPS
emerged in \textbf{quantum many-body physics}, to study one-dimensional spin
chains. The same ansatz was then reinvented in numerical analysis, and
\emph{both} vocabularies remain in active use.

\paragraph{Early precursors in physics (1940s--1980s).}
Kramers and Wannier (1941) introduced transfer matrices for the
two-dimensional Ising model --- early matrix product operators. Baxter
(1968, 1981) developed corner transfer matrices and variational wavefunctions
resembling MPS. Fannes, Nachtergaele and Werner (1992) formally defined the
finitely correlated states of quantum spin chains, establishing mathematically
what are now called MPS~\cite{Fannes1992}.

\paragraph{The DMRG breakthrough (1992--2000s).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{DMRG} --- White's density matrix renormalization
    group~\cite{White1992}: a variational algorithm in the MPS manifold, and a
    massive numerical breakthrough for ground states of one-dimensional local
    gapped Hamiltonians.
  \item Östlund and Rommer (1995) realized that DMRG implicitly assumes the
    ground state to be an MPS~\cite{Ostlund1995}.
  \item Vidal, Verstraete and Cirac (2000s) standardized MPS as a language for
    entanglement, time evolution and one-dimensional many-body
    systems~\cite{Vidal2003,Vidal2004,Verstraete2008}. The MPS is an
    \emph{entanglement-informed} ansatz: the area law~\cite{Hastings2007}
    explains why $\chi$ can stay bounded. The standard reference for canonical
    forms and sweeping is~\cite{Schollwoeck2011}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Independent discovery as tensor trains (2000s--present).}
Oseledets and Tyrtyshnikov independently rediscovered the same structure in
numerical linear algebra and named it the tensor train
decomposition~\cite{Oseledets2011}; the quantics variant~\cite{Khoromskij2011}
and cross approximation~\cite{Oseledets2010} followed. Applied mathematics then
brought the tools to data science, machine learning and high-dimensional
differential equations.

\margintext{Note the seventeen-year gap between White (1992) and Oseledets
(2011). The numerical-analysis rediscovery was independent; the dictionaries
were matched up only afterwards.}
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Quantum many-body} & \textsc{Numerical analysis} \\
    \midrule
    Matrix product state (MPS)   & Tensor train (TT) \\
    Matrix product operator (MPO) & TT operator / TT matrix \\
    Bond dimension $\chi$        & TT-rank \\
    Entanglement across a cut    & Singular-value decay \\
    DMRG sweep                   & Alternating least squares \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The columns denote the same objects and the same algorithms. For a review from
the physics side see~\cite{Orus2014}; from the numerical-linear-algebra side,
\cite{Oseledets2011}.

\paragraph{Other tensor networks: expressivity versus cost.}
The MPS is the simplest member of a family, and the family is organised by a
trade-off between expressivity and computational cost.
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Network} & \textsc{Loops} & \textsc{Correlations} & \textsc{Cost} \\
    \midrule
    MPS / TT                     & none: canonical form & exponential decay & $\chi^3$ \\
    Tree TN / hierarchical Tucker & none: canonical form & critical          & $\chi^4$ \\
    PEPS, MERA                   & loopy: no canonical form & critical      & up to $\chi^8$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
The MPS has by far the largest toolbox of efficient algorithms and known MPOs,
which is why this course uses it throughout. Tree tensor networks are a natural
next step for multivariate functions~\cite{Tindall2024a}. A loopy network has no
canonical form obtainable by single-tensor operations, and its contraction
becomes a hard optimization problem in its own right~\cite{Gray2021}.

\paragraph{Applications and libraries.}
Tensor networks are used for functions of continuous variables
(\enquote{quantum-inspired} numerics, the subject of this course), machine
learning (compressing models, parametrizing networks), probability and
statistics, quantum physics (simulation, error mitigation, process tensors),
out-of-equilibrium statistical physics and stochastic chemical kinetics.
Mature libraries include ITensor, TeNPy, TensorNetwork, \texttt{quimb} (used in
the notebooks) and \texttt{cotengra} for contraction-order
optimization~\cite{Gray2021}.

\paragraph{Tensor networks meet fluids.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scale correlations are weak} in turbulence, so $\chi$ stays
    modest~\cite{Gourianov2022}.
  \item \textbf{Kinetic plasmas.} Vlasov--Poisson solved in MPS
    form~\cite{Ye2022}.
  \item \textbf{Reduced-order models.} Wall-bounded flows at a few percent of
    the DNS variable count~\cite{Kiffner2023}.
  \item \textbf{Turbulence PDFs} computed directly in tensor-network form,
    without sampling the full field~\cite{Gourianov2025}.
  \item \textbf{A full stack.} Incompressible flow around arbitrary immersed
    geometry, end to end~\cite{Peddinti2024}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where the field is now.}
\margintext{Our own route through the subject: the 2024 framework fixed the
encoding and the projection loop; the 2026 turbulence study asked how far the
ranks stretch in 3D; the 2025 space--time paper removed the time loop
altogether. Parts~\partref{3} and~\partref{4} follow that order.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{3D turbulence} --- compression, simulation and synthesis at
    $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda = 315$ on a $1024^3$ grid~\cite{Pisoni2026}.
  \item \textbf{Compressible flow} --- a 2D Euler solver, finalist in the
    Airbus--BMW Quantum Computing Challenge 2024~\cite{Peddinti2025}.
  \item \textbf{Space--time and data-driven} --- space and time in a single MPS,
    together with MPS-DMD~\cite{Peddinti2025a}.
  \item \textbf{Tooling} --- mature cross-interpolation libraries, \texttt{xfac}
    (C++) and \texttt{Tensor\-Cross\-Inter\-polation.jl}
    (Julia)~\cite{Nunez2025}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Quantics Tensor Trains: Functions and Operators}
\label{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}
% Keywords: binary/quantics encoding, amplitude encoding, analytic QTT of
% exp, sin, polynomials, x; functional class; bit ordering in 2D/3D;
% Laplacian as an explicit rank-3 MPO; Fourier and Laplace transforms.

So far an MPS is a way of factorizing a tensor that is given to us with many
legs. The quantics trick manufactures those legs out of a one-dimensional grid,
and turns the factorization into a statement about \emph{physics}. The key
idea: \textbf{the tensor cores label spatial scales in a dyadic fashion}. The
physical indices of the TT label the discretization of the domain, and the
values of the function are encoded in the contraction of the matrices.

\begin{definition}[Quantics encoding]
  Let $f : [0,1) \to \mathbb{R}$ be sampled on $N = 2^n$ points. Index a grid
  point by its binary digits,
  \begin{equation}
    x = \sum_{m=1}^{n} 2^{-m} x_m
      = \tfrac12 x_1 + \tfrac14 x_2 + \dots + \tfrac{1}{2^n} x_n,
    \qquad x_m \in \{0,1\}.
    \label{eq:quantics}
  \end{equation}%
  The length-$2^n$ vector of samples is thereby reshaped into an order-$n$
  tensor $f_{x_1 \cdots x_n}$ of physical dimension $d = 2$, which is then
  written as an MPS. The result is a \textbf{quantics tensor train} (QTT).
\end{definition}

\begin{itemize}
  \item In quantum language this is (up to normalization) \emph{amplitude
    encoding} on $n$ qubits,
    $|f\rangle = \sum_{x_1,\dots,x_n \in\{0,1\}} f_{x_1\cdots x_n}\,
    |x_1\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|x_n\rangle$.
  \item Digit $x_1$ selects which half of the domain the point lies in, $x_2$
    which quarter, and so on: $x_1$ is the \textbf{coarsest} scale, $x_n$ the
    \textbf{finest}. A value such as $f(0,1,1) = f(3/8)$ is read off by fixing
    the three legs and contracting the chain.
  \item The bond between legs $m$ and $m+1$ measures the coupling
    \emph{between} scales $2^{-m}$ and finer. Low rank is the same thing as low
    inter-scale correlation.
  \item The mesh size scales exponentially in $n$, but limited correlations
    (low $\chi$) keep the memory cost \emph{linear} in $n$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Analytical construction of 1D functions.}
Several elementary families have exact QTT ranks that are independent of the
number of scales (qubits) $n$~\cite{Oseledets2013}. The exponential factorizes
over the binary digits,
\begin{equation}
  e^{x} = e^{x_1/2 + x_2/4 + \dots + x_n/2^n}
  = \big(e^{1/2}\big)^{x_1}\big(e^{1/4}\big)^{x_2}\cdots\big(e^{1/2^n}\big)^{x_n}
  = \begin{pmatrix} e^0 \\ e^{1/2} \end{pmatrix} \otimes
    \begin{pmatrix} e^0 \\ e^{1/4} \end{pmatrix} \otimes \cdots \otimes
    \begin{pmatrix} e^0 \\ e^{1/2^n} \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:qtt-exp}
\end{equation}%
a product state of rank~1: for instance $e^{x_{101}} = e^{1/2}\cdot 1\cdot
e^{1/8} = e^{5/8}$. A sine or cosine needs rank~2 only because it is a sum of
two such exponentials.

For the linear function $f(x) = x$, the vector $(1, 2, \dots, 2^n)^T$ can be
built recursively from the one on $n-1$ bits, written $[n-1]$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ \vdots \\ 2^n \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \otimes [n-1]
  + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \otimes
    \Big( [n-1] + 2^{n-1} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}^{\otimes (n-1)} \Big).
  \label{eq:qtt-linear}
\end{equation}%
Unrolling the recursion, every bond carries two states --- \enquote{the constant
$1$} and \enquote{the value accumulated so far} --- so $\chi = 2$ across each
bond, and each core is a $2\times2$ matrix of vectors.

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Function} & \textsc{Exact QTT rank} \\
    \midrule
    $e^{ax}$                        & $\chi = 1$ \\
    $x$ (any affine function)       & $\chi = 2$ \\
    $\sin(ax+b)$, $\cos(ax+b)$      & $\chi = 2$ \\
    monomial $x^p$, polynomial of degree $p$ & $\chi = p+1$ \\
    sum of $m$ exponentials         & $\chi \le m$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Sums and products of low-rank functions stay low-rank: ranks add and
    multiply, and then you round.
  \item \textbf{Rank tracks smoothness and scale separation, not resolution.}
    Doubling the resolution adds one tensor, costing $O(n)$ extra parameters
    rather than $O(2^n)$. Measured on $e^{-3x}+\sin(4\pi x)$ at tolerance
    $10^{-10}$: $\chi = 3$ at every $n$ from 6 to 20, so the parameter count
    goes from 58 to 310 while the grid goes from $64$ to $10^6$ points.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What functional class?}
Tree tensor networks --- QTTs are a subclass --- achieve the optimal
approximation order for smooth functions in Sobolev or Besov spaces of any
smoothness order~\cite{Ali2023,Ali2021}. The multiscale interpolative
construction~\cite{Lindsey2023} gives constructive proofs together with
efficient compression algorithms, and rank bounds that decay for smooth
(band-limited) functions and stay controlled for functions with cusps or
discontinuities. The converse is false: a \emph{random} MPS with $\chi = 2$ is
a wildly oscillating, bursty signal. Low rank is necessary for a nice function,
not sufficient. The multiscale reading of tensor networks has a precise
counterpart in the connection between MERA and wavelets~\cite{Evenbly2016}.

\begin{remark}[Sharpness is not the enemy]
  \margintext{Stated as a warning because the wrong intuition here is both
  natural and actively misleading: it sends you looking for a fix to a problem
  you do not have, and past the one you do.}
  Ask a numerical analyst what a step function costs and the answer is
  \enquote{a lot} --- discontinuities are the classical enemy of spectral
  accuracy. In the quantics encoding the answer is $\chi = 2$, at any
  resolution and wherever the jump sits.

  The reason is that quantics never sees a jump. The indicator
  $\mathbb{1}[i > m]$ is decided digit by digit, most significant first, by an
  automaton with two states: \emph{still equal to $m$ so far}, and
  \emph{already decided}. Two states, bond dimension~2 --- the same counting
  argument that gives the shift operator its rank below.

  What is expensive is a feature that is not aligned with the binary index: an
  oscillation the coarse cores cannot resolve ($\sin(1/(x+10^{-2}))$ costs
  $\chi=12$), or, in two dimensions, a curved boundary. A half-plane
  $\mathbb{1}[x > 0.3]$ stays at $\chi=2$ from $N=64$ to $N=1024$; a disc of the
  same size goes $17 \to 50 \to 128$ over that range, because a circle couples
  every length scale at once. \Cref{subsec:tt-cross-geometry} is about paying
  that bill.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Higher dimensions: bit ordering in 2D, 3D and D+T.}
With more than one coordinate there is the freedom to choose the bit ordering,
and the choice should be physically motivated. For a field $u(x_i, y_j, z_k)$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stacked} --- $i_1 \cdots i_{n}\, j_1 \cdots j_{n}\, k_1 \cdots
    k_{n}$: all $x$ scales, then all $y$ scales, then all $z$ scales.
  \item \textbf{Interleaved} --- $i_1 j_1 k_1\, i_2 j_2 k_2 \cdots$: the
    coarsest scale of every coordinate, then the next, and so on. In 2D this is
    the Morton or Z-ordering of the pixels: quadrants, then quadrants of
    quadrants~\cite{Latorre2005,Markeeva2020}.
  \item \textbf{Concatenated} --- an extra leading leg $p \mapsto \{u, v, w\}$
    labels the components of a vector field, followed by the spatial bits.
\end{itemize}

Interleaving keeps each \emph{scale} local along the chain, which matches the
physics of a turbulent cascade. Stacking instead places a single cut between
$x_n$ and $y_1$ that every $x$--$y$ correlation must cross, at every scale at
once.

\begin{remark}[Which ordering wins depends on what you measure]
  \margintext{The lesson generalises: a compression claim is meaningless until
  you say whether the tolerance or the rank was held fixed.}
  There is no universally better ordering, and the folklore in either direction
  comes from testing on too narrow a class of fields.

  A product $f(x)g(y)$ has rank exactly~1 across the stacked cut, \emph{by
  definition of a product}. Stacking is therefore optimal for anything
  separable --- and a great many textbook test functions, including the
  isotropic Gaussian $e^{-a(x-\frac12)^2}e^{-a(y-\frac12)^2}$, are separable
  without looking it.

  For genuinely two-dimensional fields the two comparisons disagree. At matched
  \emph{tolerance} (fix $\varepsilon$, let $\chi$ be what it needs), stacking is
  more efficient on a synthetic $k^{-5/3}$ field. At matched \emph{bond
  dimension} (fix $\chi$, measure the error), interleaving is more accurate and
  degrades more gracefully. In a large simulation you never get to choose the
  tolerance; you choose $\chi$, because $\chi$ is what fits in memory.
\end{remark}

The choice is not cosmetic. In three dimensions it decides whether non-Gaussian
turbulence statistics survive compression~\cite{Pisoni2026}, and the diagnostic
that separates the orderings is not the $\ell_2$ error at all --- it is the
probability distribution of velocity increments. The evidence is in
\cref{subsec:expressivity}.

\paragraph{Finite-difference operators as MPOs.}
Finite-difference shift, first-derivative and Laplacian operators have
\textbf{exact} QTT (MPO) representations of rank
$\chi_{\mathrm{MPO}} = 2\text{--}4$, independent of
$n$~\cite{Kazeev2012,Kazeev2013}. The cores are written down in closed form;
nothing is fitted or truncated. The Laplacian is the example of the slides.
Take the (positive) second-difference matrix
$\Delta_{2^n} = \mathrm{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1)$ and define
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{1}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
  J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
  \Delta_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
           = 2\,\mathbb{1}_2 - (J + J^{T}).
\end{equation*}
Splitting $\Delta_4$ into $2\times2$ blocks indexed by the coarse bit,
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta_4 = \mathbb{1}_2 \otimes \Delta_2 - \big(J \otimes J^{T} + J^{T}\otimes J\big)
  = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1}_2 & -J & -J^{T} \end{pmatrix} \bowtie
    \begin{pmatrix} \Delta_2 \\ J^{T} \\ J \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}
where $\bowtie$ is the matrix product over the bond index with the entries
--- themselves $2\times2$ matrices --- multiplied by Kronecker products
(Kazeev and Khoromskij's strong Kronecker product). Recursively,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{2^n} =
  \begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1}_2 & -J & -J^{T} \end{pmatrix} \bowtie
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathbb{1}_2 & -J & -J^{T} \\
    0 & J^{T} & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & J
  \end{pmatrix}^{\bowtie (n-2)} \bowtie
  \begin{pmatrix} \Delta_2 \\ J^{T} \\ J \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:qtt-laplacian}
\end{equation}%
an MPO of bond dimension $\chi = 3$. The middle core propagates the corner
coupling $E_{2^{n-1},1} = J^{T}\otimes\cdots\otimes J^{T}$ between the two
halves of the domain, one bit at a time. Boundary conditions can be included at
small cost, by changing the edge cores only. The same object has a low-rank
explicit inverse~\cite{Kazeev2012}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Applying an MPO to an MPS costs
    $O(n \chi^2 \chi_{\mathrm{MPO}}^2)$ and returns a state of bond dimension
    $\chi \chi_{\mathrm{MPO}}$, so it is always followed by rounding.
  \item The dense $2^n \times 2^n$ matrix is never formed. It never exists in
    memory at any point.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}
  Rank~3 for a second derivative is not a coincidence. A three-point stencil is
  a sum of three shifts, and a shift by one grid point is a carry operation on
  the binary digits --- a finite automaton with a two-state carry register.
  Small automaton, small bond dimension. Adding the three shift MPOs gives
  rank $2+2+1 = 5$, and rounding brings it to the $3$
  of~\eqref{eq:qtt-laplacian}.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Beyond finite differences: Fourier and Laplace transforms.}
Spectral operators are low rank too. The quantum Fourier transform has small
entanglement, so the discrete Fourier transform is a QTT operator of modest
bond dimension that saturates as the number of qubits grows~\cite{Chen2023},
and it can be built directly by interpolation~\cite{Chen2025}; the same holds
for the (non-unitary) Laplace transform~\cite{Jaseem2026}. This
\enquote{dequantization} of the QFT is what puts FFT-based solvers within reach
of the QTT format. García-Ripoll~\cite{GarciaRipoll2021} tabulates the cost of
the resulting quantum-inspired algorithms --- expected values, Fourier
transforms, interpolation, PDE evolution, state construction --- against their
classical and quantum counterparts; in every case the exponential $2^{Nm}$ of
the classical method becomes a polynomial in the number of bits times the cost
of MPS simplification, $O(T_{\mathrm{sweeps}}\,Nm\,d^3\chi^3)$.

\subsection{Heat Equation (Explicit Time Stepping)}
\label{subsec:heat-explicit-euler}
% Keywords: 1D heat equation, explicit Euler, MPO-MPS product + rounding,
% cost and memory, stability (CFL), rank behaviour, sources of error.

We now have enough to solve a PDE. The heat equation is deliberately the easiest
possible choice --- diffusion smooths, so the ranks ought to behave --- which
makes it the right place to watch the machinery work and to isolate what can go
wrong.

\paragraph{The setup.}
Take
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u = \alpha\, \partial_x^2 u,
  \qquad x \in [0,1],
  \qquad N = 2^n \text{ points},
  \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}%
with $\Delta x = 2^{-n}$. Three ingredients suffice, and there is nothing else:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the initial condition $u_0$ as an MPS/QTT;
  \item the discrete Laplacian $L$ as an MPO of rank~3,
    $L = -\Delta_{2^n}/\Delta x^2$ with $\Delta_{2^n}$
    from~\eqref{eq:qtt-laplacian};
  \item one scalar, the diffusion number $\alpha \Delta t / \Delta x^2$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{One timestep.}
Explicit Euler is a single line of tensor arithmetic,
\begin{equation}
  u^{k+1} = \left(\mathbb{1} + \alpha \Delta t\, L\right) u^{k},
  \label{eq:heat-explicit}
\end{equation}%
consisting of exactly two operations: an \textbf{MPO--MPS product}, and then
\textbf{rounding} $(\chi,\epsilon)$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Cost is $O(n\chi^3)$ per step with $O(n\chi^2)$ memory. Both are
    \textbf{logarithmic in the mesh size} $N = 2^n$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Rank remark.}
The diffusive dynamics smooths the solution, so the bond dimension should stay
flat or fall. If it climbs, suspect the encoding, the boundary treatment, or too
loose a rounding tolerance. But if we do not truncate at each step, the rank
grows like $3^k\chi_0$: each step multiplies the bond dimension by
$\chi_{\mathrm{MPO}} = 3$, the memory grows like $9^k$, and you are back to
storing $2^n$ numbers within about twenty steps. Skipping the rounding is the
classic first mistake.

\paragraph{Stability remark.}
\textbf{Compression does not rescue stability.} The amplification factor of a
Fourier mode with grid angle $\theta$ is $G(\theta) = 1 - 4\mu\sin^2(\theta/2)$
with $\mu = \alpha \Delta t / \Delta x^2$, so the condition is still
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\alpha \Delta t}{\Delta x^2} \le \frac12 .
  \label{eq:heat-cfl}
\end{equation}%
But with $\Delta x = 2^{-n}$ this forces $\Delta t \sim 4^{-n}$: the step count
grows exponentially in $n$, while the memory grows only linearly. Compression
buys resolution and then the explicit scheme charges for it in time. This is
exactly the motivation for \cref{subsec:heat-implicit-euler}.

\paragraph{Two sources of error.}
\margintext{Monitor both, and always know which one dominates before tightening
either.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Discretization} --- $O(\Delta t, \Delta x^2)$, present in the
    dense scheme too.
  \item \textbf{Truncation} --- $O(\epsilon)$ per step, from rounding.
\end{itemize}
Comparing the QTT solution with the \emph{same} Euler scheme run densely
isolates the second; comparing that dense run with the exact solution isolates
the first. Beyond first order, tensor-train time integrators based on projection
onto the tangent space of the fixed-rank manifold give higher order with rank
adaptivity~\cite{Lubich2015}; Part~\partref{3} takes this up.
