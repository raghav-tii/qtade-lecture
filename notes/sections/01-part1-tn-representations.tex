\section{Part I: Tensor Network Representations}
\label{sec:part1}
\lecture{28}{09}{2026}

% Mirrors slides.qmd "Part I" outline. One subsection per outline item.

Everything in this course rests on a single bet. A field sampled on a fine grid
is an enormous vector, but it is not an \emph{arbitrary} enormous vector: it is
smooth, it is multiscale, and the coupling between well-separated scales is
weak. If that is true, the vector lies on a manifold of far lower dimension than
the space it sits in, and we should compute on the manifold rather than in the
ambient space.

Part~\partref{1} builds the machinery needed to state that bet precisely: a
diagrammatic language (\cref{subsec:penrose-notation}), a short account of where
the ideas came from (\cref{subsec:literature-survey}), the scaling wall they are
meant to break (\cref{subsec:numerical-methods-intro}), the ansatz itself
(\cref{subsec:mps}), the binary re-indexing that makes it multiscale
(\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}), and --- by the end --- a working PDE
solver whose cost is logarithmic in the number of grid points
(\cref{subsec:heat-explicit-euler}).

\subsection{Penrose Diagram Notation}
\label{subsec:penrose-notation}
% Keywords: tensor as a node, legs = indices, contraction = joined legs,
% scalars/vectors/matrices/tensors as diagrams, index summation convention,
% trace, identity/copy tensor, diagrammatic vs. index notation.

Index expressions with five or six summations are correct and unreadable.
Diagrams are the working notation of the field for a concrete reason: they carry
exactly the same information, and they make the \emph{cost} of a computation
something you can see~\cite{bridgeman2017}.
\margintext{Diagrammatic tensor notation is due to Penrose (1971); it entered
numerical practice through the DMRG community in the 1990s. Bridgeman and Chubb
(2017) is the readable modern introduction.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A tensor is a \textbf{node}; every index is a \textbf{leg}. The rank of a
    tensor is its number of legs.
  \item A scalar has no legs, a vector $v_i$ one, a matrix $M_{ij}$ two, a
    general tensor $T_{ijk}$ three.
  \item Nothing is lost in the translation. The diagram carries precisely what
    the index expression carries.
\end{itemize}

There are only two moves, and every construction in this course is assembled
from them: \emph{place nodes} and \emph{join legs}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Joining two legs means \textbf{summing over that shared index}:
    \begin{equation*}
      \sum_j M_{ij} N_{jk}
      \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
      \text{two nodes, one bond.}
    \end{equation*}
  \item \textbf{Dangling legs} are the free indices of the result. Counting them
    tells you what object you have built.
  \item A \textbf{closed loop} has no free legs, so the diagram evaluates to a
    number. It is a trace.
\end{itemize}

A small vocabulary of recurring nodes covers most diagrams you will meet.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Identity} --- a bare line.
  \item \textbf{Copy (or $\delta$) tensor} --- a branching node, one index shared
    by three legs.
  \item \textbf{Isometry} --- drawn shaded. A tensor $A$ reshaped into a matrix
    is an isometry when $A^{\dagger}A = \mathbb{1}$, so contracting it with its
    conjugate collapses to a bare line.
\end{itemize}

\begin{notation}
  Throughout these notes physical legs point \textbf{down} and virtual (bond)
  legs run \textbf{horizontally}. Conventions differ between papers; a diagram
  only means something once the convention is fixed.
\end{notation}

\begin{remark}[Order is cost]
  The payoff is not compactness. It is that the \emph{order} of contraction is
  visible in the diagram, and order sets the cost. Take the chain of $n$ rank-3
  tensors of \cref{subsec:mps}, each of bond dimension $\chi$ and physical
  dimension $d$. Contract it sequentially and the cost is $O(n \chi^3)$;
  contract it by first forming the full $d^{\,n}$-component vector and the cost
  is $O(d^{\,n})$. Same diagram, same numbers out, exponentially different bills.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Literature Survey}
\label{subsec:literature-survey}
% Keywords: DMRG (White 1992), matrix product states, tensor train
% decomposition (Oseledets), quantics/quantized tensor trains (QTT),
% tensor networks for PDEs, tensor networks for CFD, review articles.

A short orientation, partly because the subject has an unusual history. The same
ansatz was invented twice, in two communities that did not talk to each other,
and \emph{both} vocabularies remain in active use.

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Quantum many-body} & \textsc{Numerical analysis} \\
    \midrule
    Matrix product state (MPS)   & Tensor train (TT) \\
    Bond dimension $\chi$        & TT-rank \\
    Entanglement across a cut    & Singular-value decay \\
    DMRG sweep                   & Alternating least squares \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The columns denote the same objects and the same algorithms. For a review from
the physics side see~\cite{orus2014}; from the numerical-linear-algebra side,
\cite{oseledets2011}.

\paragraph{Where it came from.}
\margintext{Note the seventeen-year gap between White (1992) and Oseledets
(2011). The numerical-analysis rediscovery was independent; the dictionaries
were matched up only afterwards.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{DMRG} --- variational optimization over matrix product states,
    born in condensed-matter physics~\cite{white1992}.
  \item \textbf{MPS formalism} --- the standard reference for canonical forms
    and sweeping~\cite{schollwoeck2011}.
  \item \textbf{Tensor-train decomposition} --- the same ansatz rederived for
    numerical linear algebra, with its own error analysis~\cite{oseledets2011}.
  \item \textbf{Quantics} --- binary indexing gives $O(d \log N)$ approximation
    of $N$-$d$ tensors~\cite{khoromskij2011}.
  \item \textbf{TT-cross} --- build a tensor train from samples, without ever
    forming the full array~\cite{oseledets2010ttcross}.
  \item \textbf{Area law} --- the theorem that explains why $\chi$ can stay
    bounded in one dimension~\cite{hastings2007}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Tensor networks meet fluids.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scale correlations are weak} in turbulence, so $\chi$ stays
    modest~\cite{gourianov2022}.
  \item \textbf{Kinetic plasmas.} Vlasov--Poisson solved in MPS
    form~\cite{ye2022vlasov}.
  \item \textbf{Reduced-order models.} Wall-bounded flows at a few percent of
    the DNS variable count~\cite{kiffner2023rom}.
  \item \textbf{Turbulence PDFs} computed directly in tensor-network form,
    without sampling the full field~\cite{gourianov2025pdf}.
  \item \textbf{A full stack.} Incompressible flow around arbitrary immersed
    geometry, end to end~\cite{peddinti2024cfd}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where the field is now.}
\margintext{Our own route through the subject: the 2024 framework fixed the
encoding and the projection loop; the 2026 turbulence study asked how far the
ranks stretch in 3D; the 2025 space--time paper removed the time loop
altogether. Parts~\partref{3} and~\partref{4} follow that order.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{3D turbulence} --- compression, simulation and synthesis at
    $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda = 315$ on a $1024^3$ grid~\cite{pisoni2026turbulence}.
  \item \textbf{Compressible flow} --- a 2D Euler solver, finalist in the
    Airbus--BMW Quantum Computing Challenge 2024~\cite{peddinti2025compressible}.
  \item \textbf{Space--time and data-driven} --- space and time in a single MPS,
    together with MPS-DMD~\cite{peddinti2025spacetime}.
  \item \textbf{Tooling} --- mature cross-interpolation libraries, \texttt{xfac}
    (C++) and \texttt{Tensor\-Cross\-Inter\-polation.jl}
    (Julia)~\cite{nunez2025tci}.
\end{itemize}

Parts~\partref{3} and~\partref{4} are built directly on the first three.

\subsection{Numerical Methods: A Brief Introduction}
\label{subsec:numerical-methods-intro}
% Keywords: curse of dimensionality, grid-based discretization, finite
% differences, why classical solvers struggle at high resolution/dimension,
% compressed representations, low-rank structure.

The motivation is a scaling wall, so state the wall carefully before claiming to
climb it.

\paragraph{The curse of dimensionality.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Discretize a field on a mesh with $N = 2^n$ points per dimension: in $d$
    dimensions that is $2^{nd}$ unknowns.
  \item A $1024^3$ mesh is $2^{30} \approx 10^9$ unknowns --- \emph{per field,
    per timestep}.
  \item Three-dimensional direct numerical simulation needs
    $\sim\!\mathrm{Re}^{9/4}$ degrees of freedom
    (\cref{subsec:navier-stokes}).
  \item An aircraft at cruise sits near $\mathrm{Re} \sim 10^{7}$, so
    $\sim\!10^{16}$ degrees of freedom. Exascale hardware does not close a gap
    of that size. It moves it by a couple of orders of magnitude.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why structure is the only way out.}
\margintext{Every classical accelerator --- adaptive meshes, multigrid, spectral
methods, POD and reduced-order models --- exploits structure. The
tensor-network ansatz is another one, not a different kind of thing.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Finite differences, finite volumes and finite elements all change the
    constant, not the scaling.
  \item \textbf{The tensor-network claim:} a smooth multiscale field is not an
    arbitrary point in $\mathbb{R}^{2^n}$. It sits on a manifold described by
    $O(n\chi^2)$ numbers.
  \item \textbf{The price:} exact linear algebra becomes approximate arithmetic,
    truncated at every step.
\end{itemize}

That trade --- exponential memory for a truncation error you must monitor --- is
the recurring theme of the whole course.

\subsection{Matrix Product States}
\label{subsec:mps}
% Keywords: MPS/tensor train ansatz, bond dimension, canonical form
% (left/right/mixed), SVD-based compression, singular value decay,
% area law of entanglement, exact vs. approximate ranks, orthogonality center.

\begin{definition}[Matrix product state / tensor train]
  A vector with $2^n$ components is written as a chain of rank-3 tensors,
  \begin{equation}
    v_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_n}
      = A^{(i_1)}_1 A^{(i_2)}_2 \cdots A^{(i_n)}_n ,
    \label{eq:mps}
  \end{equation}%
  where each $A^{(i_k)}_k$ is a matrix of size at most $\chi \times \chi$, with
  $A_1$ a row vector and $A_n$ a column vector so the product is a scalar. The
  \textbf{bond dimension} $\chi$ is the size of the virtual legs.
\end{definition}

The parameter count of~\eqref{eq:mps} is at most $2n\chi^2$ rather than $2^n$.
That is exponential compression --- \emph{provided $\chi$ stays bounded as $n$
grows}. Almost everything that follows is about when that proviso holds.

\paragraph{Why an MPS can work at all.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Cut the chain at bond $k$. The bond dimension needed there equals the
    rank of $v$ reshaped into a matrix across that cut.
  \item That rank is governed by the \textbf{decay of the singular values} of
    the reshaped matrix. Physicists read the same quantity as the entanglement
    between the two halves.
  \item For ground states of gapped one-dimensional local Hamiltonians an
    \textbf{area law} bounds that entanglement independently of system
    size~\cite{hastings2007}, hence $\chi$ independent of $n$.
  \item Smooth, multiscale functions behave the same way. Noise and random data
    do not: their singular values decay slowly or not at all.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Building an MPS: TT-SVD.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Reshape the vector, take an SVD, split off one tensor, repeat along the
    chain.
  \item Truncating small singular values at each of the $n-1$ cuts is
    \textbf{quasi-optimal}: the result is within a factor $\sqrt{n-1}$ of the
    best rank-$\chi$ approximation in the Frobenius
    norm~\cite{oseledets2011}. The $\sqrt{n-1}$ comes from accumulating $n-1$
    independent truncation errors in quadrature.
  \item Exact and fully controlled --- but it needs the dense $2^n$ vector in
    memory, so it is practical only for modest $n$.
\end{itemize}

\Cref{subsec:finding-qtt} returns to this with methods that build an MPS
\emph{without} ever forming the full array. That is what makes the programme
viable.

\paragraph{Canonical forms and rounding.}
\margintext{Gauge freedom is not a nuisance to tolerate; fixing it is what makes
truncation optimal and inner products cheap. In practice you carry the
orthogonality centre along with the state.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The factorization~\eqref{eq:mps} is not unique: insert $G G^{-1}$ on any
    bond and nothing changes. Fixing this \textbf{gauge freedom} gives the
    canonical forms.
  \item A tensor is \textbf{left-orthogonal} when
    $\sum_{i} A^{(i)\dagger} A^{(i)} = \mathbb{1}$, and right-orthogonal under
    the mirror condition. Sweeping QR from the left and from the right produces
    left, right and \textbf{mixed} canonical form, the last carrying a single
    non-orthogonal tensor called the orthogonality centre.
  \item In mixed canonical form the environment on either side of the centre is
    the identity. Truncating the centre by SVD is then optimal for that bond,
    and an inner product costs $O(n\chi^3)$ instead of $O(2^n)$.
  \item \textbf{Rounding is the workhorse of the subject.} Every operation
    inflates ranks --- an elementwise product of two MPSs has bond dimension
    $\chi_1\chi_2$, a sum has $\chi_1 + \chi_2$ --- so recompress back to $\chi$
    once per step, every step, at cost $O(n\chi^3)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Quantics Tensor Trains: Functions and Operators}
\label{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}
% Keywords: binary/quantics encoding of a grid, QTT representation of
% functions, exponential compression, QTT rank vs. function smoothness,
% representing differential/difference operators in QTT form, QTT of
% derivative/Laplacian operators.

So far an MPS is a way of factorizing a long vector. The quantics trick turns it
into a statement about \emph{physics}, by making each tensor in the chain
correspond to one length scale.

\begin{definition}[Quantics encoding]
  Index a grid point by its binary digits,
  \begin{equation}
    x = \sum_{k=1}^{n} i_k 2^{-k}, \qquad i_k \in \{0,1\}.
    \label{eq:quantics}
  \end{equation}%
  A length-$2^n$ vector is thereby reshaped into an $n$-leg tensor of physical
  dimension $d = 2$, which is then written as an MPS. The result is a
  \textbf{quantics tensor train} (QTT).
\end{definition}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Digit $i_1$ selects which half of the domain the point lies in, $i_2$
    which quarter, and so on. Each tensor therefore carries \textbf{one length
    scale}: leg~1 the coarsest, leg~$n$ the finest.
  \item The chain becomes a multiscale, wavelet-like object. The bond between
    legs $k$ and $k+1$ measures the coupling \emph{between} scales $2^{-k}$ and
    finer.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Which functions are low-rank?}
Several elementary families have exact, resolution-independent QTT
ranks~\cite{oseledets2013functions}:

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Function} & \textsc{Exact QTT rank} \\
    \midrule
    $e^{ax}$                        & $\chi = 1$ \\
    $\sin(ax+b)$, $\cos(ax+b)$      & $\chi = 2$ \\
    polynomial of degree $p$        & $\chi = p+1$ \\
    sum of $m$ exponentials         & $\chi \le m$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{remark}
  The exponential is rank~1 because $e^{ax}$ factorizes over the binary digits:
  $e^{a\sum_k i_k 2^{-k}} = \prod_k e^{a i_k 2^{-k}}$, a product of independent
  per-digit factors. A sine needs rank~2 only because it is a sum of two such
  exponentials.
\end{remark}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Sums and products of low-rank functions stay low-rank: ranks add and
    multiply, and then you round.
  \item \textbf{Rank tracks smoothness and scale separation, not resolution.}
    Doubling the resolution adds one tensor, costing $O(n)$ extra parameters
    rather than $O(2^n)$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Bit ordering in two and three dimensions.}
With more than one coordinate there is a genuine choice.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stacked} --- $x_1 x_2 \cdots x_n\, y_1 y_2 \cdots y_n$: all $x$
    scales, then all $y$ scales.
  \item \textbf{Interleaved} --- $x_1 y_1 x_2 y_2 \cdots$: the coarsest scale of
    both coordinates, then the next, and so on.
\end{itemize}

Interleaving keeps each \emph{scale} local along the chain, which matches the
physics of a turbulent cascade. Stacking instead places a cut between $x_n$ and
$y_1$ that every $x$--$y$ correlation must cross, at every scale at once. The
choice is not cosmetic: it decides whether non-Gaussian turbulence statistics
survive compression~\cite{pisoni2026turbulence}. The evidence is in
\cref{subsec:expressivity}.

\paragraph{Operators as MPOs.}
\begin{definition}[Matrix product operator]
  The operator analogue of an MPS: a chain in which each tensor carries two
  physical legs, one incoming and one outgoing, in addition to its two virtual
  bonds.
\end{definition}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Finite-difference shift, first-derivative and Laplacian operators have
    \textbf{exact} QTT representations of rank
    $\chi_{\mathrm{MPO}} = 2\text{--}4$, independent of
    $n$~\cite{kazeev2012laplace}. The cores are written down in closed form;
    nothing is fitted or truncated.
  \item Applying an MPO to an MPS costs
    $O(n \chi^2 \chi_{\mathrm{MPO}}^2)$ and returns a state of bond dimension
    $\chi \chi_{\mathrm{MPO}}$, so it is always followed by rounding.
  \item The dense $2^n \times 2^n$ matrix is never formed. It never exists in
    memory at any point.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}
  Rank~3 for a second derivative is not a coincidence. A three-point stencil is
  a sum of three shifts, and a shift by one grid point is a carry operation on
  the binary digits --- a finite automaton with a two-state carry register.
  Small automaton, small bond dimension.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Solving the Heat Equation (Explicit Euler Method)}
\label{subsec:heat-explicit-euler}
% Keywords: 1D/2D heat equation, explicit time-stepping, stability
% (CFL-type) condition, QTT representation of the state, applying the
% discrete Laplacian in QTT form, rounding/recompression after each step,
% numerical example.

We now have enough to solve a PDE. The heat equation is deliberately the easiest
possible choice --- diffusion smooths, so the ranks ought to behave --- which
makes it the right place to watch the machinery work and to isolate what can go
wrong.

\paragraph{The setup.}
Take
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u = \alpha\, \partial_x^2 u,
  \qquad x \in [0,1],
  \qquad N = 2^n \text{ points},
  \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}%
with $\Delta x = 2^{-n}$. Three ingredients suffice, and there is nothing else:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the initial condition $u^0$ as an MPS;
  \item the discrete Laplacian $L$ as an MPO of rank~3
    (\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators});
  \item one scalar, the diffusion number $\alpha \Delta t / \Delta x^2$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{One timestep.}
Explicit Euler is a single line of tensor arithmetic,
\begin{equation}
  u^{k+1} = \left(\mathbb{1} + \alpha \Delta t\, L\right) u^{k},
  \label{eq:heat-explicit}
\end{equation}%
consisting of exactly two operations: an \textbf{MPO--MPS product}, and then
\textbf{rounding} to a tolerance $\varepsilon$ or to a fixed $\chi$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Cost is $O(n\chi^3)$ per step with $O(n\chi^2)$ memory. Both are
    \textbf{logarithmic in the mesh size} $N = 2^n$.
  \item Skipping the rounding is the classic first mistake. Each step multiplies
    the bond dimension by $\chi_{\mathrm{MPO}} = 3$, so after $k$ steps the rank
    is $3^k \chi_0$ and you are back to storing $2^n$ numbers within about
    twenty steps.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What to watch.}
\margintext{Two error sources, kept apart: discretization
$O(\Delta t, \Delta x^2)$ and truncation $O(\varepsilon)$ per step. Monitor
both, and always know which one dominates.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Compression does not rescue stability.} The condition is still
    $\alpha \Delta t / \Delta x^2 \le 1/2$. With $\Delta x = 2^{-n}$ this forces
    $\Delta t \sim 4^{-n}$, so the step count grows like $4^{n}$ while the
    memory grows like $n$. Compression buys resolution and then the explicit
    scheme charges for it in time. This is exactly the motivation for
    \cref{subsec:heat-implicit-euler}.
  \item \textbf{Watch $\chi(t)$.} Diffusion smooths the solution, so the bond
    dimension should stay flat or fall. If it climbs, suspect the encoding, the
    boundary treatment, or too loose a rounding tolerance.
  \item Beyond first order, tensor-train time integrators based on projection
    onto the tangent space of the fixed-rank manifold give higher order with
    rank adaptivity~\cite{lubich2015timeint}.
\end{itemize}

  timesteps you get before memory runs out. Compare with the $3^k$ estimate
  above.
\end{exercise}
