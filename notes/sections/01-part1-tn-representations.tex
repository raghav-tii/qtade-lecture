The general introduction stated the bet on which this course rests: physical
fields have weak coupling between well-separated scales, and therefore lie close
to a low-dimensional manifold. Part~\partref{1} makes that bet precise and
computes on the manifold explicitly. It starts from the exponential cost of
grids (\cref{subsec:numerical-methods-intro}), introduces tensors and their
diagrams (\cref{subsec:penrose-notation}), builds the matrix product state by
repeated SVDs and learns to compute with it (\cref{subsec:mps}), and then shows
how a binary re-indexing of the grid turns the bond dimension into a measure of
inter-scale coupling, for functions and for operators
(\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}). It ends with a working solver for the
heat equation whose cost per step is logarithmic in the number of grid points
(\cref{subsec:heat-explicit-euler}), and with a short history of the subject
(\cref{subsec:literature-survey}).

\textbf{Pipeline stages covered:} TN encoding of fields and operators (stage~3)
in full; and all five stages once, for the heat equation with explicit time
stepping.

\subsection{The Exponential Wall and the Way Out}
\label{subsec:numerical-methods-intro}
% Keywords: curse of dimensionality, grid cost, structure, sparsity,
% sampling, low rank, factorized tensors.

\paragraph{The cost of a grid.}
Grid-based PDE solvers store one number per grid point. Discretizing a field
with $N = 2^n$ points per coordinate gives $2^{nd}$ unknowns in $d$ dimensions,
so a $1024^3$ mesh already holds $2^{30} \approx 10^9$ unknowns --- \emph{per
field, per timestep}. The physics can demand far more. Three-dimensional direct
numerical simulation needs $\sim\!\mathrm{Re}^{9/4}$ degrees of freedom
(\cref{subsec:navier-stokes}); an aircraft at cruise sits near
$\mathrm{Re} \sim 10^{7}$, which means $\sim\!10^{16}$ degrees of freedom.
Exascale hardware does not close a gap of that size; it moves it by a couple of
orders of magnitude.

The same wall appears for any array labelled by many independent indices. An
array with six indices, each taking ten values, already has $10^6$ entries, and
the number of entries grows exponentially with the number of indices. This is
the \emph{curse of dimensionality}, and the grid of a PDE solver is one instance
of it: each coordinate, and each time level, is one more index.

\paragraph{Structure as the way out.}
Finite differences, finite volumes and finite elements change the constant in
front of this cost, not its scaling. What changes the scaling is
\emph{structure} in the data. Three kinds of structure can be exploited:
\emph{sparsity}, when most entries vanish; \emph{sampling}, when a few
well-chosen entries determine the rest; and \emph{low-rank structure}, when the
array factorizes into small pieces. Every classical accelerator --- adaptive
meshes, multigrid, spectral methods, POD and reduced-order models --- exploits
one of these. Tensor networks take the third route, and are in that sense
another accelerator of the same kind, not a different kind of method.

\paragraph{A factorized example.}
The smallest example shows the mechanism. A function $f(x,y,z)$ of three binary
variables $x,y,z \in \{0,1\}$ is an array of $8$ entries. If it happens to
factorize, $f(x,y,z) = p(x)\,q(y)\,r(z)$, then $6$ numbers suffice, and any
entry --- say $f(0,1,0) = p(0)\,q(1)\,r(0)$ --- is recovered by multiplying the
factors. Tensor networks generalize this: they replace one large array by many
small ones, joined so that the large array can be reconstructed on demand. The
rest of this part makes the joining precise and asks when it is cheap.

\subsection{Tensors and Diagram Notation}
\label{subsec:penrose-notation}
% Keywords: tensor = multidimensional array, diagram notation, contraction,
% trace, isometry.

\paragraph{Tensors.}
A tensor is a multi-dimensional array, the generalization of a vector ($v_i$,
one index) and a matrix ($M_{ij}$, two indices) to any number of indices. The
number of indices is the \textbf{order} of the tensor. Tensors occur wherever
data are labelled by several independent attributes: images (horizontal
position, vertical position, colour channel), medical records (age, height,
weight, symptom), the discretization of a function of several variables, and
the wavefunctions of quantum many-body physics, where the methods of this
course originated.

\paragraph{Diagram notation.}
Index expressions with five or six summations are correct and unreadable.
Diagrams~\cite{Penrose1971} are the working notation of the field for a
concrete reason: they carry exactly the same information, and they make the
\emph{cost} of a computation visible~\cite{Bridgeman2017}.
\margintext{Diagrammatic tensor notation is due to Penrose (1971); it entered
numerical practice through the DMRG community in the 1990s. Bridgeman and Chubb
(2017) and \texttt{tensornetwork.org} are readable modern introductions.}
A tensor is drawn as a \textbf{node}, and each of its indices as a
\textbf{leg}: a vector $v_j$ has one leg, a matrix $M_{ij}$ two, an order-3
tensor $T_{ijk}$ three, and a scalar none. The diagram carries precisely what
the index expression carries, so the names of the indices can be omitted
without ambiguity. A general tensor of order $n$ is one node with $n$ legs; a
tensor network replaces that node by many small nodes joined by bonds, and the
compression of \cref{subsec:numerical-methods-intro} comes from the bonds being
narrow.

\paragraph{Contraction.}
Every construction in this course is assembled from two moves: \emph{place
nodes} and \emph{join legs}. Joining two legs means summing over the shared
index. The inner product $\sum_j v_j w_j$ is two nodes joined by one bond; the
matrix--vector product $\sum_j M_{ij} v_j$ and the contraction of an order-3
tensor with a vector, $\sum_j T_{ijk} v_j$, are drawn the same way. The legs
left dangling are the free indices of the result, so counting them tells you
what kind of object the diagram represents. A diagram with no dangling legs is a
closed loop and represents a number, for instance the trace $\sum_j M_{jj}$ or
$\sum_{ij} A_{ij}B_{ji} = \operatorname{Tr}[AB]$.

\paragraph{Recurring nodes.}
Two special nodes cover most of the diagrams that follow. The identity is a
bare line. An \textbf{isometry} is drawn as a triangle: a tensor $A$ reshaped
into a matrix is an isometry when $A^{\dagger}A = \mathbb{1}$, so contracting
it with its conjugate collapses to a bare line.

\begin{notation}
  Throughout these notes physical legs point \textbf{down} and virtual (bond)
  legs run \textbf{horizontally}. Conventions differ between papers; a diagram
  only means something once the convention is fixed.
\end{notation}

\subsection{Matrix Product States}
\label{subsec:mps}
% Keywords: low-rank structure, SVD, Eckart-Young, truncation by rank or by
% error, recursive SVD (TT-SVD), MPS/tensor train, canonical form, rounding,
% primitives (sum, product, inner product, MPO), contraction order.

\subsubsection{From the SVD to the tensor train}

\paragraph{The singular value decomposition.}
For matrices, low-rank structure is uncovered by the singular value
decomposition. Any matrix factorizes as
\begin{equation}
  M = U S V^{\dagger}, \qquad U^{\dagger}U = \mathbb{1}_{\chi}, \quad
  V^{\dagger}V = \mathbb{1}_{\chi},
  \label{eq:svd}
\end{equation}%
where $S$ is diagonal and holds the singular values $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2
\ge \dots \ge \lambda_{\chi} > 0$: real, non-negative and decreasingly ordered.
Grouping $A = U\sqrt{S}$ and $B = \sqrt{S}V^{\dagger}$ writes $M$ as two nodes
joined by a bond that runs over $\chi = \operatorname{rank}(M)$ values.
\textbf{Truncating} the smallest singular values, $M \approx U\tilde{S}V^{\dagger}
= \tilde{A}\tilde{B}$, gives the optimal approximation of that rank in the
$\ell_2$ (and Frobenius) norm; this is the Eckart--Young theorem.

\begin{example}[A $4\times 3$ matrix]
  The matrix
  \begin{equation*}
    M = \begin{pmatrix}
      0.435839 & 0.223707 & \phantom{-}0.10\\
      0.435839 & 0.223707 & -0.10\\
      0.223707 & 0.435839 & \phantom{-}0.10\\
      0.223707 & 0.435839 & -0.10
    \end{pmatrix}
  \end{equation*}
  has singular values $(0.933,\ 0.300,\ 0.200)$, with $U$ built from
  $\pm\tfrac12$ entries and $V$ from $(1,\pm1)/\sqrt2$ and $(0,0,1)$. Dropping
  the smallest singular value gives a rank-2 approximation with
  $\|M_{\chi=2} - M\|^2 = 0.2^2$; dropping two gives
  $\|M_{\chi=1}-M\|^2 = 0.2^2 + 0.3^2$. The squared error is exactly the sum of
  the squared discarded singular values.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Two truncation strategies.}
A truncation can be specified in two ways. Fixing a \textbf{maximum allowed
rank} $\chi$ determines the approximation error,
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \Big(\sum_{j > \chi} \lambda_j^2\Big)^{1/2}.
  \label{eq:trunc-error}
\end{equation}
Fixing a \textbf{maximum allowed error} $\varepsilon$ instead determines the
smallest $\chi$ such that
$\varepsilon \ge \big(\sum_{j > \chi} \lambda_j^2\big)^{1/2}$. Both are used in
practice, and we write a truncation as $(\chi,\varepsilon)$.

\paragraph{TT-SVD.}
The SVD extends to a tensor with $n$ legs of dimension $d$ by applying it
repeatedly. Reshape the tensor into a matrix whose rows are indexed by the first
leg and whose columns are indexed by all the others, apply the SVD, and keep
$U_1$ as the first node. Then multiply the remainder $S_1 V_1^{\dagger}$ and
reshape it again, this time with the bond and the second leg as the row index,
and apply the SVD once more. Sweeping in this way through the tensor until the
last leg produces a chain of order-3 tensors.

\begin{definition}[Matrix product state / tensor train]
  A tensor with $n$ legs is written as a chain of order-3 tensors,
  \begin{equation}
    v_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_n}
      = A^{(i_1)}_1 A^{(i_2)}_2 \cdots A^{(i_n)}_n ,
    \label{eq:mps}
  \end{equation}%
  where each $A^{(i_k)}_k$ is a matrix of size at most $\chi \times \chi$, with
  $A_1$ a row vector and $A_n$ a column vector so that the product is a scalar.
  The \textbf{bond dimension} $\chi$ is the size of the virtual legs. Numerical
  analysts call~\eqref{eq:mps} a tensor train (TT); physicists call it a matrix
  product state (MPS), the name used for its diagrams.
\end{definition}

  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Quantum many-body} & \textsc{Numerical analysis} \\
    \midrule
    Matrix product state (MPS)   & Tensor train (TT) \\
    Matrix product operator (MPO) & TT operator / TT matrix \\
    Bond dimension $\chi$        & TT-rank \\
    Entanglement across a cut    & Singular-value decay \\
    DMRG sweep                   & Alternating least squares \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent
The two columns denote the same objects and the same algorithms. For a review
from the physics side see~\cite{Orus2014}; from the numerical-linear-algebra
side,~\cite{Oseledets2011}.

\paragraph{Parameter count and quasi-optimality.}
The parameter count of~\eqref{eq:mps} is at most $n d\chi^2$ rather than $d^n$.
That is exponential compression, \emph{provided $\chi$ stays bounded as $n$
grows}; almost everything that follows is about when that proviso holds.
Truncating small singular values at each of the $n-1$ cuts is
\textbf{quasi-optimal}: the result is within a factor $\sqrt{n-1}$ of the best
approximation with the same ranks in the Frobenius norm~\cite{Oseledets2011},
the factor coming from $n-1$ independent truncation errors accumulated in
quadrature. TT-SVD is therefore exact and fully controlled, but it needs the
dense tensor in memory and is practical only for modest sizes.
\Cref{subsec:finding-qtt} returns to this with methods that build an MPS
\emph{without} ever forming the full array.

\subsubsection{Computing in the format}
\label{subsec:tt-computing}

An MPS is useful because it can be manipulated without ever being expanded.
Each operation, however, enlarges the bond dimension, and the format stays
compact only if the result is compressed again. Compression is optimal only in a
particular gauge, so we begin there.

\paragraph{Canonical forms.}
\margintext{Gauge freedom is not a nuisance to tolerate; fixing it is what makes
truncation optimal and inner products cheap. In practice the orthogonality
centre is carried along with the state.}
The factorization~\eqref{eq:mps} is not unique: inserting $G G^{-1}$ on any
bond changes nothing. Fixing this \textbf{gauge freedom} gives the canonical
forms. A tensor is \textbf{left-orthogonal} when
$\sum_{i} A^{(i)\dagger} A^{(i)} = \mathbb{1}$, and right-orthogonal under the
mirror condition; the $U_k$ produced by TT-SVD are exactly left-orthogonal.
Sweeping QR decompositions from the left and from the right produces left,
right and \textbf{mixed} canonical form, the last with a single
non-orthogonal tensor called the orthogonality centre. In mixed canonical form
the environment on either side of the centre is the identity, so truncating the
centre by SVD is optimal for that bond.

\paragraph{Rounding.}
\textbf{Rounding} recompresses an MPS to a prescribed $(\chi,\varepsilon)$ by
moving the orthogonality centre along the chain and truncating each bond in
turn, at cost $O(n\chi^3)$. It is the workhorse of the subject. Every primitive
below inflates the bond dimension, so in any algorithm that iterates, rounding
is applied once per step, every step.

\paragraph{Primitives and their rank bills.}
The basic operations, each with the bond dimension of its result, are the
following.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sum.} The cores are stacked block-diagonally, so
    $\chi_{a+b} \le \chi_a + \chi_b$.
  \item \textbf{Element-wise product.} The bond dimensions multiply,
    $\chi_{a\odot b} \le \chi_a\chi_b$; recursive sketching algorithms avoid
    forming the product in full.
  \item \textbf{Inner product.} The two chains are zipped together from one
    end, absorbing one pair of cores at a time into a $\chi\times\chi$
    environment, at cost $O(n d\chi^3)$. The inner product of two vectors with a
    \emph{trillion} entries each ($n=40$ cores with $d=2$) takes about
    $10\,$ms at $\chi = 100$.
  \item \textbf{Perfect sampling} of configurations with probability
    $\propto |v_{i_1\cdots i_n}|^2$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{definition}[Matrix product operator]
  The operator analogue of an MPS (a TT operator): a chain in which each tensor
  carries two physical legs, one incoming and one outgoing, in addition to its
  two virtual bonds. Its bond dimension is written $\chi_A$.
\end{definition}

Contracting an MPO with an MPS leg by leg (the \textbf{MPO--MPS product})
returns an MPS of bond dimension $\chi\chi_A$, at cost
$O(n d^2 \chi^2 \chi_A^2)$. The dense matrix is never formed, and the product is
always followed by rounding.

\begin{remark}[Order is cost]
  A diagram fixes \emph{what} is computed, not \emph{in which order}, and the
  order sets the cost. Contracting the same three-tensor network pairwise in two
  different orders can cost $O(\chi^4)+O(\chi^4)$ or $O(\chi^5)+O(\chi^4)$ for
  bond dimension $\chi$; for $A_{ij}B_{jk}C_{kl}v_l$ the order $((AB)C)v$ costs
  $O(\chi^3)$ while $A(B(Cv))$ costs $O(\chi^2)$. For the chain~\eqref{eq:mps}
  of $n$ order-3 tensors of bond dimension $\chi$ and physical dimension $d$,
  sequential contraction costs $O(n \chi^3)$, whereas first forming the full
  $d^{\,n}$-component vector costs $O(d^{\,n})$. The diagram and the result are
  the same; the bills differ exponentially. Finding the optimal order for a
  \emph{general} network is NP-hard~\cite{Gray2021}; for a chain it is obvious.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Quantics Tensor Trains: Functions and Operators}
\label{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}
% Keywords: binary/quantics encoding, scales as legs, analytic QTT of
% exp, sin, polynomials, x; functional class; bit ordering in 2D/3D;
% Laplacian as an explicit rank-3 MPO; Fourier and Laplace transforms.

So far an MPS is a way of factorizing a tensor that is given to us with many
legs. The quantics construction manufactures those legs out of a
one-dimensional grid, and in doing so gives the bond dimension a physical
meaning. That meaning is the thesis of this part: \textbf{in a quantics tensor
train each core is one length scale, and the bond between two cores measures
how strongly the coarser scales are coupled to the finer ones. Low rank is low
inter-scale coupling.}

\subsubsection{Scales become legs}

\begin{definition}[Quantics encoding]
  Let $f : [0,1) \to \mathbb{R}$ be sampled on $N = 2^n$ points. Index a grid
  point by its binary digits,
  \begin{equation}
    x = \sum_{m=1}^{n} 2^{-m} x_m
      = \tfrac12 x_1 + \tfrac14 x_2 + \dots + \tfrac{1}{2^n} x_n,
    \qquad x_m \in \{0,1\}.
    \label{eq:quantics}
  \end{equation}%
  The length-$2^n$ vector of samples is thereby reshaped into an order-$n$
  tensor $f_{x_1 \cdots x_n}$ of physical dimension $d = 2$, which is then
  written as an MPS. The result is a \textbf{quantics tensor train} (QTT).
\end{definition}
\margintext{In quantum-computing language the reshape is \emph{amplitude
encoding} on $n$ qubits, $|f\rangle = \sum f_{x_1\cdots x_n}\,
|x_1\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|x_n\rangle$ up to normalization; each binary
digit is one qubit.}

Digit $x_1$ selects which half of the domain a point lies in, $x_2$ which
quarter, and so on: $x_1$ is the \textbf{coarsest} scale and $x_n$ the
\textbf{finest}. A value such as $f(0,1,1) = f(3/8)$ is read off by fixing the
three legs and contracting the chain. The grid size grows exponentially in $n$,
but if the coupling between scales stays limited (low $\chi$), the memory cost
grows only \emph{linearly} in $n$.

\paragraph{Why an MPS can work at all.}
Cut the chain at bond $m$. The bond dimension needed there equals the rank of
the tensor reshaped into a matrix across that cut, with the digits
$x_1,\dots,x_m$ as row index and $x_{m+1},\dots,x_n$ as column index, and that
rank is governed by the \textbf{decay of the singular values} of the reshaped
matrix. In the quantics encoding the row index says in which cell of width
$2^{-m}$ a point lies and the column index says where inside that cell it lies,
so the reshaped matrix has one row per coarse cell and one column per fine-scale
offset. Its rank is small exactly when the fine-scale behaviour inside a cell
varies little from one coarse cell to the next, that is, when the coarse and
fine scales are weakly coupled. Smooth, multiscale functions have this property.
Noise and random data do not: their singular values decay slowly or not at all.
\margintext{The physics precedent: for ground states of gapped one-dimensional
local Hamiltonians an \emph{area law} bounds the entanglement across any cut
independently of system size~\cite{Hastings2007}, hence $\chi$ independent of
the chain length. Entanglement across a cut is singular-value decay across a
reshaping.}

\paragraph{Analytical construction of 1D functions.}
Several elementary families have exact QTT ranks that are independent of the
number of scales $n$~\cite{Oseledets2013}. The exponential factorizes over the
binary digits,
\begin{equation}
  e^{x} = e^{x_1/2 + x_2/4 + \dots + x_n/2^n}
  = \big(e^{1/2}\big)^{x_1}\big(e^{1/4}\big)^{x_2}\cdots\big(e^{1/2^n}\big)^{x_n}
  = \begin{pmatrix} e^0 \\ e^{1/2} \end{pmatrix} \otimes
    \begin{pmatrix} e^0 \\ e^{1/4} \end{pmatrix} \otimes \cdots \otimes
    \begin{pmatrix} e^0 \\ e^{1/2^n} \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:qtt-exp}
\end{equation}%
a product state of rank~1: for instance $e^{x_{101}} = e^{1/2}\cdot 1\cdot
e^{1/8} = e^{5/8}$. A sine or cosine needs rank~2 only because it is a sum of
two such exponentials.

For the linear function $f(x) = x$, the vector $(1, 2, \dots, 2^n)^T$ can be
built recursively from the one on $n-1$ bits, written $[n-1]$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ \vdots \\ 2^n \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \otimes [n-1]
  + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \otimes
    \Big( [n-1] + 2^{n-1} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}^{\otimes (n-1)} \Big).
  \label{eq:qtt-linear}
\end{equation}%
Unrolling the recursion, every bond carries two states --- \enquote{the constant
$1$} and \enquote{the value accumulated so far} --- so $\chi = 2$ across each
bond, and each core is a $2\times2$ matrix of vectors.

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Function} & \textsc{Exact QTT rank} \\
    \midrule
    $e^{ax}$                        & $\chi = 1$ \\
    $x$ (any affine function)       & $\chi = 2$ \\
    $\sin(ax+b)$, $\cos(ax+b)$      & $\chi = 2$ \\
    monomial $x^p$, polynomial of degree $p$ & $\chi = p+1$ \\
    sum of $m$ exponentials         & $\chi \le m$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

Sums and products of low-rank functions stay low-rank: their ranks add and
multiply as in \cref{subsec:tt-computing}, and rounding then brings them back
down. The table also shows that \textbf{rank tracks smoothness and scale
separation, not resolution}. Doubling the resolution adds one core, so the
parameter count grows as $O(n)$ rather than $O(2^n)$. On
$e^{-3x}+\sin(4\pi x)$ at tolerance $10^{-10}$ the measured rank is $\chi = 3$
at every $n$ from 6 to 20, so the parameter count goes from 58 to 310 while the
grid goes from $64$ to $10^6$ points.

\paragraph{The functional class.}
Tree tensor networks, of which QTTs are a subclass, achieve the optimal
approximation order for smooth functions in Sobolev or Besov spaces of any
smoothness order~\cite{Ali2023,Ali2021}. The multiscale interpolative
construction~\cite{Lindsey2023} gives constructive proofs together with
efficient compression algorithms, and rank bounds that decay for smooth
(band-limited) functions and stay controlled for functions with cusps or
discontinuities. The converse is false: a \emph{random} MPS with $\chi = 2$ is
a wildly oscillating, bursty signal. Low rank is necessary for a nice function,
not sufficient. The multiscale reading of tensor networks has a precise
counterpart in the connection between MERA and wavelets~\cite{Evenbly2016}.

\begin{remark}[Sharpness is not the enemy]
  Ask a numerical analyst what a step function costs and the answer is
  \enquote{a lot}: discontinuities are the classical enemy of spectral
  accuracy. In the quantics encoding the answer is $\chi = 2$, at any
  resolution and wherever the jump sits.

  The reason is that quantics never sees a jump. The indicator
  $\mathbb{1}[i > m]$ is decided digit by digit, most significant first, by an
  automaton with two states: \emph{still equal to $m$ so far}, and
  \emph{already decided}. Two states give bond dimension~2, by the same
  counting argument that gives the shift operator its rank in
  \cref{subsec:qtt-operators}.

  What is expensive is a feature that is not aligned with the binary index: an
  oscillation the coarse cores cannot resolve ($\sin(1/(x+10^{-2}))$ costs
  $\chi=12$), or, in two dimensions, a curved boundary. A half-plane
  $\mathbb{1}[x > 0.3]$ stays at $\chi=2$ from $N=64$ to $N=1024$; a disc of the
  same size goes $17 \to 50 \to 128$ over that range, because a circle couples
  every length scale at once. \Cref{subsec:tt-cross-geometry} is about paying
  that bill.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Bit ordering in several dimensions}
\label{subsec:qtt-ordering}

With more than one coordinate the bits can be placed along the chain in
different orders, and the choice should be physically motivated. For a field
$u(x_i, y_j, z_k)$ there are three standard choices.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stacked} --- $i_1 \cdots i_{n}\, j_1 \cdots j_{n}\, k_1 \cdots
    k_{n}$: all $x$ scales, then all $y$ scales, then all $z$ scales.
  \item \textbf{Interleaved} --- $i_1 j_1 k_1\, i_2 j_2 k_2 \cdots$: the
    coarsest scale of every coordinate, then the next, and so on. In 2D this is
    the Morton or Z-ordering of the grid points: quadrants, then quadrants of
    quadrants~\cite{Latorre2005,Markeeva2020}.
  \item \textbf{Concatenated} --- an extra leading leg $p \mapsto \{u, v, w\}$
    labels the components of a vector field, followed by the spatial bits.
\end{itemize}

Interleaving keeps each \emph{scale} local along the chain, which matches the
physics of a turbulent cascade. Stacking instead places a single cut between
$x_n$ and $y_1$ that every $x$--$y$ correlation must cross, at every scale at
once.

\begin{remark}[Which ordering wins depends on what you measure]
  \margintext{The lesson generalizes: a compression claim is meaningless until
  you say whether the tolerance or the rank was held fixed.}
  There is no universally better ordering, and the folklore in either direction
  comes from testing on too narrow a class of fields.

  A product $f(x)g(y)$ has rank exactly~1 across the stacked cut, \emph{by
  definition of a product}. Stacking is therefore optimal for anything
  separable, and a great many textbook test functions, including the
  isotropic Gaussian $e^{-a(x-\frac12)^2}e^{-a(y-\frac12)^2}$, are separable
  without looking it.

  For genuinely two-dimensional fields the two comparisons disagree. At matched
  \emph{tolerance} (fix $\varepsilon$, let $\chi$ be what it needs), stacking is
  more efficient on a synthetic $k^{-5/3}$ field. At matched \emph{bond
  dimension} (fix $\chi$, measure the error), interleaving is more accurate and
  degrades more gracefully. In a large simulation you never get to choose the
  tolerance; you choose $\chi$, because $\chi$ is what fits in memory.
\end{remark}

The choice is not cosmetic. In three dimensions it decides whether non-Gaussian
turbulence statistics survive compression~\cite{Pisoni2026}, and the diagnostic
that separates the orderings is not the $\ell_2$ error at all but the
probability distribution of velocity increments. The evidence is in
\cref{subsec:expressivity}.

\subsubsection{Operators as MPOs}
\label{subsec:qtt-operators}

\paragraph{Finite-difference operators.}
Operators inherit the same economy. Finite-difference shift, first-derivative
and Laplacian operators have \textbf{exact} QTT (MPO) representations of rank
$\chi_A = 2\text{--}4$, independent of $n$~\cite{Kazeev2012,Kazeev2013}. The
cores are written down in closed form; nothing is fitted or truncated. Take the
Laplacian. With the (positive) second-difference matrix
$\Delta_{2^n} = \mathrm{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1)$, define
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{1}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
  J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
  \Delta_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
           = 2\,\mathbb{1}_2 - (J + J^{T}).
\end{equation*}
Splitting $\Delta_4$ into $2\times2$ blocks indexed by the coarse bit gives
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta_4 = \mathbb{1}_2 \otimes \Delta_2 - \big(J \otimes J^{T} + J^{T}\otimes J\big)
  = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1}_2 & -J & -J^{T} \end{pmatrix} \bowtie
    \begin{pmatrix} \Delta_2 \\ J^{T} \\ J \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}
where $\bowtie$ is the matrix product over the bond index with the entries
--- themselves $2\times2$ matrices --- multiplied by Kronecker products
(Kazeev and Khoromskij's strong Kronecker product). Recursively,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{2^n} =
  \begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1}_2 & -J & -J^{T} \end{pmatrix} \bowtie
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathbb{1}_2 & -J & -J^{T} \\
    0 & J^{T} & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & J
  \end{pmatrix}^{\bowtie (n-2)} \bowtie
  \begin{pmatrix} \Delta_2 \\ J^{T} \\ J \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:qtt-laplacian}
\end{equation}%
an MPO of bond dimension $\chi_A = 3$. The middle core propagates the corner
coupling $E_{2^{n-1},1} = J^{T}\otimes\cdots\otimes J^{T}$ between the two
halves of the domain, one bit at a time. Applying it to an MPS is the MPO--MPS
product of \cref{subsec:tt-computing}; the dense $2^n \times 2^n$ matrix never
exists in memory.

\begin{remark}[Why rank three]
  Rank~3 for a second derivative is not a coincidence. A three-point stencil is
  a sum of three shifts, and a shift by one grid point is a carry operation on
  the binary digits: a finite automaton with a two-state carry register.
  A small automaton means a small bond dimension. Adding the three shift MPOs
  gives rank $2+2+1 = 5$, and rounding brings it to the $3$
  of~\eqref{eq:qtt-laplacian}.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Fourier and Laplace transforms.}
Spectral operators are low rank too. The discrete Fourier transform is a QTT
operator of modest bond dimension that saturates as the number of bits
grows~\cite{Chen2023}, and it can be built directly by
interpolation~\cite{Chen2025}; the same holds for the (non-unitary) Laplace
transform~\cite{Jaseem2026}. This puts FFT-based solvers within reach of the
QTT format.
\margintext{In quantum-computing terms: the quantum Fourier transform generates
little entanglement, so it can be run classically as an MPO. This is called
its \emph{dequantization}.}
García-Ripoll~\cite{GarciaRipoll2021} tabulates the cost of the resulting
quantum-inspired algorithms --- expected values, Fourier transforms,
interpolation, PDE evolution, state construction --- against their classical
and quantum counterparts. In every case a cost exponential in the number of bits
becomes a polynomial in the number of bits times the cost of MPS
simplification.

\subsection{The Heat Equation: One Pass Through the Pipeline}
\label{subsec:heat-explicit-euler}
% Keywords: 1D heat equation, explicit Euler, MPO-MPS product + rounding,
% cost and memory, stability (CFL), rank behaviour, sources of error,
% implicit Euler as Ax=b.

We now have enough to solve a PDE, and we do so by walking once through the
five stages of the pipeline. The heat equation is deliberately the easiest
possible choice: diffusion smooths, so the ranks ought to behave. That makes it
the right place to watch the machinery work and to isolate what can go wrong.

\paragraph{Stage 1: equation.}
The problem is the one-dimensional heat equation with diffusivity $\alpha$,
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u = \alpha\, \partial_x^2 u,
  \qquad x \in [0,1],
  \qquad N = 2^n \text{ points}.
  \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}%

\paragraph{Stage 2: problem formulation.}
A uniform grid with $\Delta x = 2^{-n}$, the three-point finite-difference
Laplacian, and explicit Euler in time with step $\Delta t$. The only free
scalar is the diffusion number $\mu = \alpha \Delta t / \Delta x^2$.

\paragraph{Stage 3: TN encoding.}
The initial condition $u_0$ is a QTT, built analytically or by TT-SVD as in
\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}. The discrete Laplacian is the rank-3
MPO $L = -\Delta_{2^n}/\Delta x^2$, with $\Delta_{2^n}$
from~\eqref{eq:qtt-laplacian}. Boundary conditions are included at small cost
by changing the edge cores only.

\paragraph{Stage 4: time evolution.}
Explicit Euler is a single line of tensor arithmetic,
\begin{equation}
  u^{k+1} = \left(\mathbb{1} + \alpha \Delta t\, L\right) u^{k},
  \label{eq:heat-explicit}
\end{equation}%
consisting of exactly two operations: an \textbf{MPO--MPS product}, and then
\textbf{rounding} to $(\chi,\varepsilon)$. The cost is $O(n\chi^3)$ per step
with $O(n\chi^2)$ memory, both \textbf{logarithmic in the number of grid
points} $N = 2^n$.

\paragraph{Stage 5: solution retrieval.}
The solution is never expanded. A point value $u^k(x)$ is read off by fixing the
$n$ legs to the binary digits of $x$ and contracting the chain, and integrals
and norms are inner products (\cref{subsec:tt-computing}); both cost a
polynomial in $\chi$ times $n$.

\paragraph{Rank behaviour.}
Diffusion smooths the solution, so the bond dimension should stay flat or fall.
If it climbs, the likely causes are the encoding, the boundary treatment, or too
loose a rounding tolerance. Rounding itself is not optional. Each step
multiplies the bond dimension by $\chi_A = 3$, so without truncation the rank
grows like $3^k\chi_0$ and the memory like $9^k$, and within about twenty steps
the state costs as much as storing all $2^n$ numbers. Skipping the rounding is
the classic first mistake.

\paragraph{Stability.}
\textbf{Compression does not rescue stability.} The amplification factor of a
Fourier mode with grid angle $\theta$ is $G(\theta) = 1 - 4\mu\sin^2(\theta/2)$,
so the stability condition is still
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\alpha \Delta t}{\Delta x^2} \le \frac12 .
  \label{eq:heat-cfl}
\end{equation}%
With $\Delta x = 2^{-n}$ this forces $\Delta t \sim 4^{-n}$: the number of steps
grows exponentially in $n$, while the memory grows only linearly. Compression
buys resolution, and the explicit scheme then charges for it in time.

\paragraph{Two sources of error.}
\margintext{Monitor both, and always know which one dominates before tightening
either.}
The computed solution carries two errors. The \textbf{discretization} error,
$O(\Delta t, \Delta x^2)$, is present in the dense scheme too. The
\textbf{truncation} error, $O(\varepsilon)$ per step, comes from rounding.
Comparing the QTT solution with the \emph{same} Euler scheme run densely
isolates the second; comparing that dense run with the exact solution isolates
the first. Beyond first order, tensor-train time integrators based on
projection onto the tangent space of the fixed-rank manifold give higher order
with rank adaptivity~\cite{Lubich2015}; Part~\partref{3} takes this up.

\paragraph{The implicit step as a linear system.}
The way around~\eqref{eq:heat-cfl} is standard: evaluate the Laplacian at the
new time level. Implicit Euler is unconditionally stable for the heat
equation, so $\Delta t$ is set by accuracy rather than by $\Delta x$. Each step
then reads
\begin{equation}
  \left(\mathbb{1} - \alpha \Delta t\, L\right) u^{k+1} = u^{k},
  \label{eq:heat-implicit-axb}
\end{equation}%
a linear system $Ax = b$ with the MPO $A = \mathbb{1} - \alpha\Delta t\, L$ of
bond dimension at most $1 + \chi_A$, the known MPS $b = u^k$, and the unknown
MPS $x = u^{k+1}$. Nothing in this part solves it. Every tool developed so far
--- TT-SVD, rounding, MPO--MPS products --- computes a known tensor from known
tensors; none of them finds an unknown one, and there is no SVD shortcut that
does. For the Laplacian alone a low-rank explicit inverse is
known~\cite{Kazeev2012}, but that is a property of one operator, not a method
for every MPO. Solving~\eqref{eq:heat-implicit-axb} on the low-rank manifold is
the subject of Part~\partref{2} (\cref{subsec:heat-implicit-euler}).

The companion notebook \texttt{01-representations.ipynb} runs this scheme with

\subsection{Context and Further Reading}
\label{subsec:literature-survey}
% Keywords: transfer matrices, finitely correlated states, DMRG, MPS
% formalism, tensor-train rediscovery, other TNs (TTN, PEPS, MERA),
% applications, libraries, TNs for fluids.

\paragraph{Two origins.}
The matrix product state emerged in quantum many-body physics, as a tool for
one-dimensional spin chains, and was later reinvented in numerical analysis;
this is why both vocabularies of the table in \cref{subsec:mps} remain in use.
On the physics side the precursors go back to Kramers and Wannier (1941), whose
transfer matrices for the two-dimensional Ising model are early matrix product
operators, and to Baxter (1968, 1981), whose corner transfer matrices and
variational wavefunctions resemble MPS. Fannes, Nachtergaele and Werner (1992)
defined the finitely correlated states of quantum spin chains, establishing
mathematically what are now called MPS~\cite{Fannes1992}. In the same year
White introduced the density matrix renormalization group
(DMRG)~\cite{White1992}, a variational algorithm in the MPS manifold and a
numerical breakthrough for ground states of one-dimensional local gapped
Hamiltonians. Östlund and Rommer (1995) showed that DMRG implicitly assumes the
ground state to be an MPS~\cite{Ostlund1995}, and in the 2000s Vidal,
Verstraete and Cirac made the MPS a standard language for one-dimensional
many-body systems and their time evolution~\cite{Vidal2003,Vidal2004,Verstraete2008}. The standard reference for canonical forms and sweeping
is~\cite{Schollwoeck2011}.

\margintext{Note the nineteen-year gap between White (1992) and Oseledets
(2011). The numerical-analysis rediscovery was independent; the dictionaries
were matched up only afterwards.}
Oseledets and Tyrtyshnikov independently rediscovered the same structure in
numerical linear algebra and named it the tensor train
decomposition~\cite{Oseledets2011}; the quantics variant~\cite{Khoromskij2011}
and cross approximation~\cite{Oseledets2010} followed, and applied mathematics
carried the tools into data science, machine learning and high-dimensional
differential equations.

\paragraph{Other tensor networks.}
The MPS is the simplest member of a family organized by a trade-off between
expressivity and cost. The MPS has no loops, so it has a canonical form; it
captures exponentially decaying correlations, and its algorithms cost
$O(\chi^3)$. Tree tensor networks, or hierarchical Tucker formats, are also
loop-free and have a canonical form; they capture critical correlations at cost
$O(\chi^4)$, and are a natural next step for multivariate
functions~\cite{Tindall2024a}. PEPS and MERA capture critical correlations as
well, but a loopy network has no canonical form obtainable by single-tensor
operations, costs up to $O(\chi^8)$, and its contraction becomes a hard
optimization problem in its own right~\cite{Gray2021}. The MPS has by far the
largest toolbox of efficient algorithms and known MPOs, which is why this
course uses it throughout.

\paragraph{Applications and libraries.}
Tensor networks are used for functions of continuous variables (the
\enquote{quantum-inspired} numerics of this course), in machine learning
(compressing models, parametrizing networks), in probability and statistics, in
quantum physics (simulation, error mitigation, process tensors), and in
out-of-equilibrium statistical physics and stochastic chemical kinetics.
Mature libraries include ITensor, TeNPy, TensorNetwork, \texttt{quimb} (used
from Part~\partref{3} on) and \texttt{cotengra} for contraction-order
optimization~\cite{Gray2021}.

\paragraph{Tensor networks for fluids.}
The application closest to this course rests on the observation that scale
correlations in turbulence are weak, so that $\chi$ stays
modest~\cite{Gourianov2022}. Since then the Vlasov--Poisson equations of
kinetic plasmas have been solved in MPS form~\cite{Ye2022}; reduced-order
models of wall-bounded flows have been built at a few percent of the DNS
variable count~\cite{Kiffner2023}; turbulence probability distributions have
been computed directly in tensor-network form, without sampling the full
field~\cite{Gourianov2025}; and incompressible flow around arbitrary immersed
geometry has been solved end to end~\cite{Peddinti2024}.

\paragraph{Summary and hand-off.}
Part~\partref{1} has shown that a field on $2^n$ grid points can be stored as a
chain of $n$ small tensors, that the SVD finds such a chain and rounding keeps
it small, and that in the quantics encoding the bond dimension measures the
coupling between scales, which is weak for the smooth and multiscale fields of
physics. Finite-difference operators are exact MPOs of rank three or less, so an
explicit time step costs $O(n\chi^3)$. Two debts remain. Explicit stepping is
bound by~\eqref{eq:heat-cfl}, and the stable alternative is the linear
system~\eqref{eq:heat-implicit-axb}, which nothing in this part can solve; and
every initial condition here was either written down analytically or compressed
from a dense vector. Part~\partref{2} pays both debts.
