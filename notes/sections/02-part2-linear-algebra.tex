Part~\partref{1} closed with two debts. The first is stability. Explicit Euler
for the heat equation is stable only under the Courant
condition~\eqref{eq:heat-cfl}, which at quantics resolution forces
$\Delta t \sim 4^{-n}$; the cure is an implicit scheme, and an implicit scheme
turns each time step into a linear system $Au = b$ that cannot be solved by
applying an operator and rounding. The second debt is data. Every construction
in Part~\partref{1} assumed that the initial condition, the boundary data and the
operator were already available as tensor trains, or that the full $N = 2^n$
vector could be formed and compressed by SVD. This part pays both debts. It first
develops the sweeping solvers that solve $Au = b$ directly on the low-rank
manifold, applies them to the implicit heat equation, and then shows how
functions, boundary conditions and an immersed geometry are brought into the
format without ever touching all $N$ grid points. It closes with the problems
that remain open, the last of which, nonlinearity, leads into
Part~\partref{3}.

\paragraph{Pipeline stages covered:} TN encoding (stage~3: initial and boundary
conditions, geometry) and time evolution (stage~4: implicit schemes and the
linear solves they require).

\subsection{Implicit Time Stepping and Its Price}
\label{subsec:heat-implicit-euler}
% Keywords: motivation (stability), CFL/Courant criterion, implicit Euler,
% A-stability, one linear solve per time step.

\paragraph{Why stability matters.}
An implicit solver is needed for stability, and stability is needed because it
decouples the time discretization from the space discretization. Under the
Courant condition~\eqref{eq:heat-cfl}~\cite{Courant1928} the time step is tied
to the grid spacing: with $\Delta x = 2^{-n}$, memory grows like $n$ while the
number of steps grows like $4^{n}$. At $n \gtrsim 20$ the bound is unusable,
and every additional bit of resolution that quantics makes affordable in memory
makes the explicit scheme four times more expensive in time. Compression pays
off only in combination with an implicit scheme.

\paragraph{Implicit Euler.}
Backward Euler replaces the explicit update by the linear
system~\eqref{eq:heat-implicit-axb} of Part~\partref{1}. The scheme is A-stable: the amplification factor is
$(1 + \alpha \Delta t\, \lambda)^{-1}$ for every eigenvalue $\lambda \ge 0$ of
$-L$, so it never exceeds one, whatever $\Delta t$ is. The choice of $\Delta t$
therefore affects only accuracy, not stability, and the step is set by the error
that can be tolerated rather than by $\Delta x^2$.

More generally, a one-step implicit scheme for a linear problem has the form
\begin{equation}
  A\, u^{k+1} = B\, u^{k},
  \label{eq:one-step-scheme}
\end{equation}
with $A = \mathbb{1} - \alpha\Delta t\, L$ and $B = \mathbb{1}$ for backward
Euler. Both $A$ and $B$ are MPOs; applying $B$ is a Part~\partref{1} operation,
but inverting $A$ is not.

\paragraph{The price.}
Each time step now requires a linear solve instead of a matrix--vector product,
which is strictly more work per step. In tensor-train format the solve is also
harder than it is for dense vectors, because the set on which it must be carried
out is not a vector space: tensor trains of fixed rank form a smooth
manifold~\cite{Holtz2012a}, and the sum of two trains of rank $\chi$ has rank up
to $2\chi$. The next three subsections pay that price.

\subsection{Solving \texorpdfstring{$Au = b$}{Au = b} in Tensor-Train Format}
\label{subsec:linear-solvers}
% Keywords: direct / iterative / variational solvers, fixed-rank TT manifold,
% TT-GMRES, Riemannian solvers, DMRG, ALS, mALS, AMEn, two-site sweep, cost.

\paragraph{Ground rules.}
The operator $A$ is given as an MPO of bond dimension $\chi_A$, the right-hand
side $b$ as a tensor train, and the solution $u$ is wanted as a tensor train. We
assume that $A$ and $b$ have low rank, but we do not know the rank of $u$ in
advance, so the ideal solver finds both the solution and its rank.

\paragraph{Three families of solvers.}
Classical linear solvers come in three kinds. Direct solvers operate on the
matrix elements until the matrix is easy to invert (LU, Cholesky). Iterative
solvers improve a guess step by step using global operations (CG, GMRES).
Variational solvers vary the parameters of the solution to minimize a loss
function. Because the fixed-rank manifold is not a vector space, the three
families fare differently in tensor format:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Family} & \textsc{In TT format?} & \textsc{Examples} \\
    \midrule
    Direct      & no  & no easy access to matrix elements \\
    Iterative   & yes & TT-GMRES, Riemannian solvers \\
    Variational & yes & TT-ALS, TT-mALS, TT-AMEn \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

Direct inversion is ruled out. There is no easy access to matrix elements, and
$A^{-1}$ has no reason to be low rank even when $A$ and $u$ both are. In
favourable cases an MPO for the inverse can be constructed explicitly; the QTT
Laplacian is the instructive exception, since its inverse admits an explicit
low-rank representation~\cite{Kazeev2012}.

Iterative methods are possible, but often not worth the effort. Fixed-point
(CG) and Krylov (GMRES) methods have been carried over to TT
format~\cite{Dolgov2012a,Ballani2013}, with a rounding step after every
operation. The difficulty is that the rank of the \emph{intermediate} iterates
can blow up even when the final solution has low rank: each matrix--vector
product multiplies $\chi$ by $\chi_A$, and each vector addition adds ranks.
Truncation must then be aggressive, and aggressive truncation destroys the
orthogonality of the Krylov basis.

Riemannian optimization over the fixed-rank manifold~\cite{Kressner2016} avoids
the rank blow-up by construction. It is useful for nonlinear problems, but for
linear problems it is unclear whether it repays the extra effort: it needs
well-behaved retraction and vector-transport routines, and it is not rank
adaptive.

\paragraph{The variational form.}
\margintext{The one-site/two-site distinction is the same one that separates
White's original DMRG from its later single-site variants.}
The variational route reformulates the linear system as an optimization
problem~\cite{Oseledets2012},
\begin{equation}
  \min_u \norm{Au - b}^2
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  \min_u \tfrac{1}{2}\, u^{\top}\! A u - u^{\top} b \quad (A \succ 0),
  \label{eq:tt-linear-min}
\end{equation}%
the second form being available when $A$ is symmetric positive definite. It
should be preferred when available, because the residual form squares the
condition number and the energy form does not. A tensor train is multilinear in
its cores, so if all cores but one are frozen, the objective becomes a quadratic
function of the remaining core, and its minimization is a small linear problem
in that core alone. Solving these local problems one core after another, and
sweeping back and forth along the chain, gives the family of \textbf{sweeping
solvers (ALS, mALS/DMRG, AMEn)}. Their common ancestor is DMRG~\cite{White1992,
Schollwoeck2011}, the variational optimization of an MPS by local updates.

\paragraph{The two-site sweep.}
Let $d$ be the dimension of a physical leg ($d = 2$ for quantics). One step of
the two-site sweep at sites $k$ and $k+1$ proceeds as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Bring the train into mixed canonical form with the orthogonality centre
    at site $k$ (\cref{subsec:tt-computing}).
  \item Merge sites $k$ and $k+1$ into a single two-site tensor with
    $\chi^2 d^2$ entries.
  \item Solve the local problem in that tensor alone, with every other core held
    fixed. The effective operator is assembled from cached left and right
    \textbf{environments}, the partial contractions of the MPO with the frozen
    parts of the chain; they are updated one site at a time rather than rebuilt.
  \item Split the result by SVD, truncating to a tolerance $\varepsilon$ or a
    rank cap $\chi_{\max}$.
  \item Move the orthogonality centre one site along and repeat, sweeping right
    and then left.
\end{enumerate}
The gauge freedom is what makes this work. Because the canonical form makes the
environments orthonormal, the local problem in step~3 is an exact restriction of
the global one rather than an approximation of it, it inherits the conditioning
of $A$, and the global objective decreases monotonically. Ground-state
calculations use the same sweep with a local eigenproblem in place of the local
linear system. The same solver reappears as the pressure-Poisson step of the
Navier--Stokes solver in Part~\partref{3} and as the all-at-once space--time
solve in Part~\partref{4}.

\paragraph{Local problem size.}
A one-site update solves for a core with $\chi^2 d$ unknowns; a two-site update
solves for $\chi^2 d^2$ unknowns. These are the only two local sizes that occur.
For fixed $d$, a dense local solve on the two-site block costs up to
$O(\chi^6)$, and the environment contractions cost
$O(n \chi^3 d^2 + n \chi^2 d\, \chi_A)$ per sweep.

\paragraph{The three sweeping solvers.}
The members of the family differ in what they update and in how they adapt the
rank. \textbf{TT-ALS}~\cite{Holtz2012} updates one core at a time at fixed rank.
Each local solve is exact, so the objective decreases monotonically, but $\chi$
must be fixed in advance and the method is sensitive to the initial guess; it
can stall in a local minimum of the fixed-rank manifold.
\textbf{TT-mALS}~\cite{Holtz2012} (modified ALS, equivalently two-site DMRG)
optimizes a pair of neighbouring cores and splits them by SVD, as in the sweep
above. The truncation tolerance of that SVD sets the new bond dimension, so the
method is rank adaptive, and because the two-site block explores directions
unavailable to any single core, it escapes local minima that trap one-site
sweeps. Its price is the larger local problem. \textbf{TT-AMEn}~\cite{Dolgov2014}
returns to one-site updates but adapts the rank by \textbf{subspace enrichment}
with directions taken from the current residual $b - Au$. It recovers rank
adaptivity at roughly one-site cost, comes with convergence guarantees beyond
symmetric positive definite matrices, and is often the best default.

\paragraph{Practical settings.}
The worst-case $O(\chi^6)$ assumes that the local solve runs to full accuracy at
maximum rank on every site of every sweep, which in practice it rarely does. The
gap is closed by the practical settings: the sweep count, the local tolerance,
the rank cap and, above all, the starting guess. A good schedule starts with a
loose tolerance and a low rank cap and tightens both over successive sweeps. In
a time-stepping context a \textbf{warm start} from the previous time step
matters more than any other setting, because consecutive solutions are close;
from a warm start a few sweeps usually suffice, and for the small steps of the
heat equation one or two (\cref{subsec:heat-implicit-results}). Because the SVD in the
two-site split may return a larger rank than it started with, it is the
tolerance $\varepsilon$, not $\chi$, that becomes the control parameter.

\begin{remark}[Rule of thumb]
  Use sweeping solvers for structured PDE operators, where the MPO is known in
  closed form and warm starts are available. Keep Krylov methods for operators
  that are effectively black boxes.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Choosing Tolerances}
\label{subsec:error-budget}
% Keywords: error sources, error budget, residual vs. error, grid refinement.

Before measuring what the implicit solver buys, we need to decide what
\enquote{solved} means, because the answer fixes both the tolerance and the
stopping criterion.

\paragraph{Error sources.}
With an implicit scheme the error budget has three parts rather than the two of
Part~\partref{1}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{discretization} error, in space $O(\Delta x^2)$ and in time
    $O(\Delta t)$;
  \item \textbf{representation} error from truncation, set by $\varepsilon$
    and $\chi_{\max}$;
  \item \textbf{solver} error, which is new and entirely under the user's
    control.
\end{enumerate}
The solver error is measured by the relative residual of the one-step
scheme~\eqref{eq:one-step-scheme},
\begin{equation}
  \rho_k = \frac{\norm{A u^{k+1} - B u^{k}}}{\norm{B u^{k}}} ,
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
and this is the residual meant everywhere in these notes.

\paragraph{Which error should dominate.}
The discretization error should dominate. If it does not, the solver converges
efficiently to the wrong answer. In practice this means targeting a relative
residual~\eqref{eq:residual}, and a truncation tolerance $\varepsilon$, about one
order of magnitude below the expected discretization error, and no tighter,
since sweeping beyond that point adds cost without adding accuracy. The rank cap
$\chi_{\max}$ is then a safeguard on memory rather than an accuracy parameter.

\paragraph{Residual and error.}
A low residual does not imply a low error. The residual certifies the solve, not
the solution: the error is bounded by the residual times the condition number of
$A$, and for the Laplacian the condition number grows like $4^{n}$. The decisive
test of a numerical solution is therefore convergence under grid refinement.
Each refinement $n \to n+1$ should reduce the error by the factor the scheme
promises, $4$ for a second-order spatial discretization. When it stops doing so,
one of the other two error sources has taken over.

\subsection{Back to the Heat Equation}
\label{subsec:heat-implicit-results}
% Keywords: assembling (1 - alpha dt L) as an MPO, warm starts, time to
% target error, explicit vs implicit comparison.

\paragraph{Assembly and solution.}
The operator $A = \mathbb{1} - \alpha \Delta t\, L$ is built directly as an MPO.
Its bond dimension is about $3$, and its cores are written down
analytically~\cite{Kazeev2012}, so there is no numerical construction step and
no assembly error. The right-hand side $B u^k = u^k$ is simply the previous
state. The system is solved with DMRG or AMEn, warm-started from $u^k$; since
consecutive solutions differ by $O(\Delta t)$, one or two sweeps usually suffice.

\paragraph{Explicit against implicit.}
We compare explicit Euler at $\alpha\Delta t/\Delta x^2 = 0.4$ with backward
Euler and DMRG at the largest $\Delta t$ that meets a target error of $10^{-2}$,
on grids of $2^n$ points, $n = 5, \dots, 12$. Measured as wall-clock time to the
target error, explicit Euler is faster on coarse grids, but its cost grows like
$4^n$ through the step count. Backward Euler with DMRG overtakes it around
$n = 9$. At $n = 12$ it needs only $32$ steps and is about $89\times$ faster than
the projected explicit run, which no longer fits a $120\,\mathrm{s}$ budget. The
per-step relative residual~\eqref{eq:residual} of the DMRG solve sits at
$10^{-8}$--$10^{-6}$, well below the target error. As
\cref{subsec:error-budget} explains, this certifies the solve; whether the
solution is right is decided by the error target and by refinement.

\begin{remark}[How to compare schemes]
  Compare \textbf{total wall-clock time to a target error}, never cost per step.
  Implicit stepping loses on the second measure and wins on the first, because
  it takes far fewer steps.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Other schemes on the same solver.}
Changing the scheme around the same solver is inexpensive. Crank--Nicolson,
\begin{equation*}
  \bigl(\mathbb{1} - \tfrac{\alpha\Delta t}{2}L\bigr)u^{k+1}
  = \bigl(\mathbb{1} + \tfrac{\alpha\Delta t}{2}L\bigr)u^{k},
\end{equation*}
is the one-step scheme~\eqref{eq:one-step-scheme} with
$B = \mathbb{1} + \tfrac{\alpha\Delta t}{2}L$. It is second order and still
unconditionally stable, and the extra MPO application on the right-hand side is
negligible against the solve.

\subsection{Getting Functions into Tensor-Train Format}
\label{subsec:finding-qtt}
% Keywords: explicit QTT constructions, TT-SVD, TT-cross / TCI, matrix cross
% interpolation, maxvol, nested pivots, variants of TT-cross, failure modes.

So far we have assumed that the initial condition and the right-hand side are
available as tensor trains. That assumption must now be discharged, and the way
it is discharged decides whether the method scales.

\paragraph{Three routes.}
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Route} & \textsc{Cost} & \textsc{Requires} \\
    \midrule
    Explicit constructions & none                        & closed form for $f$ \\
    TT-SVD                 & $O(2^n)$                    & $f$ on the entire grid \\
    TT-cross / TCI         & $O(n\chi^2)$ evaluations    & only a black box $f$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
For polynomials, sines, cosines, exponentials and the other families tabulated
in \cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}, the cores are written down by
hand~\cite{Oseledets2013}; the construction is exact and requires no sampling.
TT-SVD is exact up to the truncation tolerance and quasi-optimal
(\cref{subsec:mps}), and it is efficient for moderate grids, but it needs the
dense vector of all $N$ values and is memory-bound in
practice~\cite{Roehrig2022}; randomized variants exist. TT-cross, also called
tensor cross interpolation (TCI), queries the function adaptively and builds the
tensor train from $O(n\chi^2)$ evaluations of $f$, without ever forming the full
tensor~\cite{Oseledets2010}. It has no convergence guarantee, but it is the
route that scales, and it is the one developed below.
\margintext{TT-cross is what keeps the programme from being circular. Without
it you would need the $2^n$-vector in order to compress the $2^n$-vector.}

\paragraph{Matrix cross interpolation.}
Write the full row and column index sets of a matrix $M$ as $\mathbb{I}$ and
$\mathbb{J}$, and select $\chi$ rows $\mathcal{I} \subset \mathbb{I}$ and $\chi$
columns $\mathcal{J} \subset \mathbb{J}$, called \textbf{pivots}. Then
\begin{equation}
  M = M(\mathbb{I}, \mathbb{J})
  \approx M(\mathbb{I}, \mathcal{J})\, M(\mathcal{I}, \mathcal{J})^{-1}\,
  M(\mathcal{I}, \mathbb{J}),
  \label{eq:matrix-ci}
\end{equation}%
that is, we keep $\chi$ columns and $\chi$ rows and invert the $\chi \times \chi$
\textbf{pivot matrix} at their intersection~\cite{Goreinov2010}. This is the
skeleton, or CUR, decomposition. If $M$ has exact rank $\chi$ and the pivot
matrix is nonsingular, \eqref{eq:matrix-ci} is exact, and heuristic pivot
searches find such pivots and recover the matrix to machine precision.

The choice of pivots decides the stability, because the inverse of the pivot
matrix appears in~\eqref{eq:matrix-ci} and a badly conditioned pivot matrix
destroys the reconstruction. The \textbf{maxvol} principle chooses the
$\chi \times \chi$ submatrix of maximum volume, that is, of largest
$\abs{\det}$.\margintext{The maximum-volume principle is due to Goreinov and
Tyrtyshnikov (2001); Oseledets and Tyrtyshnikov carried it up to tensor trains
in 2010.} For that choice the interpolation coefficients
$M(\mathbb{I},\mathcal{J})M(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})^{-1}$ are bounded by one in
modulus, and for an approximately rank-$\chi$ matrix the error
of~\eqref{eq:matrix-ci} is within a factor $(\chi+1)$ of the best rank-$\chi$
approximation in the Chebyshev (entrywise maximum) norm. Finding the true
maximum-volume submatrix is NP-hard. The practical algorithm swaps rows greedily
until no single swap increases $\abs{\det}$; it lands within a controlled factor
of the maximum and costs $O(M\chi^2)$ per sweep for $M$ rows.

\paragraph{Extension to tensor trains.}
Cross interpolation is applied recursively down the chain. Unfold the tensor as
(first index) $\times$ (all others) and cross-interpolate; the remainder is a
tensor with one index fewer, which is unfolded again, and so on. The result is a
chain of sampled fibres separated by inverse pivot matrices, which merge into an
ordinary tensor train. The pivot sets are kept \textbf{nested}: the left
multi-indices chosen at bond $k+1$ extend those chosen at bond $k$, so that the
result interpolates $f$ exactly on all sampled fibres. In algorithmic form, keep
for each bond $k$ a set of row multi-indices $\mathcal{I}_k$ and column
multi-indices $\mathcal{J}_k$, and proceed as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Initialize $\mathcal{I}_k$ and $\mathcal{J}_k$, either at random or from
    a coarse pass over the domain.
  \item Sweeping left to right, form the slice
    $F_k = f(\mathcal{I}_k,\, i_k,\, \mathcal{J}_k)$, reshaped as a
    $\chi d \times \chi$ matrix, by varying only the local index $i_k$ while the
    pivots hold everything else fixed. This is the only place $f$ is called.
  \item Apply maxvol to $F_k$: select the $\chi$ rows of maximum $\abs{\det}$
    and use them to update $\mathcal{I}_{k+1}$.
  \item Set the core to $F_k \hat{F}_k^{-1}$, where $\hat{F}_k$ is the selected
    maximum-volume submatrix, and carry the remaining factor to the right.
  \item Sweep back from right to left, and repeat until the cross error,
    measured on fresh sample points, plateaus.
\end{enumerate}
Each core costs $O(d\chi^2)$ evaluations, the whole train $O(nd\chi^2)$
evaluations and $O(n\chi^3)$ arithmetic, independent of $N = 2^n$. Quantics
combined with cross interpolation gives \textbf{QTCI}, a parsimonious
representation of multivariate functions at very high
resolution~\cite{Ritter2024}.

\begin{remark}[Interpolation property]
  The resulting tensor train is exact on every fibre it sampled; that is what
  $\hat{F}_k^{-1}$ in step~4 enforces. This is both the algorithm's main
  strength and its main weakness: it reproduces sampled values exactly,
  including sampled noise, and it says nothing about the fibres it never
  examined.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Variants of TT-cross.}
The variants differ mainly in how the pivots are found~\cite{Nunez2025}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{TCI}: nested pivots for a global interpolation;
  \item \textbf{rook search}: the pivots are greedily increased after each
    sweep;
  \item \textbf{informed pivots}: the structure of the function is used to
    choose the initial pivots;
  \item \textbf{DMRG-cross}~\cite{Savostyanov2011}: a two-site block is formed
    to search for new pivots, which makes the rank adaptive;
  \item \textbf{AMEn-cross}: AMEn residual directions supply new pivots;
  \item \textbf{TT-Opt}: TT-cross is used for grid-search optimization.
\end{enumerate}
Rectangular maxvol keeps more than $\chi$ pivots and improves robustness at
modest extra cost; it is generally worth enabling. Rank-adaptive variants insert
pivots where the local error is largest instead of fixing $\chi$ in
advance~\cite{Nunez2025}. Mature implementations exist in \texttt{xfac} and
\texttt{Tensor\-Cross\-Inter\-polation.jl}~\cite{Nunez2025}.

\paragraph{TT-cross in practice.}
The working recipe is short:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item choose the bit ordering, interleaved or stacked
    (\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators});
  \item set a tolerance;
  \item sweep until the cross error plateaus;
  \item round.
\end{enumerate}
Convergence is heuristic, and there is no guarantee for adversarial or noisy
black boxes. The result should always be validated on points the cross never
sampled.

\begin{remark}[Failure modes to expect]
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A feature narrower than the pivots can reach is missed entirely. A
      Gaussian spike of width $10^{-3}$ is recovered to machine precision; the
      same spike at width $10^{-5}$, placed at an irrational position, comes
      back as \emph{identically zero} at every rank we tried, with a reported
      cross error that stays small throughout.
    \item Poorly placed pivots cause features to be missed. The method never
      samples them, so it never learns that they exist, and the reported cross
      error stays small while the answer is wrong. If the interesting region is
      known, the algorithm should be told (informed pivots).
    \item A noisy $f$ is interpolated faithfully, noise included: extra pivots
      fit the noise, so adding rank makes the error worse and the rank is
      overestimated. Exact interpolation on the sampled fibres is a virtue for
      clean data and a liability for noisy data
      (\cref{subsec:part2-open-problems}).
  \end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Boundary Conditions in the Operator}
\label{subsec:boundary-conditions}
% Keywords: Dirichlet, periodic, Neumann, ghost points, edge cores, lifting,
% boundary data on the right-hand side.

Initial conditions enter as tensor trains built by the routes above. Boundary
conditions enter differently: in a finite-difference discretization they are
part of the operator and of the right-hand side, and each standard
treatment~\cite{LeVeque2007} has a direct counterpart in the MPO.

\paragraph{Homogeneous Dirichlet conditions.}
The second-difference matrix $\Delta_{2^n} = \mathrm{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1)$
of~\eqref{eq:qtt-laplacian} already encodes $u = 0$ at both ends. Its first and
last rows are the three-point stencil with the boundary value, which is zero,
removed. No modification is needed, and the MPO keeps bond dimension~$3$.

\paragraph{Periodic conditions.}
Periodicity couples the first and last grid points, adding $-1$ in the two
corners of the matrix:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta^{\mathrm{per}}_{2^n} = \Delta_{2^n} - J^{\otimes n} - (J^{T})^{\otimes n},
  \label{eq:periodic-laplacian}
\end{equation}
with $J$ as in~\eqref{eq:qtt-laplacian}. The corner matrix $e_N e_1^{T}$ is the
Kronecker product of $n$ copies of $J^{T} = e_2 e_1^{T}$, because the last grid
point has all bits equal to one and the first has all bits equal to zero; each
corner is therefore a rank-1 MPO, and the periodic Laplacian has bond dimension
at most $3 + 1 + 1 = 5$ before rounding. In the carry-automaton picture of
Part~\partref{1}, the difference is a single choice in the edge core: the
non-periodic shift discards a carry that leaves the most significant bit, while
the periodic shift wraps it around. Only the edge cores change.

\paragraph{Inhomogeneous Dirichlet conditions.}
For $u(0) = g_0$ and $u(1) = g_1$, standard practice moves the known boundary
values to the right-hand side. Writing $L$ for the discrete Laplacian with
homogeneous conditions, the discrete operator applied to $u$ becomes
$L u + (g_0\, e_1 + g_1\, e_N)/\Delta x^2$, and implicit
Euler~\eqref{eq:heat-implicit-axb} becomes
\begin{equation*}
  \left(\mathbb{1} - \alpha \Delta t\, L\right) u^{k+1}
  = u^{k} + \frac{\alpha \Delta t}{\Delta x^2}\left(g_0\, e_1 + g_1\, e_N\right).
\end{equation*}
The unit vectors $e_1$ and $e_N$ are rank-1 tensor trains (products of one-hot
bit vectors), so the boundary data raise the bond dimension of the right-hand
side by at most two and leave the operator unchanged. An equivalent route is
\emph{lifting}: write $u = w + \ell$ with a known function $\ell$ that satisfies
the boundary conditions and solve for $w$ with homogeneous conditions. For the
heat equation with constant $g_0, g_1$, the linear interpolant $\ell$ has bond
dimension~2 (\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}) and is annihilated by the
second difference, so $w$ obeys the homogeneous problem.

\paragraph{Neumann conditions and ghost points.}
A zero-flux condition is imposed through a ghost point outside the domain. With
the first-order closure $u_0 = u_1$, the ghost value is eliminated and the first
diagonal entry of $\Delta_{2^n}$ changes from $2$ to $1$. That change is the
rank-1 MPO $e_1 e_1^{T} = E^{\otimes n}$ with $E = \mathrm{diag}(1,0)$, so the
corrected operator again has small bond dimension. The same ghost-point device
folds inflow and outflow conditions into the differential MPOs of the
Navier--Stokes solver in Part~\partref{3}.

\paragraph{Several dimensions.}
In $D$ dimensions the Laplacian is a sum of one-dimensional Laplacians in
Kronecker form, for example $L_x \otimes \mathbb{1} + \mathbb{1} \otimes L_y$ in
two dimensions. Each one-dimensional factor carries its own boundary condition,
so mixed conditions such as periodic in $x$ and Dirichlet in $y$ cost nothing
beyond the one-dimensional constructions above.

\subsection{Case Study: An Immersed-Body Mask}
\label{subsec:tt-cross-geometry}
% Keywords: TT-cross for geometry, mapped domains, indicator functions,
% smoothed MPS masks, curved boundaries, Hadamard product.

Geometry is the first place where the tensor-network constraints force a
modelling decision rather than an implementation detail, and it is a hard test
of TT-cross. The literature uses TT-cross for geometry in two ways. Spectral
collocation and finite-element solvers in TT format map a reference square
$[-1,1]^2 \ni (\xi, \eta)$ onto a curved quadrilateral by a map
$\Phi(\xi,\eta)$, and need the Jacobian $J_\Phi = \partial(x,y)/\partial(\xi,\eta)$
and related geometric factors as tensor trains; TT-cross builds them from
pointwise evaluations~\cite{Adak2024,Kornev2024}. Alternatively, the Cartesian
grid is kept and the body is encoded as a \textbf{mask} tensor train that is
applied to the field. This is the route taken in Part~\partref{3}, and the
subject of this case study.

\paragraph{Sharp boundaries.}
The goal is to place an arbitrary immersed object, such as a cylinder or an
airfoil, on a grid of $N$ points per coordinate without touching all of them.
The natural object is an indicator function $\theta(x)$, built from a level-set
function $q$ that vanishes on the boundary: $\theta$ is one in the fluid and
zero in the body. A common expectation is that a sharp edge has slowly decaying
singular values, so that $\chi$ explodes. That expectation is wrong, and it is
worth being precise about why, because it points to the wrong fix.

A one-dimensional step function has quantics rank~\textbf{2}, at any resolution
and wherever the jump sits. Quantics never sees the jump; it sees the comparison
$\mathbb{1}[i > i_0]$, which a two-state automaton decides digit by digit
(\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}). Sharpness alone costs no rank.

What is expensive is a boundary that is \emph{curved}, and therefore not aligned
with the binary index. A circle cuts across every length scale at once, so every
bond has to carry information about where it is, and unlike everything else in
\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}, the cost grows as the grid is refined. A
straight edge stays at $\chi=2$ from $N=64$ to $N=1024$, whereas a disc indicator
on an $N \times N$ grid needs $\chi = 17, 50, 128$ at $N = 64, 256, 1024$.

\paragraph{Where TT-cross fails.}
The more serious problem with a sharp indicator is not its rank but its
behaviour under TT-cross. Built by cross interpolation, a sharp indicator shows
the failure modes of \cref{subsec:finding-qtt}: pieces of the boundary are
missed or misplaced in blocks. Measured pointwise, the error
$\abs{\mathrm{TT}_\theta - \theta(x)}$ of a TT-cross encoding of the sharp
indicator consists of whole blocks of the circle that are wrong. The remedy is to
give up the sharp edge deliberately, in a controlled way, so that the function
handed to TT-cross is one it can sample reliably.

\paragraph{The smoothed mask.}
\margintext{The smoothed mask is from Peddinti \emph{et al.} (2024).}
Replace the indicator $\theta$ by a smooth mask $m$, with $q$ vanishing on the
object boundary $\partial\Omega$~\cite{Peddinti2024},
\begin{equation}
  m(x) = 1 - e^{-\beta\left(q(x) + \abs{q(x)}\right)},
  \qquad q|_{\partial\Omega} = 0 ,
  \label{eq:smoothed-mask}
\end{equation}%
where $\beta > 0$ sets the sharpness, and build it by TT-cross as a tensor train
$m(x) \approx A_1^{i_1} A_2^{i_2} \cdots A_n^{i_n}$. The pointwise error
$\abs{\mathrm{TT}_m - \theta(x)}$ of this encoding is confined to a thin, uniform
band along the boundary instead of whole blocks.

The construction has two properties that make its error controllable. First,
$q + \abs{q}$ vanishes identically wherever $q < 0$, so $m$ is exactly zero
inside the body and rises smoothly outside it; no approximation is made in the
interior. Second, the $\ell_2$ distance to the true indicator scales as
$\sqrt{\ell_0^{D-2}/\beta}$ in $D$ dimensions, where $\ell_0$ is the boundary
length scale. The error is therefore controlled by a single parameter and decays
as $\beta^{-1/2}$; a fit on a disc at $N=512$ gives an exponent of $-0.53$. The
approximation is a modelling choice with a known error bar.

\paragraph{What the smoothing buys.}
It is tempting to summarize~\eqref{eq:smoothed-mask} as \enquote{sharp edges
have high rank, so smooth them}. The measurement does not support that. On a
disc at $N=512$:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    & $\chi$ at $10^{-1}$ & $\chi$ at $10^{-2}$ & $\chi$ at $10^{-6}$
      & $\ell_2$ to indicator \\
    \midrule
    sharp indicator  & 33 & 75 & 80 & 0 \\
    $\beta = 50$     & 13 & 34 & 92 & 0.28 \\
    $\beta = 1000$   & 27 & 74 & 94 & 0.059 \\
    $\beta = 5000$   & 31 & 78 & 88 & 0.023 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
At tight tolerance the smoothed mask is no cheaper than the sharp indicator; it
is a different function with its own structure. A rank saving appears only at
loose tolerance and small $\beta$, where $\beta$ trades geometric fidelity
against rank at a known price. The main benefit lies elsewhere: the smoothed mask
makes TT-cross robust. Its errors are confined to a band of known width along the
boundary rather than scattered in blocks, and the size of that band is set by
$\beta$. This resolves the apparent tension above. Sharpness costs no rank in
itself, and the edge is softened not to save rank but to make the encoding
reliable and its error quantifiable. Part~\partref{3} applies the mask to the
velocity field by an elementwise (Hadamard) product at every time step.

\subsection{Open Problems}
\label{subsec:part2-open-problems}
% Keywords: TT preconditioning, explicit interpolative QTT constructions,
% distributed TT algorithms, robust TT recovery, rank blow-up, error control,
% nonlinearity.

The solvers and encodings of this part work well on the heat equation, but
several questions about them remain open. Four concern the algorithms
themselves.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TT preconditioning for stiff problems.} The condition number of
    the discrete Laplacian grows like $4^n$, so at high quantics resolution the
    solver error of \cref{subsec:error-budget} is amplified accordingly.
    Multilevel preconditioners that remain stable in low-rank format exist for
    elliptic problems~\cite{Bachmayr2020}; a general recipe does not. The
    difficulty is structural: a good preconditioner approximates $A^{-1}$, and a
    low-rank $A$ rarely has a low-rank inverse.
  \item \textbf{Explicit QTT construction from known interpolation schemes.}
    Instead of sampling adaptively, the cores can be built directly from
    multiscale interpolation~\cite{Lindsey2023}, or a coarse tensor-network or
    quantum-state encoding can be upsampled to a finer grid~\cite{Guzman2026}.
  \item \textbf{Distributed TT algorithms.} Sweeps are inherently sequential.
    GPU-accelerated solvers~\cite{Hoelscher2025} and multicore studies of TT
    linear solvers~\cite{Roehrig2025} are a start, not an answer.
  \item \textbf{Robust TT recovery.} TT-cross overestimates the ranks under
    noise (\cref{subsec:finding-qtt}). Whether this can be avoided is open.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Rank blow-up and error control.}
Two further questions concern the solve as a whole. The first is rank blow-up.
Non-smooth data, sharp interfaces and shocks push the rank of the solution up,
and the iterates of a Krylov method or an early sweep can exceed the rank of the
final solution by a wide margin; it is not known in general where the method
stops paying for itself. The second is error control. There is no sharp
a-posteriori bound that links the per-step truncation tolerance $\varepsilon$ to
the PDE error accumulated over many steps. In practice one assumes linear
accumulation, $O(K\varepsilon)$ after $K$ steps, and checks it empirically.

\paragraph{Nonlinearity.}
The largest open question is nonlinearity, and it is where the course goes next.
Everything in this part relied on the operator being linear: $A$ is a fixed MPO,
and each step is one linear solve. A nonlinear term requires products of tensor
trains, and an elementwise product squares the bond dimension before rounding.
Picard iteration converges linearly and Newton iteration quadratically, but every
iteration re-rounds, and the interaction between iteration error and truncation
error is not understood.

\paragraph{Summary and hand-off.}
This part paid the two debts left by Part~\partref{1}. Implicit time stepping
removes the Courant restriction at the cost of a linear solve per step, and the
sweeping solvers (ALS, mALS/DMRG, AMEn) carry out that solve on the low-rank
manifold, with the residual~\eqref{eq:residual} certifying the solve and grid
refinement certifying the solution. TT-cross brings functions and geometry into
the format from $O(n\chi^2)$ samples, boundary conditions enter through the edge
cores of the MPO and the right-hand side, and a smoothed mask makes an immersed
body a reliable input for TT-cross. Part~\partref{3} takes these tools to the
incompressible Navier--Stokes equations, where the advection term is nonlinear
and a linear pressure solve is needed at every step, and asks how far the ranks
stretch when the scales are strongly coupled.
