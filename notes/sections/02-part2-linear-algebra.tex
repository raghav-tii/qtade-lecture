\paragraph{Recap.}
Part~\partref{1} represented functions as quantics tensor trains (QTTs) and
revisited the heat equation~\eqref{eq:heat} with explicit Euler,
\begin{equation*}
  \partial_t u = \alpha\, \partial_x^2 u
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  u^{k+1} = \left(\mathbb{1} + \alpha \Delta t\, L\right) u^{k},
\end{equation*}%
carrying out every operation with two primitives: apply an operator, then round.
Is an explicit scheme enough? It is not, and the moment a method requires
\emph{solving} rather than \emph{applying}, we need genuine linear algebra on
the MPS manifold.

Part~\partref{2} follows the three blocks of the slides. \emph{Implicit
time-stepping}: why stability forces an implicit scheme
(\cref{subsec:heat-implicit-euler}), how to solve $Au = b$ in tensor format
(\cref{subsec:linear-solvers}), what that buys for the heat equation
(\cref{subsec:heat-implicit-results}), and how to decide on bond dimension and
stopping criteria (\cref{subsec:error-budget}). \emph{Encoding boundary and
initial conditions}: constructing an MPS from a function we can only sample
(\cref{subsec:finding-qtt}), which is what lets us put an immersed body on a
$2^{30}$-cell mesh without touching $2^{30}$ cells
(\cref{subsec:tt-cross-geometry}). \emph{Open problems}:
\cref{subsec:part2-open-problems}.

\subsection{Implicit Time-Stepping}
\label{subsec:heat-implicit-euler}
% Keywords: motivation (stability), CFL/Courant criterion, implicit Euler,
% A-stability, one linear solve per time step.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Two questions, two short answers.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Why do we need implicit solvers?} Stability.
  \item \textbf{Why do we need stability?} To decouple the time discretization
    from the space discretization.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The Courant criterion.}
Explicit Euler~\eqref{eq:heat-explicit} is stable only under the Courant
(CFL) condition~\cite{Courant1928}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\alpha\, \Delta t}{\Delta x^{2}} \le \frac{1}{2}.
  \label{eq:cfl-heat}
\end{equation}%
With $\Delta x = 2^{-n}$ this forces $\Delta t \sim 4^{-n}$: memory grows like
$n$, the number of steps like $4^{n}$. At $n \gtrsim 20$ the bound is unusable,
and the argument strengthens with every bit of resolution quantics buys.
Above the bound the highest-frequency mode is amplified by
$\abs{1 - 4\alpha\Delta t/\Delta x^2} > 1$ per step, so round-off alone is
enough to destroy the solution.

\begin{remark}
  Note the shape of the trade. Quantics makes very large $n$ affordable in
  memory; the explicit stability condition then makes large $n$ unaffordable in
  time. The compression only pays off in combination with an implicit scheme,
  which is only affordable because of \cref{subsec:linear-solvers}.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Implicit Euler.}
Backward Euler replaces the update by a linear system,
\begin{equation}
  \left(\mathbb{1} - \alpha \Delta t\, L\right) u^{k+1} = u^{k}.
  \label{eq:heat-implicit}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item It is \textbf{A-stable with respect to $\Delta t$}. The amplification
    factor is $(1 + \alpha \Delta t\, \lambda)^{-1}$ for every eigenvalue
    $\lambda \ge 0$ of $-L$, so it never exceeds one whatever $\Delta t$ is.
  \item Only \textbf{accuracy}, not stability, is affected by the choice of
    $\Delta t$; the step is set by the error you can tolerate, not by
    $\Delta x^2$.
  \item The price: it \textbf{needs a linear system solver for each timestep}
    instead of a matrix--vector product --- strictly more work per step.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Solving \texorpdfstring{$Au = b$}{Au = b} in Tensor-Train Format}
\label{subsec:linear-solvers}
% Keywords: direct / iterative / variational solvers, fixed-rank TT manifold,
% TT-GMRES, Riemannian solvers, DMRG, ALS, mALS, AMEn, cost.

\paragraph{Three families of solvers.}
Classically, linear solvers for $Au = b$ come in three kinds:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{direct} solvers operate on the matrix elements until the matrix
    is easy to invert (LU, Cholesky);
  \item \textbf{iterative} solvers recursively improve a guess until
    convergence, using global operations (CG, GMRES);
  \item \textbf{variational} solvers vary the parameters of the solution to
    minimize a loss function.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Ground rules in the MPS representation.}
The task: $A$ is given as an MPO, $b$ as an MPS, and we want $u$ as an MPS.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Assume that the operator $A$ and the vector $b$ admit a low-rank MPS
    (MPO).
  \item We might not know the rank of the solution MPS in advance.
  \item The ideal solver should find the solution \emph{and} its rank.
\end{itemize}
A useful fact to keep in mind: MPS of fixed ranks form a smooth
\textbf{manifold}~\cite{Holtz2012a}. It is not a vector space --- the sum of two
rank-$\chi$ trains has rank up to $2\chi$ --- which is what makes every family
above behave differently in tensor format.

\paragraph{Which families survive.}
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Family} & \textsc{In TT format?} & \textsc{Examples} \\
    \midrule
    Direct      & no  & no easy access to matrix elements \\
    Iterative   & yes & TT-GMRES, Riemannian solvers \\
    Variational & yes & TT-ALS, TT-mALS, TT-AMEn \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Direct inversion is out.} There is no easy access to matrix
    elements, and $A^{-1}$ has no reason to be low-rank even when $A$ and $u$
    both are. If you are lucky you can construct an MPO for the inverse
    explicitly: the QTT Laplacian is the instructive exception, whose inverse
    admits an explicit low-rank representation~\cite{Kazeev2012}.
  \item \textbf{Iterative methods are possible, but often not worth it.} For
    fixed-point (CG) or Krylov-based (GMRES) methods in TT
    format~\cite{Dolgov2012a,Ballani2013}, with a rounding step after every
    operation, the rank of the \emph{intermediate} iterates can blow up even
    though the final solution has low rank: each matrix--vector product
    multiplies $\chi$ by $\chi_{\mathrm{MPO}}$ and each vector addition adds
    ranks. Truncation must then be aggressive, and aggressive truncation
    destroys the orthogonality of the Krylov basis.
  \item \textbf{Riemannian optimization} over the fixed-rank
    manifold~\cite{Kressner2016} avoids the rank blow-up by construction. It is
    quite useful for nonlinear problems, but for linear problems it is unclear
    whether it is worth the extra effort: it needs well-behaved retraction and
    vector-transport routines, and it is not rank adaptive by construction.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{DMRG in a slide.}
\margintext{The one-site/two-site distinction is the same one that separates
White's original DMRG from its later single-site variants. The trade is rank
adaptivity against local cost.}
Reformulate the linear system as a variational problem~\cite{Oseledets2012},
\begin{equation}
  \min_x \norm{Ax - b}^2
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  \min_x \tfrac{1}{2}\, x^{\top}\! A x - x^{\top} b \quad (A \succ 0),
  \label{eq:tt-linear-min}
\end{equation}%
the second form being available when $A$ is symmetric positive definite. Prefer
it when you can: it avoids squaring the condition number, which the residual
form does. Then:
\begin{itemize}
  \item tensor networks are \textbf{multilinear} objects;
  \item so \textbf{freeze all tensors but one}, which reduces the problem to a
    small linear problem in that tensor alone;
  \item solve the local (reduced) problems by \textbf{sweeping} through the
    network;
  \item use the \textbf{gauge freedom} to your advantage: with the chain in
    mixed canonical form centred on the active tensor, the local problem is a
    dense system of size $\chi^2 d$ that inherits the conditioning of $A$.
\end{itemize}
The derivation of the local problem and its environments is given in
\cref{subsec:appendix-dmrg}.

\paragraph{Variants of DMRG solvers.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TT-ALS}~\cite{Holtz2012} --- single-site updates at fixed rank.
    Each local solve is exact, so the objective decreases monotonically, but
    $\chi$ is fixed in advance and the method is sensitive to the initial
    guess; it can stall in a local minimum of the fixed-rank manifold.
  \item \textbf{TT-mALS}~\cite{Holtz2012} (modified ALS, i.e.\ two-site DMRG)
    --- optimize a \emph{pair} of neighbouring tensors, then split them by SVD.
    The truncation tolerance of that SVD sets the new bond dimension, so the
    method is rank adaptive and escapes local minima that trap one-site sweeps.
    The price is that local solves are expensive: size $\chi^2 d^2$, cost up to
    $O(\chi^6)$.
  \item \textbf{TT-AMEn}~\cite{Dolgov2014} --- single-site updates whose
    rank is adapted by \textbf{subspace enrichment} with directions taken from
    the current residual $b - Ax$. This recovers rank adaptivity at roughly
    one-site cost, comes with convergence guarantees beyond SPD matrices, and
    is often the best default.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Rule of thumb]
  Use sweeping methods for structured PDE operators, where the MPO is known in
  closed form and warm starts are available. Keep Krylov methods for when the
  operator is effectively a black box.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{What the solve actually costs.}
This is not a peripheral concern. In our incompressible CFD solver the
DMRG-type Poisson solve dominates every other part of the
pipeline~\cite{Peddinti2024}:

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Quantity} & \textsc{Value} \\
    \midrule
    Share of total runtime  & $80.1\%$ \\
    Worst-case local cost   & $O(n\chi^6)$ \\
    Observed total cost     & $O(n\chi^{4.1})$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
\margintext{The measured exponent $4.1$ against a worst case of $6$ is where all
the practical engineering lives. Warm-starting from the previous timestep is the
single largest contributor.}
The gap between the worst case and what is measured is where the practical knobs
live: sweep count, local tolerance, rank cap, and above all
\textbf{warm-starting from the previous timestep}. The worst-case $\chi^6$
assumes the local solve runs to full accuracy at maximum rank on every site of
every sweep; warm starts mean it almost never does.

\subsection{Back to the Heat Equation}
\label{subsec:heat-implicit-results}
% Keywords: assembling (1 - alpha dt L) as an MPO, warm starts, time to
% target error, explicit vs implicit comparison.

\paragraph{Assembling and solving.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Build $A = \mathbb{1} - \alpha \Delta t\, L$ directly as an MPO. Its
    rank is $\approx 3$ and the cores are written down
    analytically~\cite{Kazeev2012}; there is no numerical construction
    step and no assembly error.
  \item The right-hand side is simply the previous state MPS.
  \item Solve with DMRG or AMEn, \textbf{warm-started from $u^k$}. Consecutive
    solutions differ by $O(\Delta t)$, so one or two sweeps usually suffice.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The comparison.}
The slides compare explicit Euler at $\alpha\Delta t/\Delta x^2 = 0.4$ with
backward Euler + DMRG at the largest $\Delta t$ that meets a target error
$\varepsilon = 10^{-2}$, on grids of $2^n$ points, $n = 5, \dots, 12$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Wall-clock time to target error.} Explicit Euler is faster on
    coarse grids, but its cost grows like $4^n$ through the step count.
    Backward Euler + DMRG overtakes it around $n = 9$; at $n = 12$ it needs
    only $32$ steps and is about $89\times$ faster than the (projected)
    explicit run, which no longer fits a $120\,\mathrm{s}$ budget.
  \item \textbf{Residual of each step's equation.} The per-step relative
    residual $\norm{Au^{k+1} - Bu^{k}}/\norm{Bu^{k}}$ of the DMRG solve sits
    at $10^{-8}$--$10^{-6}$, orders of magnitude above that of the explicit
    update, yet the implicit solution meets the same error target. A small
    residual is not what is being bought; see \cref{subsec:error-budget}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[How to compare schemes]
  Compare \textbf{total wall-clock time to a target error}, never cost per step.
  Implicit stepping loses on the second measure and wins on the first, because
  it takes far fewer steps.
\end{remark}
\margintext{Forward pointer: this exact solve reappears in
\cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks} as the pressure-Poisson step of Chorin
projection, where it consumes $80\%$ of the runtime.}

Swapping the scheme around the same solver is cheap. Crank--Nicolson,
$\bigl(\mathbb{1} - \tfrac{\alpha\Delta t}{2}L\bigr)u^{k+1}
= \bigl(\mathbb{1} + \tfrac{\alpha\Delta t}{2}L\bigr)u^{k}$, is second order and
still unconditionally stable; the extra MPO application on the right-hand side
is negligible against the solve.

\subsection{Which Bond Dimension? And When to Stop?}
\label{subsec:error-budget}
% Keywords: error sources, error budget, residual vs. error, grid refinement.

\paragraph{Error sources.}
The budget now has three parts rather than two:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{discretization} error, in space $O(\Delta x^2)$ and time
    $O(\Delta t)$;
  \item \textbf{representation} error from truncation, set by $\varepsilon$
    and $\chi_{\max}$;
  \item \textbf{solver} error, measured by the residual
    $\norm{Au^{k+1} - u^{k}}$ --- new, and entirely under your control.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Rules.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rule of thumb: ensure the discretization error is the dominant
    error.} Otherwise you converge efficiently to the wrong answer, which is
    the least useful of all possible outcomes. In practice: target a relative
    residual (and a truncation tolerance) about one order of magnitude below the
    expected discretization error, and no tighter, since sweeping past that
    point is pure cost.
  \item \textbf{A low residual does not mean a low error.} The residual
    certifies the \emph{solve}, not the \emph{solution}: use it as a
    certificate of merit, not as a certificate of authenticity. The error is
    bounded by the residual times the condition number, and for the Laplacian
    the condition number grows like $4^{n}$.
  \item \textbf{The real test of a numerical solution: it should converge under
    grid refinement.} Each refinement $n \to n+1$ should reduce the error by the
    factor the scheme promises ($4$ for a second-order spatial
    discretization). When it stops doing so, one of the other two error
    sources has taken over.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Encoding Boundary and Initial Conditions}
\label{subsec:finding-qtt}
% Keywords: explicit QTT constructions, TT-SVD, TT-cross / TCI, matrix cross
% interpolation, maxvol, nested pivots, variants of TT-cross, failure modes.

So far we assumed that all initial and boundary conditions are easily available
in MPS form. That assumption has to be discharged, and how it is discharged
decides whether the method scales. Now we look at how to encode arbitrary
functions into QTTs.

\paragraph{Encoding functions as QTTs.}
There are three routes.

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Route} & \textsc{Cost} & \textsc{Requires} \\
    \midrule
    Explicit constructions & free                        & closed form for $f$ \\
    TT-SVD                 & $O(2^n)$                    & $f$ on the entire grid \\
    TT-cross / TCI         & $O(n\chi^2)$ evaluations    & only a black box $f$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Explicit constructions.} Polynomials, sine, cosine,
    exponential: for the families tabulated in
    \cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators} the small cores are written down by
    hand~\cite{Oseledets2013}. Exact, with no sampling and no error.
  \item \textbf{TT-SVD.} Requires the function value on the entire grid. It is
    exact and quasi-optimal (\cref{subsec:mps}) and efficient for reasonable
    grid sizes, but it needs the dense $2^n$ vector, and in practice it is
    memory-bound~\cite{Roehrig2022}. Randomized variants exist.
  \item \textbf{TT-cross (TCI).} Queries the function adaptively and
    interpolates within the QTT, building the MPS from $O(n\chi^2)$
    \textbf{evaluations} of $f$ without ever forming the full
    tensor~\cite{Oseledets2010}. There are no guarantees of convergence, but it
    is extremely efficient.
\end{itemize}

The third route is the one that scales, and the one that makes the method
practical rather than merely elegant.
\margintext{TT-cross is what keeps the programme from being circular. Without
it you would need the $2^n$-vector in order to compress the $2^n$-vector.}

\paragraph{Matrix cross interpolation.}
Write the full row and column index sets of a matrix $A$ as $\mathbb{I}$ and
$\mathbb{J}$, and select $r$ rows $\mathcal{I} \subset \mathbb{I}$ and $r$
columns $\mathcal{J} \subset \mathbb{J}$, called \textbf{pivots}. Then
\begin{equation}
  A = A(\mathbb{I}, \mathbb{J})
  \approx A(\mathbb{I}, \mathcal{J})\, A(\mathcal{I}, \mathcal{J})^{-1}\,
  A(\mathcal{I}, \mathbb{J}),
  \label{eq:matrix-ci}
\end{equation}%
i.e.\ we keep $r$ columns and $r$ rows and invert the intersecting
$r \times r$ submatrix, the \textbf{pivot matrix}~\cite{Goreinov2010}. This is
the skeleton (CUR) decomposition~\eqref{eq:skeleton}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item If $A$ has exact rank $r$ and the pivot matrix is nonsingular,
    \eqref{eq:matrix-ci} is exact, and heuristic pivot searches find such pivots
    and recover the matrix to machine precision.
  \item The quality of the pivots decides the stability. \textbf{maxvol}
    chooses the $r \times r$ submatrix of (near-)maximum volume, i.e.\ largest
    $\abs{\det}$; it is near-optimal and computable greedily by row swaps, and
    it bounds the interpolation coefficients
    $A(\mathbb{I},\mathcal{J})A(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})^{-1}$ by about one.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Extension to tensor trains.}
Apply~\eqref{eq:matrix-ci} recursively down the chain. Unfold the tensor as
(first index) $\times$ (all others) and cross-interpolate; the remainder is a
tensor with one index fewer, unfold it again, and so on. The result is a chain
of sampled fibres (\enquote{$T$-cores}) separated by inverse pivot matrices,
which merge into an ordinary MPS. The pivot sets are kept \textbf{nested}: the
left multi-indices chosen at bond $k+1$ extend those chosen at bond $k$, so that
the result interpolates $f$ exactly on all sampled fibres. Each core costs
$O(d\chi^2)$ evaluations, the whole train $O(nd\chi^2)$. Quantics combined with
cross interpolation gives \textbf{QTCI}: parsimonious representations of
multivariate functions at very high resolution~\cite{Ritter2024}. The
algorithm is spelled out in \cref{subsec:appendix-tt-cross}.

\paragraph{Variants of TT-cross.}
The variants differ mainly in how pivots are found~\cite{Nunez2025}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{TCI} --- nesting pivots for a global interpolation;
  \item \textbf{rook search} --- greedily increasing the pivots after each
    sweep;
  \item \textbf{informed pivots} --- using the structure of the function to
    choose the initial pivots;
  \item \textbf{DMRG-cross}~\cite{Savostyanov2011} --- creating a two-site
    block to search for new pivots, which makes the rank adaptive;
  \item \textbf{AMEn-cross} --- using AMEn residual directions for new pivots;
  \item \textbf{TT-Opt} --- using TT-cross for grid-search optimization.
\end{enumerate}
Mature implementations exist in \texttt{xfac} and
\texttt{Tensor\-Cross\-Inter\-polation.jl}~\cite{Nunez2025}.

\paragraph{TT-cross in practice.}
The working recipe is short:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item choose the bit ordering, interleaved or stacked
    (\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators});
  \item set a tolerance;
  \item sweep until the cross error plateaus;
  \item round.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Failure modes to expect]
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A feature narrower than the pivots can reach is missed entirely. A
      Gaussian spike of width $10^{-3}$ is recovered to machine precision; the
      same spike at width $10^{-5}$, placed at an irrational position, comes
      back as \emph{identically zero} at every rank we tried --- with a reported
      cross error that stays small throughout.
    \item Poorly placed pivots cause features to be missed entirely. The method
      never samples them, so it never learns they exist, and the reported cross
      error stays small while the answer is wrong. If you know where the
      interesting region is, tell the algorithm (informed pivots).
    \item A noisy $f$ is interpolated faithfully, noise included: extra pivots
      fit the noise, so adding rank makes the error worse and the rank is
      overestimated. Cross interpolation is exact on the fibres it
      samples, which is a virtue for clean data and a liability for dirty data
      (\cref{subsec:part2-open-problems}).
  \end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Encoding Geometry via TT-Cross}
\label{subsec:tt-cross-geometry}
% Keywords: TT-cross for geometry, mapped domains, indicator functions,
% smoothed MPS masks, curved boundaries, Hadamard product.

Geometry is where TT-cross earns its keep, and the first place where the
tensor-network constraints force a genuine modelling decision rather than an
implementation detail. Two uses appear in the literature.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mapped domains.} Spectral-collocation and finite-element
    solvers in TT format map a reference square $[-1,1]^2 \ni (\xi, \eta)$ onto
    a curved quadrilateral by a map $N(\xi,\eta)$, and need the Jacobian
    $J = \partial(x,y)/\partial(\xi,\eta)$ and related geometric factors
    as tensor trains; TT-cross builds them from pointwise
    evaluations~\cite{Adak2024,Kornev2024}.
  \item \textbf{Immersed bodies.} Keep a Cartesian grid and encode the body as
    a \textbf{mask} MPS that is applied to the flow field. This is the route we
    take in Part~\partref{3}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The problem with sharp boundaries.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The goal is to place an arbitrary immersed object --- a cylinder, an
    airfoil --- on a $2^n$ mesh \textbf{without touching $2^n$ cells}.
  \item The natural object is an indicator function $\theta(x)$, built from a
    level-set function $q$ vanishing on the boundary: one in the fluid, zero in
    the body.
  \item The reflex at this point is to say that a sharp edge has slowly decaying
    singular values, so $\chi$ explodes. \emph{That reflex is wrong}, and it is
    worth being precise about why, because it sends you looking for the wrong
    fix.
\end{itemize}

\margintext{Measure it before you believe either story. A one-dimensional
Heaviside is rank~2 at any resolution; a disc indicator on a $2^n\times2^n$ grid
needs $\chi = 17, 50, 128$ at $N = 64, 256, 1024$.}
A one-dimensional step function has quantics rank~\textbf{2}, at any resolution
and wherever the jump sits: quantics never sees the jump, it sees the comparison
$\mathbb{1}[i > m]$, which a two-state automaton decides digit by digit
(\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}). Sharpness alone is free.

What is expensive is a boundary that is \emph{curved}, and therefore not aligned
with the binary index. A circle cuts across every length scale at once, so every
bond has to carry information about where it is --- and unlike everything else in
\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}, the cost grows as the grid is refined. A
straight edge stays at $\chi=2$ from $N=64$ to $N=1024$; a disc goes from
$\chi=17$ to $\chi=128$ over the same range. Built by TT-cross, a sharp
indicator also shows the failure modes of \cref{subsec:finding-qtt}: pieces of
the boundary are missed or misplaced in blocks.

The fix is to give up the sharp edge \emph{deliberately}, and then bound what
that costs.

\paragraph{MPS mask generation.}
\margintext{This construction is from our 2024 framework. The point of the
$\sqrt{\ell_0^{D-2}/\alpha}$ bound is that the approximation is a modelling
choice with an error bar, not an undocumented hack.}
Replace the indicator $\theta$ by a smooth mask $m$, with $q$ vanishing on the
object boundary $\partial\Omega$~\cite{Peddinti2024},
\begin{equation}
  m(x) = 1 - e^{-\alpha\left(q(x) + \abs{q(x)}\right)},
  \qquad q|_{\partial\Omega} = 0 ,
  \label{eq:smoothed-mask}
\end{equation}%
and build it by TT-cross: $m(x) \approx A_1^{i_1} A_2^{i_2} \cdots A_N^{i_N}$.
On the slides, the pointwise error $\abs{\mathrm{MPS}_\theta - \theta(x)}$ of a
TT-cross encoding of the sharp indicator shows whole blocks of the circle
wrong, while $\abs{\mathrm{MPS}_m - \theta(x)}$ for the smoothed mask is
confined to a thin, uniform band along the boundary.

\begin{itemize}
  \item The combination $q + \abs{q}$ vanishes identically wherever $q < 0$, so
    $m$ is exactly zero inside the body and rises smoothly outside it. No
    approximation is made in the interior.
  \item The $\ell_2$ distance to the true indicator scales as
    $\sqrt{\ell_0^{D-2}/\alpha}$ in $D$ dimensions, where $\ell_0$ is the
    boundary length scale. The error is therefore controlled by a single knob
    and decays as $\alpha^{-1/2}$ --- a fit on a disc at $N=512$ gives an
    exponent of $-0.53$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[What the smoothing does and does not buy]
  It is tempting to summarise \eqref{eq:smoothed-mask} as \enquote{sharp edges
  have high rank, so smooth them}. The measurement does not support that. On a
  disc at $N=512$:

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
      \toprule
      & $\chi$ at $10^{-1}$ & $\chi$ at $10^{-2}$ & $\chi$ at $10^{-6}$
        & $\ell_2$ to indicator \\
      \midrule
      sharp indicator  & 33 & 75 & 80 & 0 \\
      $\alpha = 50$    & 13 & 34 & 92 & 0.28 \\
      $\alpha = 1000$  & 27 & 74 & 94 & 0.059 \\
      $\alpha = 5000$  & 31 & 78 & 88 & 0.023 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  At \emph{tight} tolerance the smoothed mask is no cheaper --- it is simply a
  different function with its own structure. The saving appears at the accuracy
  you actually want, and the defensible statement is therefore: \textbf{$\alpha$
  prices geometric fidelity against rank, and the price is known.} That the
  price is known is the whole point; it is what separates this from an
  undocumented hack.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Putting it together.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Build $m$ as an MPS by TT-cross to tolerance $\varepsilon$, at a cost
    independent of the mesh size.
  \item Apply it by an \textbf{elementwise (Hadamard) MPS product} on the
    velocity field each step (the mask-application stage of the solver
    pipeline: initialization, mask, divergence-free projection, time
    integration). No-slip is enforced without ever meshing the body, and without
    an immersed-boundary force term.
  \item Inlet and outlet conditions fold into the differential MPOs via
    \textbf{ghost cells}, so open boundary conditions keep the domain small.
  \item Demonstrated on flow past a cylinder (vortex shedding) and on a
    \textbf{NACA 0040 airfoil}: $\chi = 45$, $18{,}800$ parameters, a
    compression factor of $27.9$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Open Problems}
\label{subsec:part2-open-problems}
% Keywords: TT preconditioning, explicit interpolative QTT constructions,
% distributed TT algorithms, robust TT recovery, rank blow-up, error control,
% conservation.

The slides close with four questions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TT preconditioning for solving stiff problems.} The condition
    number of the discrete Laplacian grows like $4^n$, so at high quantics
    resolution the solver error of \cref{subsec:error-budget} is amplified
    accordingly. Multilevel preconditioners that remain stable in low-rank
    format exist for elliptic problems~\cite{Bachmayr2020}; a general recipe
    does not. The difficulty is structural: a good preconditioner approximates
    $A^{-1}$, and a low-rank $A$ rarely has a low-rank inverse.
  \item \textbf{Explicit QTT construction using known interpolation schemes.}
    Instead of sampling adaptively, build the cores directly from multiscale
    interpolation~\cite{Lindsey2023}, or upsample a coarse tensor-network or
    quantum-state encoding to a finer grid~\cite{Guzman2026}.
  \item \textbf{Distributed TT algorithms.} Sweeps are inherently sequential.
    GPU-accelerated solvers~\cite{Hoelscher2025} and multicore studies of TT
    linear solvers~\cite{Roehrig2025} are a start, not an answer.
  \item \textbf{Robust TT recovery.} TT-cross overestimates the ranks under
    noise (\cref{subsec:finding-qtt}). Can this be solved?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Further open questions.}
\margintext{Of everything in this subsection, preconditioning has the largest
immediate payoff. Recall that the Poisson solve is $80\%$ of runtime.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rank blow-up.} Shocks, sharp interfaces, non-smooth data and
    high-$\mathrm{Re}$ turbulence all push $\chi$ up. Where exactly does the
    method stop paying for itself?
  \item \textbf{Nonlinearity.} Products of MPSs square the bond dimension.
    Picard iteration converges linearly and Newton quadratically, but every
    iteration re-rounds, and the interaction between iteration error and
    truncation error is not understood.
  \item \textbf{Error control.} There is no sharp a-posteriori bound linking the
    per-step truncation tolerance $\varepsilon$ to the PDE error accumulated
    over many steps. In practice one assumes linear accumulation, $O(K\varepsilon)$
    after $K$ steps, and checks it empirically.
  \item \textbf{Conservation.} SVD truncation preserves neither mass nor energy
    nor $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$; it is optimal in the Frobenius norm, which
    knows nothing about the physics. Structure-preserving rounding is wide open.
  \item \textbf{Fair benchmarking.} Honest comparison against tuned classical
    CFD --- multigrid, spectral methods --- on equal hardware.
\end{itemize}
