\label{sec:part4}
\lecture{01}{10}{2026}

Parts~\partref{1}--\partref{3} assumed that the equation was known. They built
a representation for fields and operators, the linear algebra on top of it, and
spent both on the forward problem of solving the Navier--Stokes equations. This
part drops that assumption. What we have instead are \textbf{snapshots}
$\{\mathbf u_k\}$ of a state, from an experiment, from DNS output or from
sensors; what we lack is the operator, the closure, and sometimes the equation
itself. The claim of this part is that the curse of dimensionality that hits PDE
solvers hits data-driven models just as hard, in the SVD of DMD and in the
dictionary of SINDy, and that the same compressed format lifts it from both
without giving up interpretability.

Pipeline stages covered: the TN encoding (data loaded as a space--time tensor
train), time evolution (forecasting with the learned operator) and solution
retrieval (modes, eigenvalues and sparse equations). The first stage, the
equation, is now the unknown. Part~\partref{4} reuses the Part~\partref{3}
solver in two places: as the time-stepping alternative in
\cref{subsec:dmd-space-time}, and as the physical prior in the hybrid methods of
\cref{subsec:part4-outlook}. Otherwise it depends only on
Parts~\partref{1} and~\partref{2}.

\subsection{From Equations to Data}
\label{subsec:inverse-problem}
% Keywords: simulation vs. inverse problems vs. discovering physics,
% motivation, problem statement, the spectrum of data-driven methods, the
% curse of dimensionality in data.

\paragraph{A classification of tasks.}
\margintext{The three-way split follows the ETH course \emph{AI in the Sciences
and Engineering}~\cite{Mishra2024}.}
Write a scientific task as
\begin{equation}
  b = F(a),
  \label{eq:task}
\end{equation}%
with $a$ the input conditions, $F$ the physical model (usually a PDE) and $b$
the resulting properties. Which symbol is unknown classifies the task:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulation:} $F$ and $a$ are known, $b$ is wanted. This is what
    Parts~\partref{1}--\partref{3} did.
  \item \textbf{Inverse problems:} $F$ and $b$ are known, $a$ is wanted
    (initial data, parameters, sources).
  \item \textbf{Discovering unknown physics:} $F$ itself is wanted. Given pairs
    $\{(a_i, b_i)\}_{i=1}^{M}$, choose it from a model class $\mathcal M$,
    \begin{equation}
      \hat F = \arg\min_{G\in\mathcal M} \sum_i \lVert b_i - G(a_i)\rVert^2 .
      \label{eq:discover-physics}
    \end{equation}%
\end{itemize}
This part is about the third task, in the form it takes for dynamical systems.
It is not about the classical inverse problem of the second item, which is why
the part is titled \enquote{learning dynamics} rather than \enquote{inverse
problems}.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Data-driven models are worth building after three parts of PDE solvers because
measurements vastly outnumber models: most real systems are far easier to
observe than to write down. Deep learning is the obvious candidate, but applied
naively to scientific tasks it tends to give models that cannot be interpreted,
generalize poorly, and need large amounts of training data.

\paragraph{Problem statement.}
We are given measurement data as a time series,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D = \{\mathbf u_k\}_{k=0}^{K}, \qquad
  \mathbf u_k = \mathbf u(t_k) \in \mathbb R^{N_x}, \qquad
  t_k = k\,\Delta t ,
  \label{eq:time-series}
\end{equation}%
where $N_x$ is the number of spatial grid points. The continuous dynamics
$\mathrm d\mathbf u/\mathrm dt = \mathbf f(\mathbf u)$ become a discrete map
$\mathbf u_{k+1} = \mathbf F(\mathbf u_k)$, and \eqref{eq:discover-physics}
specializes to
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf F} = \arg\min_{\mathbf G\in\mathcal M}
  \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \bigl\lVert \mathbf u_{k+1} - \mathbf G(\mathbf u_k)\bigr\rVert_2^2 .
  \label{eq:inverse-ls}
\end{equation}%
The fitted map can be used to forecast future states, and, if it is
interpretable, to explain them.

\paragraph{The spectrum of methods.}
Data-driven methods line up between two extremes:
\begin{center}
  \small
  ML \& NNs $\;\to\;$ PINNs \& FNOs $\;\to\;$ DMD (Koopman)
  $\;\to\;$ SINDy $\;\to\;$ solving PDEs \\[2pt]
  \emph{fully data-driven} \hfill \emph{fully physics-driven}
\end{center}
Neural networks~\cite{Raissi2019,Lu2021,Li2021} sit at the data end; they can
forecast well and still return nothing that can be read as a model. The solvers
of Part~\partref{3} sit at the physics end. DMD and SINDy sit in between: they
learn from data, but what they return is a model you can read --- modes and
eigenvalues for DMD, a sparse equation for SINDy. They are the two methods of
this part.

\paragraph{The same curse, again.}
Both methods run into the wall of \cref{subsec:numerical-methods-intro} in a new
form. A snapshot matrix is $N_x \times K$ with $N_x = 2^n$, so even its SVD is
prohibitive: it takes $\mathcal O(N_x K \min(N_x,K))$ work and
$\mathcal O(N_x K)$ memory. SINDy's dictionary of candidate functions grows
exponentially with the state dimension (\cref{subsec:sindy-mandy}). Data also
brings problems that equations do not: measurements are sparse and noisy, time
series are short and may be non-stationary, and there is no guarantee that a
closed model exists at all. The tensor-network response is the one of
Parts~\partref{1}--\partref{3}: compress both space and time, or every state
dimension, carry out the linear algebra inside the compressed format, and keep
the output interpretable.

\subsection{Dynamic Mode Decomposition}
\label{subsec:dmd}
% Keywords: DMD algorithm, snapshot matrices, regression A = X' X^+,
% diagnostics and future-state prediction, Koopman operator viewpoint.

\paragraph{The idea.}
Assume the dynamics are approximately linear in the state,
$\mathbf u_{k+1} \approx A\,\mathbf u_k$, find the best such $A$ from the data
--- \eqref{eq:inverse-ls} with $\mathcal M$ the linear maps --- and read off its
eigenpairs $A\boldsymbol\phi_j = \lambda_j\boldsymbol\phi_j$~\cite{Schmid2010,Kutz2016}.
Each eigenvalue gives a \textbf{growth rate and a frequency}; each eigenvector
gives a \textbf{spatial mode}. The output is therefore a set of individually
interpretable physical structures, and the same linear model also forecasts
future states.

\paragraph{The standard recipe.}
Collect the snapshots into two shifted matrices and regress:
\begin{equation}
  X  = [\mathbf u_0, \dots, \mathbf u_{K-1}], \qquad
  X' = [\mathbf u_1, \dots, \mathbf u_{K}], \qquad
  X' = AX \;\Longrightarrow\; A = X'X^{+} .
  \label{eq:dmd-regression}
\end{equation}%
$A$ is $N_x\times N_x$ and is never formed. With the SVD $X = U\Sigma V^\dagger$
truncated to its leading $\chi$ singular values,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{A} = U^{\dagger} X' V \Sigma^{-1}, \qquad
  \tilde{A} W = W\Lambda, \qquad
  \Phi = UW .
  \label{eq:dmd-recipe}
\end{equation}%
The matrix $\tilde{A}$ is the projection of $A$ onto the leading $\chi$ left
singular vectors, so it is $\chi \times \chi$. The DMD modes are the columns of
$\Phi$, and the cost is dominated by the SVD.

\begin{remark}[Koopman view]
  Equation~\eqref{eq:dmd-recipe} is a finite-rank approximation of the linear,
  infinite-dimensional Koopman operator acting on
  observables~\cite{Mezic2005,Rowley2009,Tu2014}. The nonlinear dynamics are
  linear in the space of observables, but that space is infinite-dimensional;
  DMD truncates it to $\chi$ dimensions. That is why a linear method can
  describe nonlinear dynamics without contradiction, and also why it fails when
  $\chi$ modes do not span the relevant observables~\cite{Williams2015,Colbrook2024}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Data as a Space--Time Tensor Train}
\label{subsec:space-time-data}
% Keywords: space-time tensorization, binary encoding of x and t, loading
% measured snapshots, bit ordering, temporal bond dimension.

\paragraph{Discretization.}
Binarize \textbf{both} coordinates~\cite{Peddinti2025a}, exactly as in
Part~\partref{1}:
\begin{equation}
  x = \sum_{l=1}^{n} i_l\, 2^{-l}, \qquad
  t = T \sum_{l=1}^{n_t} j_l\, 2^{-l}, \qquad
  i_l, j_l \in \{0,1\},
  \label{eq:space-time-bits}
\end{equation}%
so that $f(x,t) \longleftrightarrow f(i_1,\dots,i_n,j_1,\dots,j_{n_t})$, with
$N_x = 2^n$ spatial grid points and $K = 2^{n_t}$ time samples.

\paragraph{Encoding.}
Put all the bits on one chain, space first and then time:
\begin{equation}
  f(x,t) \;\to\; A_1^{(i_1)} A_2^{(i_2)} \cdots A_n^{(i_n)}\,
  A_{n+1}^{(j_1)} A_{n+2}^{(j_2)} \cdots A_{n+n_t}^{(j_{n_t})} .
  \label{eq:space-time-mps}
\end{equation}%
Time stops being a loop and becomes $n_t = \log_2 K$ more legs on the same
chain. The whole data set is held in $\mathcal O((n+n_t)\chi^2)$ numbers rather
than $N_x K$.

\paragraph{Loading measured snapshots.}
Data arrives as snapshots, not as a function that can be queried, so the main
route into the format is sequential. Compress each snapshot $\mathbf u_k$ into
an $n$-site train (\cref{subsec:mps}), stack the snapshot trains one after
another along the time legs, and truncate after each addition. This one-off
construction is linear in the number of time steps $K$. Everything after it ---
the decomposition, the projection, the forecast --- works on the compressed
train. When the data come from a space--time solve instead, the train is
produced directly and this step disappears (\cref{subsec:dmd-space-time}).

\paragraph{Bit ordering.}
Ordering matters here as it did for several spatial coordinates in
\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}. Space-then-time is the general choice.
For the one-dimensional nonlinear Schr\"odinger (NLS) equation,
$i\,\partial_t\psi = -\partial_x^2\psi + g\,\lvert\psi\rvert^2\psi$ for a
complex field $\psi(x,t)$ with coupling $g$, one of the test cases
of~\cite{Peddinti2025a}, the time-then-space ordering converged better in the
space--time solve and gave smoother solutions.

\paragraph{The temporal bond as a diagnostic.}
The \textbf{temporal} bond dimension, the rank across the cut separating space
legs from time legs, is a direct measure of temporal complexity. A standing wave
$f(x)g(t)$ has rank $1$ across that cut, a travelling sine wave rank $2$, and
white noise saturates the bond. Smooth dynamics means small $\chi$ across the
cut.

\subsection{MPS-DMD}
\label{subsec:mps-dmd}
% Keywords: snapshot matrices in MPS form, POD from the mixed-canonical
% gauge, projection, eigendecomposition, prediction, complexity, prior
% tensor-based DMD, results, noise vs truncation.

Standard DMD needs an SVD of an $N_x \times K$ matrix. Once the data is a
space--time train, that SVD is unnecessary: \textbf{MPS-DMD}~\cite{Peddinti2025a}
runs the entire recipe \eqref{eq:dmd-regression}--\eqref{eq:dmd-recipe}
\emph{inside} the train and never forms a snapshot matrix. In pipeline terms,
the encoding stage is the space--time train of \cref{subsec:space-time-data},
the four steps below replace the dense linear algebra, step~4 is the time
evolution, and the modes and eigenvalues are what is retrieved.

\paragraph{Snapshot matrices.}
The space--time train \emph{is} the matrix $X$ of \eqref{eq:dmd-regression},
with row index $i = (i_1,\dots,i_n)$ and column index
$j = (j_1,\dots,j_{n_t})$. The shifted matrix $X'$ is obtained by a rank-2
shift MPO on the time legs. The goal is $A$ with $X' = AX$.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) of $X$.} Gauge into mixed
    canonical form at the bond separating the space legs from the time legs
    (\cref{subsec:mps}). The Schmidt decomposition across that bond \emph{is}
    \begin{equation}
      X = U\Sigma V^{\dagger}
      \;\Longrightarrow\;
      A = X'X^{+} = X'V\Sigma^{-1}U^{\dagger},
      \label{eq:mps-pod}
    \end{equation}%
    so the SVD comes for free: $U$ is the left-orthogonal space half of the
    chain, $\Sigma$ the bond singular values, $V$ the right-orthogonal time half.
  \item \textbf{Projection of $A$ onto the span of $U$:}
    \begin{equation}
      \tilde{A} := U^{\dagger} A U = U^{\dagger} X' V \Sigma^{-1},
      \label{eq:mps-project}
    \end{equation}%
    a $\chi \times \chi$ matrix obtained by contracting $U^\dagger$, the shifted
    train $X'$ and $V$. The bond dimension $\chi$ plays the role of the
    truncation rank in~\eqref{eq:dmd-recipe}.
  \item \textbf{Eigendecomposition:}
    $\tilde{A}W = W\Lambda \;\Longrightarrow\; A\,UW = UW\,\Lambda$, so the
    modes $\Phi = UW$ stay in train form.
  \item \textbf{Prediction} by local contractions alone,
    \begin{equation}
      \mathbf u_{K+k} = A^k \mathbf u_K, \qquad
      A^k = U W \Lambda^k W^{-1} U^{\dagger} .
      \label{eq:mps-dmd-predict}
    \end{equation}%
\end{enumerate}
Since $\Lambda$ is diagonal, raising it to the power $k$ takes $\chi$ scalar
powers, so forecasting $1000$ steps ahead costs the same as forecasting one.

\begin{remark}[The last snapshot]
  The train holds $K$ snapshots $\mathbf u_0,\dots,\mathbf u_{K-1}$, and the
  shift MPO is not periodic: it maps them to
  $[\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_{K-1},0]$. Split the right factor by columns,
  $V^\dagger = [P \;\; v]$, so that the $\chi\times(K-1)$ block $P$ holds the
  time coefficients of $\mathbf u_0,\dots,\mathbf u_{K-2}$ and the column $v$
  those of $\mathbf u_{K-1}$. The pairs DMD should fit are
  $X_0 = U\Sigma P$ and $X_1 = [\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_{K-1}]$, and least
  squares gives $A = X_1X_0^{+} = X_1P^{+}\Sigma^{-1}U^\dagger$ with
  $P^{+} = P^\dagger(PP^\dagger)^{-1}$. The raw contraction of step~2 uses
  $P^\dagger$ in place of $P^{+}$. Because $V^\dagger$ has orthonormal rows,
  $PP^\dagger = I - vv^\dagger$, and the Sherman--Morrison formula
  $(I - vv^\dagger)^{-1} = I + vv^\dagger/(1 - v^\dagger v)$ turns the
  correction into one rank-one update of the $\chi\times\chi$ matrix
  $\tilde A$, which recovers exact DMD. Skipping it damps every eigenvalue by
  roughly $1/K$ per step: invisible in the spectrum, ruinous after a few hundred
  steps of prediction.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Prior tensor-based DMD.}
Tensor formats have been used for DMD before. Klus \emph{et
al.}~\cite{Klus2018} introduced tensor-based DMD using spatial compression,
leaving the temporal dimension uncompressed, so the cost stays linear in $K$.
\'Sroda \emph{et al.}~\cite{Sroda2025}, in independent work on tensor-train
nonequilibrium Green's functions, use DMD as a predictor with the same
complexity. Compressing time as well changes the scaling:
\begin{equation}
  \text{DMD: } \mathcal O\bigl(N_x K \min(N_x, K)\bigr), \qquad
  \text{MPS-DMD: } \mathcal O\bigl((n+n_t)\,\chi^3\bigr), \quad
  N_x = 2^n,\; K = 2^{n_t} ,
  \label{eq:mps-dmd-cost}
\end{equation}%
which is logarithmic in both $N_x$ and $K$. The one-off loading of measured
snapshots (\cref{subsec:space-time-data}) is not included in this count.

\paragraph{Results.}
On the K\'arm\'an vortex street behind a cylinder~\cite{Peddinti2025a}, MPS-DMD
trained on the first $\sim\!1000$ steps reproduces the training data and
continues the oscillation of the $x$-velocity at a probe point into the
prediction window. The prediction error falls with $\chi$ at essentially the
same rate as for exact DMD, and in the reported benchmark the wall-clock time of
MPS-DMD stays several times below that of exact DMD at every bond dimension from
$\chi = 10$ to $100$.
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.62\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Case} & \textsc{Setup and outcome} \\
    \midrule
    Burgers
      & $\chi = 10$ on $1024^2$; accurate $1000$ steps ahead, at $99\%$
        compression \\
    2D cylinder wake
      & $256\times128\times1024$ as a 25-bit MPS at $\chi = 200$; $97.42\%$
        compression, with 100 modes capturing the vortex street \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Noise and truncation.}
The truncation that makes MPS-DMD affordable is also a regularizer: discarding
small singular values discards low-amplitude structure, some of which is noise.
Whether it denoises the data or faithfully reproduces the noise depends on where
the noise sits in the singular-value spectrum. Broadband noise spreads across
all singular values and is partly removed; structured or low-rank noise is
retained exactly. Cross interpolation, likewise, fits noise faithfully if it is
allowed to (\cref{subsec:finding-qtt}). There is at present no theory that
predicts which of the two outcomes a given data set will produce, so the
interaction of truncation with measurement noise remains an open problem.

\subsection{SINDy and MANDy}
\label{subsec:sindy-mandy}
% Keywords: sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics, candidate
% dictionaries, curse of dimensionality, tensor form, MANDy, TT-based
% learning of dynamical laws, TT-WSINDy, applications.

DMD recovers an operator. The more ambitious task is to recover the
\emph{equation}, and the difference matters: an operator is tied to the regime
in which it was fitted, whereas an equation is not.

\paragraph{SINDy.}
Sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics~\cite{Brunton2016} starts from
$\dot{\mathbf u}(t) = \mathbf f(\mathbf u(t))$ for a state
$\mathbf u\in\mathbb R^p$; we write $p$ for the state dimension to keep $N$ for
grid sizes. It stacks the state and its time derivative at $K$ sample times,
one row per sample:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf U = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf u^T(t_1) \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf u^T(t_K) \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \dot{\mathbf U} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{\mathbf u}^T(t_1) \\ \vdots \\ \dot{\mathbf u}^T(t_K) \end{bmatrix}.
  \label{eq:sindy-data}
\end{equation}%
A library $\Theta(\mathbf U)$ evaluates candidate functions (constant, $u_i$,
$u_iu_j$, $\sin u_i$, \dots) column by column, and the recovered dynamics are
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathbf U} = \Theta(\mathbf U)\,\Xi ,
  \label{eq:sindy}
\end{equation}%
with $\Xi$ found by sparse regression (sequentially thresholded least squares).
The nonzero entries of $\Xi$ \emph{are} the equation. The standard test is a
trajectory of the Lorenz system, $p = 3$,
\begin{equation*}
  \dot u_1 = 10\,(u_2 - u_1), \qquad
  \dot u_2 = u_1(28 - u_3) - u_2, \qquad
  \dot u_3 = u_1u_2 - \tfrac{8}{3}\,u_3 ,
\end{equation*}
a chaotic three-variable model of convection. From samples of $\mathbf u$ and
$\dot{\mathbf u}$ along that trajectory and a polynomial library, SINDy returns
these three equations and nothing else~\cite{Brunton2016}.

\paragraph{The curse of dimensionality in SINDy.}
From here on we use the transposed convention: data
$\mathbf U\in\mathbb R^{p\times K}$, $\mathbf U[i,k] \approx u_i(t_k)$, with $K$
the number of time samples and $p$ the state dimension. Choose $J$ univariate
functions $\psi_1,\dots,\psi_J:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ and take all their
products as candidates,
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{\mathbf j}(\mathbf u) = \prod_{i=1}^{p} \psi_{j_i}(u_i), \qquad
  \Theta[\mathbf j, k] = \phi_{\mathbf j}(\mathbf U[:,k]),
  \label{eq:product-dictionary}
\end{equation}%
and solve
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\Xi}\; \bigl\lVert \dot{\mathbf U} - \Xi\,\Theta_\flat(\mathbf U) \bigr\rVert_F^2
  + \mathcal R(\Xi),
  \label{eq:sindy-regularized}
\end{equation}%
where $\Theta_\flat$ is $\Theta$ with its $\mathbf j$ indices flattened. Such a
library needs no guessing about which cross terms matter, but the dictionary
$\Theta$ has size $J^p\times K$ and the coefficients $\Xi$ size $p\times J^p$:
one column per candidate, exponential in $p$. In matrix form,
$\Theta_\flat = \Psi_1 \odot \cdots \odot \Psi_p$ is a Khatri--Rao product
with $\Psi_i[j,k] = \psi_j(\mathbf U[i,k])$.

Drawn as tensors, $\dot{\mathbf U}$ (legs $i$, $k$) is approximated by
$\Xi\in\mathbb R^{J\times\cdots\times J\times p}$ contracted with
$\Theta(\mathbf U)\in\mathbb R^{J\times\cdots\times J\times K}$ over $p$ legs
$j_1,\dots,j_p$ of size $J$: a sum over $J^p$ terms. This is the same
exponential wall as a dense $2^n$ vector, and it invites the same response.

\paragraph{MANDy.}
Multidimensional approximation of nonlinear dynamics (MANDy; Gel\ss{} \emph{et
al.}~\cite{Gelss2019}) rests on the observation that the Khatri--Rao structure
makes $\Theta(\mathbf U)$ an \textbf{exact tensor train}: its cores are the
small matrices $\Psi_1,\dots,\Psi_p$, connected by bonds of size $K$ that carry
the (known) sample index. Orthonormalizing that train gives
$\Theta_\flat = U\Sigma V^\top$ with $U = U_1\cdots U_p$ in train form, and the
unknown, unstructured $\Xi$ is then solved in closed form,
\begin{equation}
  \Xi = \dot{\mathbf U}\,\Theta_\flat^{+}
      = \bigl(\dot{\mathbf U} V \Sigma^{-1}\bigr)\, U^\top ,
  \label{eq:mandy}
\end{equation}%
itself a tensor train with cores $U_1,\dots,U_p$ and a final $p\times\chi$ core
$\dot{\mathbf U}V\Sigma^{-1}$, where $\chi$ is the rank of $\Theta_\flat$. No
$J^p$ object is formed at any stage; the bonds are at most $\min(J^l, K)$ after
$l$ cores. The price is that \eqref{eq:mandy} is the minimum-norm least-squares
solution, not a sparse one.

\paragraph{Further tensor-train methods.}
Goe\ss{}mann \emph{et al.}~\cite{Goessmann2020} represent $\Xi$ itself as a
tensor train with a selection tensor that routes each output to its relevant
cores, and learn it by alternating least squares, the sweeping machinery of
\cref{subsec:linear-solvers} used as a regression; TT-WSINDy~\cite{Houser2026}
performs the weak-form transformation, regression and sparsification of
weak-form SINDy in TT format, so that its cost grows polynomially rather than
exponentially in the state dimension as long as the TT ranks stay small. As an
application, van't Hoff \emph{et al.}~\cite{vantHoff2026} isolate the
atmospheric perturbation due to high-altitude aviation emissions by subtracting
chemistry simulations with and without them, and discover reduced-order models
of ozone columns from it by POD, SINDy and DMD.

\subsection{A Closing Remark: The Same Structure Can Produce the Data}
\label{subsec:dmd-space-time}
% Keywords: time-stepping vs. space-time methods, all-at-once solve,
% space-time solver results. Demoted from the middle of the argument.

The space--time train of \cref{subsec:space-time-data} need not come from
measurements. Consider
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u + F(u, \partial_x u, \dots, \partial_x^q u) = g(x,t) .
  \label{eq:generic-evolution}
\end{equation}%
\emph{Time-stepping}, $\mathbf u_{k+1} = \mathbf u_k + \Delta t\,
\mathbb F(\mathbf u_k)$, keeps one spatial train on $n$ sites and updates it
$K$ times; this is the Part~\partref{3} solver. A \emph{space--time method}
instead assembles the whole history as one linear system on the $n+n_t$
space--time sites,
\begin{equation}
  (\mathbb I_x \otimes \mathbb D_t + \mathbb F_x \otimes \mathbb I_t)\,
  \mathbf u = \text{b.c.} + \text{i.c.},
  \label{eq:space-time-system}
\end{equation}%
with $\mathbb D_t$ a time-difference MPO and $\mathbb F_x$ the spatial
operator, and solves it \textbf{once} with a sweeping solver
(\cref{subsec:linear-solvers}). Without a time loop there is no step-to-step
amplification, and hence no stability restriction on $\Delta t$ of the explicit
kind. The time resolution is still set by accuracy, through $n_t$, and the
cost reappears as the space--time bond dimension, which grows for sharp initial
data (Exercise~2 below). A nonlinear $F$ needs an outer Newton or Picard
iteration, with the questions Part~\partref{2} raised about it.

On a $1024 \times 1024$ space--time grid~\cite{Peddinti2025a}:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llp{0.38\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Equation} & $\chi$ & \textsc{Compression} \\
    \midrule
    Heat, wave                    & $2$--$22$ & $> 99\%$ \\
    Burgers, nonlinear diffusion  & $10$      & $> 99\%$, residual
                                                $\sim\!10^{-3}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
The space--time train that such a solve returns is exactly the input MPS-DMD
needs: the same data structure serves as solver output and as input to the
data-driven model, with no conversion in between.

\subsection{Outlook}
\label{subsec:part4-outlook}
% Keywords: open challenges in data-driven TN methods, hybrid physics+data
% approaches, uncertainty quantification.

The open problems specific to learning dynamics in tensor-train format are the
following; the interaction of truncation and noise is discussed in
\cref{subsec:mps-dmd}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Beyond $(1+1)$D.} The space--time analysis so far is
    one-dimensional in space. Two and three dimensions plus time, and the index
    ordering that goes with them (\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}), are
    open.
  \item \textbf{Nonlinear and Koopman extensions.} Extended DMD with
    tensor-train dictionaries~\cite{Williams2015,Klus2018}, and kernel DMD in
    compressed form.
  \item \textbf{Sparsity in TT format.} MANDy's closed form is not sparse;
    combining TT dictionaries with sparse or weak-form regression is active
    work~\cite{Goessmann2020,Houser2026}.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid physics and data.} Use the Part~\partref{3} solver as a
    prior and learn only the closure or the residual in TT form. Data
    assimilation is the same idea with observations in place of a learned
    term: a known but imperfect model, the Part~\partref{3} solver, is combined
    with sparse observations, and the tensor network carries the state.
  \item \textbf{Uncertainty quantification.} Error bars on tensor-network
    predictions are essentially absent today, which compounds the missing
    a-posteriori bounds of \cref{subsec:part2-open-problems}.
  \item \textbf{Software and reproducibility.} Shared TT/MPS
    tooling~\cite{Nunez2025}, standard benchmarks, and open datasets.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Summary.}
The snapshot matrix of DMD and the dictionary of SINDy suffer the same
exponential wall as a dense grid function, and the tensor train removes it in
both. Loaded as a space--time train, data costs $\mathcal O((n+n_t)\chi^2)$
numbers; MPS-DMD reads the POD off the space/time bond and forecasts with cost
logarithmic in both $N_x$ and $K$; MANDy treats the product dictionary as an
exact tensor train and never forms its $J^p$ columns. The bond dimension again
measures complexity, here the temporal complexity of the data, and the
truncation that keeps it small acts as a regularizer whose interaction with
noise is not yet understood. The conclusion that follows collects what the four
parts have shown about $\chi$.

\begin{exercise}[Companion notebook]
  \texttt{04-inverse-problem.ipynb} follows this part section by section, with
  five exercises interleaved:

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Read the temporal complexity of six datasets off the space/time bond,

      noise saturates. Then add $10^{-3}$ of noise to the standing wave and
      measure the bond at tolerance $10^{-10}$ and at $10^{-2}$: it goes from
      saturated back to 1. The bond measures complexity \emph{at the accuracy you
      asked for}, which is the only kind there is.
    \item Assemble the entire space--time system~\eqref{eq:space-time-system}

      no time loop at all. Then swap the initial condition for a narrow Gaussian
      and watch the space/time bond grow. That bond is the price of removing the
      loop.
    \item Break MPS-DMD on purpose --- a standing wave, a frequency sweep, a
      saturating nonlinearity. Note that it fails silently in every case: the
      algorithm returns eigenvalues and modes regardless, and only the in-sample
      reconstruction gives it away.

      noise amplitude. Then answer the question that matters: in a real problem

    \item Implement MANDy~\eqref{eq:mandy} for a Fermi--Pasta--Ulam chain with

  \end{enumerate}
