\section{Part IV: The Inverse Problem --- Data-Driven Models}
\label{sec:part4}
\lecture{01}{10}{2026}

% Follows slides/part4.pdf (QTADE 2026, Part IV), in slide order:
% the inverse problem -> DMD -> space-time MPS encoding and the bonus PDE
% solver -> MPS-DMD -> SINDy, MANDy and more. One subsection per block.

Parts~\partref{1}--\partref{3} built a function representation (QTTs), the
linear algebra on top of it, and spent both on the forward problem: solving the
Navier--Stokes equations. Part~\partref{4} inverts that assumption. What we have
instead are \textbf{snapshots} $\{\mathbf u(t_k)\}$ --- from an experiment, from
DNS output, or from sensors. What we lack is the operator, the closure, and
sometimes the equation itself.

The claim of this part is that the curse of dimensionality that hits PDE solvers
hits data-driven models just as hard --- in the SVD of DMD and in the dictionary
of SINDy --- and that the same compressed format lifts it from both, without
giving up interpretability.

\subsection{The Inverse Problem}
\label{subsec:inverse-problem}
% Keywords: simulation vs. inverse problems vs. discovering physics,
% motivation, problem statement, prediction vs. learning, the spectrum of
% data-driven methods.

\paragraph{A classification of tasks.}
\margintext{The three-way split follows the ETH course \emph{AI in the Sciences
and Engineering}~\cite{Mishra2024}.}
Write a scientific task as
\begin{equation}
  b = F(a),
  \label{eq:task}
\end{equation}%
with $a$ the input conditions, $F$ the physical model (usually a PDE) and $b$
the resulting properties. Which symbol is unknown classifies the task:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulation:} $F$ and $a$ are known, $b$ is wanted. This is what
    Parts~\partref{1}--\partref{3} did.
  \item \textbf{Inverse problems:} $F$ and $b$ are known, $a$ is wanted
    (initial data, parameters, sources).
  \item \textbf{Discovering unknown physics:} $F$ itself is wanted. Given pairs
    $\{(a_i, b_i)\}_{i=1}^{M}$, choose it from a model class $\mathcal M$,
    \begin{equation}
      \hat F = \arg\min_{G\in\mathcal M} \sum_i \lVert b_i - G(a_i)\rVert^2 .
      \label{eq:discover-physics}
    \end{equation}%
\end{itemize}
This part is about the third task, in the form it takes for dynamical systems.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
We just solved PDEs, so why go data-driven? Because measurements vastly
outnumber models: most real systems are observed far more easily than they are
written down. And why not simply AI/ML? Because naively using deep learning for
scientific tasks usually leads to a lack of interpretability, poor
generalization, and a large demand for training data.

\paragraph{Problem statement.}
We are given measurement data as a time series,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D = \{\mathbf u_k\}_{k=0}^{N_t}, \qquad
  \mathbf u_k = \mathbf u(t_k) \in \mathbb R^{N_x}, \qquad
  t_k = k\,\Delta t .
  \label{eq:time-series}
\end{equation}%
The continuous dynamics $\mathrm d\mathbf u/\mathrm dt = \mathbf f(\mathbf u)$
become a discrete map $\mathbf u_{k+1} = \mathbf F(\mathbf u_k)$, and
\eqref{eq:discover-physics} specializes to
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf F} = \arg\min_{\mathbf G\in\mathcal M}
  \sum_{k=0}^{N_t-1} \bigl\lVert \mathbf u_{k+1} - \mathbf G(\mathbf u_k)\bigr\rVert_2^2 .
  \label{eq:inverse-ls}
\end{equation}%
Two tasks follow, and they must be kept separate:
\margintext{Conflating these two is the most common error in the data-driven
literature. A method can be excellent at one and useless at the other.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prediction:} forecast future states,
    $\hat{\mathbf u}_{N_t+k} = \hat{\mathbf F}^{\,k}(\mathbf u_{N_t})$. An
    interpretable model is not required.
  \item \textbf{Learning:} characterize the spectrum. For a linear model
    $\hat{\mathbf F}(\mathbf u) = A\mathbf u$ this means the eigenpairs
    $A\boldsymbol\phi_j = \lambda_j\boldsymbol\phi_j$ --- and an interpretable
    model is the entire point.
\end{itemize}
Neural surrogates are the clean illustration of the split: they can predict
well and explain nothing.

\paragraph{The spectrum of methods.}
Data-driven methods line up between two extremes:
\begin{center}
  \small
  ML \& NNs $\;\to\;$ PINNs \& FNOs $\;\to\;$ DMD (Koopman)
  $\;\to\;$ SINDy $\;\to\;$ solving PDEs \\[2pt]
  \emph{fully data-driven} \hfill \emph{fully physics-driven}
\end{center}
Neural networks~\cite{Raissi2019,Lu2021,Li2021} sit at the data end, the
solvers of Part~\partref{3} at the physics end. DMD and SINDy sit in between:
they learn from data, but what they return is a model you can read --- modes and
eigenvalues for DMD, a sparse equation for SINDy. They are the two methods of
this part.

\paragraph{The same curse, again.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Snapshot matrices are $N_x \times N_t$ with $N_x = 2^n$, so even the SVD
    is prohibitive: $\mathcal O(N_x N_t \min(N_x,N_t))$ work and
    $\mathcal O(N_x N_t)$ memory. This is the wall of
    \cref{subsec:numerical-methods-intro} in a new costume.
  \item SINDy's dictionary of candidate functions grows exponentially with the
    state dimension (\cref{subsec:sindy-mandy}).
  \item The data brings its own problems on top: sparse and noisy measurements,
    short time series, non-stationarity, and no guarantee that a closed model
    exists at all.
  \item \textbf{The tensor-network promise:} compress \emph{both} space and
    time (or every state dimension), do the linear algebra inside the compressed
    format, and keep the modes interpretable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dynamic Mode Decomposition}
\label{subsec:dmd}
% Keywords: DMD algorithm, snapshot matrices, regression A = X' X^+,
% diagnostics and future-state prediction, Koopman operator viewpoint.

\paragraph{DMD in one line.}
Assume the dynamics are approximately linear in the state,
$\mathbf u_{k+1} \approx A\,\mathbf u_k$, find the best such $A$ from the data
--- \eqref{eq:inverse-ls} with $\mathcal M$ the linear maps --- and read off its
eigenpairs~\cite{Schmid2010,Kutz2016}. Each eigenvalue gives a \textbf{growth
rate and a frequency}; each eigenvector gives a \textbf{spatial mode}. That is
the whole appeal: the output is a set of individually interpretable physical
structures (diagnostics), and the same model forecasts (future-state
prediction).

\paragraph{The standard recipe.}
Collect the snapshots into two shifted matrices and regress:
\begin{equation}
  X  = [\mathbf u_0, \dots, \mathbf u_{N_t-1}], \qquad
  X' = [\mathbf u_1, \dots, \mathbf u_{N_t}], \qquad
  X' = AX \;\Longrightarrow\; A = X'X^{+} .
  \label{eq:dmd-regression}
\end{equation}%
$A$ is $N_x\times N_x$ and is never formed. With the SVD $X = U\Sigma V^\dagger$
truncated to rank $r$,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{A} = U^{\dagger} X' V \Sigma^{-1}, \qquad
  \tilde{A} W = W\Lambda, \qquad
  \Phi = UW .
  \label{eq:dmd-recipe}
\end{equation}%
The matrix $\tilde{A}$ is the projection of $A$ onto the leading $r$ left
singular vectors, so it is $r \times r$. The DMD modes are the columns of
$\Phi$, and the cost is dominated by the SVD.

\begin{remark}[Koopman view]
  Equation~\eqref{eq:dmd-recipe} is a finite-rank approximation of the linear,
  infinite-dimensional Koopman operator acting on
  observables~\cite{Mezic2005,Rowley2009,Tu2014}. The nonlinear dynamics are
  linear in the space of observables, but that space is infinite-dimensional;
  DMD truncates it to $r$ dimensions. That is why a linear method can describe
  nonlinear dynamics without contradiction, and also why it fails when $r$
  modes do not span the relevant observables~\cite{Williams2015,Colbrook2024}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Space-Time MPS Encoding and a Bonus PDE Solver}
\label{subsec:dmd-space-time}
% Keywords: space-time tensorization, binary encoding of x and t, temporal
% bond dimension, time-stepping vs. space-time methods, all-at-once solve,
% Newton with space-time Jacobian.

\paragraph{\#1. Discretization.}
Binarize \textbf{both} coordinates~\cite{Peddinti2025a}, exactly as in
Part~\partref{1}:
\begin{equation}
  x = \sum_{k=1}^{n} i_k\, 2^{-k}, \qquad
  t = T \sum_{l=1}^{m} j_l\, 2^{-l}, \qquad
  i_k, j_l \in \{0,1\},
  \label{eq:space-time-bits}
\end{equation}%
so that $f(x,t) \longleftrightarrow f(i_1,\dots,i_n,j_1,\dots,j_m)$, with
$N_x = 2^n$ and $N_t = 2^m$.

\paragraph{\#2. MPS encoding.}
Put all the bits on one chain, space first and then time:
\begin{equation}
  f(x,t) \;\to\; A_1^{(i_1)} A_2^{(i_2)} \cdots A_n^{(i_n)}\,
  A_{n+1}^{(j_1)} A_{n+2}^{(j_2)} \cdots A_{n+m}^{(j_m)} .
  \label{eq:space-time-mps}
\end{equation}%
\margintext{The conceptual move is small and the consequence is large: once time
is just more legs, there is no time loop to parallelize and no step-to-step
error accumulation along it.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Time stops being a loop and becomes $m = \log_2 N_t$ more legs on the
    same chain.
  \item \textbf{Ordering matters here too.} Space-then-time works generally;
    time-then-space converged better for the nonlinear Schr\"odinger case,
    giving smoother vectorized solutions.
  \item The \textbf{temporal} bond dimension --- the rank across the cut
    separating space legs from time legs --- is a direct measure of temporal
    complexity. A standing wave $f(x)g(t)$ has rank $1$ across that cut, a
    travelling wave rank $2$, white noise saturates. Smooth dynamics means small
    $\chi$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A bonus PDE solver.}
Consider
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u + F(u, \partial_x u, \dots, \partial_x^q u) = g(x,t) .
  \label{eq:generic-evolution}
\end{equation}%
There are two ways to use the chain.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Time-stepping:} $\mathbf u_{t+1} = \mathbf u_t +
    \mathbb F(\mathbf u_t)\,\Delta t$. One spatial MPS on $n$ sites, updated $N_t$
    times; this is the Part~\partref{3} solver.
  \item \textbf{Space-time methods:} assemble the whole history as one linear
    system on the $n+m$ space-time sites,
    \begin{equation}
      (\mathbb I_x \otimes \mathbb D_t + \mathbb F_x \otimes \mathbb I_t)\,
      \mathbf u = \text{b.c.} + \text{i.c.},
      \label{eq:space-time-system}
    \end{equation}%
    with $\mathbb D_t$ a time-difference MPO and $\mathbb F_x$ the spatial
    operator, and solve it \textbf{once} with DMRG (\cref{subsec:linear-solvers}).
    There is no time-stepping loop at all, so the scheme is not subject to a
    step-size stability condition.
  \item A nonlinear $F$ requires Newton iterations with the space--time
    Jacobian, which converge quadratically; Picard iteration is cheaper per step
    but converges only linearly.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
On a $1024 \times 1024$ space--time grid~\cite{Peddinti2025a}:

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llp{0.38\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Equation} & $\chi$ & \textsc{Compression} \\
    \midrule
    Heat, wave                    & $2$--$22$ & $> 99\%$ \\
    Burgers, nonlinear diffusion  & $10$      & $> 99\%$, residual
                                                $\sim\!10^{-3}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The space--time MPS that such a solve returns is exactly the input that MPS-DMD
needs. That is the "bonus": the same data structure serves as solver output and
as data-driven-model input, with no conversion layer in between.

\subsection{MPS-DMD}
\label{subsec:mps-dmd}
% Keywords: snapshot matrices in MPS form, POD from the mixed-canonical
% gauge, projection, eigendecomposition, prediction, complexity, prior
% tensor-based DMD, results.

Standard DMD needs an SVD of an $N_x \times N_t$ matrix. Once the data is a
space--time MPS, that SVD is unnecessary: \textbf{MPS-DMD}~\cite{Peddinti2025a}
runs the entire pipeline \eqref{eq:dmd-regression}--\eqref{eq:dmd-recipe}
\emph{inside} the train, never forming a snapshot matrix.

\paragraph{Snapshot matrices.}
The space--time MPS \emph{is} the matrix $X = [\mathbf u_0, \dots,
\mathbf u_{N_t-1}]$ with row index $i = (i_1,\dots,i_n)$ and column index
$j = (j_1,\dots,j_m)$. The shifted matrix $X' = [\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_{N_t}]$
is obtained by a rank-2 shift MPO on the time legs. The goal is $A$ with
$X' = AX$.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) of $X$.} Gauge into mixed
    canonical form at the bond separating the space legs from the time legs
    (\cref{subsec:mps}). The Schmidt decomposition across that bond \emph{is}
    \begin{equation}
      X = U\Sigma V^{\dagger}
      \;\Longrightarrow\;
      A = X'X^{+} = X'V\Sigma^{-1}U^{\dagger},
      \label{eq:mps-pod}
    \end{equation}%
    so the SVD comes for free: $U$ is the left-orthogonal space half of the
    chain, $\Sigma$ the bond singular values, $V$ the right-orthogonal time half.
  \item \textbf{Project $A$ onto the span of $U$:}
    \begin{equation}
      \tilde{A} := U^{\dagger} A U = U^{\dagger} X' V \Sigma^{-1},
      \label{eq:mps-project}
    \end{equation}%
    a $\chi \times \chi$ matrix obtained by contracting $U^\dagger$, the shifted
    train $X'$ and $V$. Here $\chi$ plays the role of the truncation rank $r$
    in~\eqref{eq:dmd-recipe}.
  \item \textbf{Eigendecomposition:}
    $\tilde{A}W = W\Lambda \;\Longrightarrow\; A\,UW = UW\,\Lambda$, so the
    modes $\Phi = UW$ stay in train form.
  \item \textbf{Prediction} by local contractions alone,
    \begin{equation}
      \mathbf u_{T+k} = A^k \mathbf u_T, \qquad
      A^k = U W \Lambda^k W^{-1} U^{\dagger} .
      \label{eq:mps-dmd-predict}
    \end{equation}%
\end{enumerate}

Raising $\Lambda$ to the power $k$ costs nothing, so forecasting $1000$ steps
ahead costs the same as forecasting one.

\begin{remark}[The last snapshot]
  The non-periodic time shift sends the final snapshot to zero, so the raw
  contraction in step~2 gives $QP^\top$ rather than the least-squares $QP^{+}$
  that DMD wants. Writing $v$ for the last column of $V^\dagger$,
  $PP^\top = I - vv^\top$, and one Sherman--Morrison rank-one correction recovers
  exact DMD. Skipping it damps every eigenvalue by roughly $1/N_t$ per step ---
  invisible in the spectrum, ruinous after a few hundred steps of prediction.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{TTs for DMD.}
Tensor formats have been used for DMD before. Klus \emph{et
al.}~\cite{Klus2018} introduced tensor-based DMD using spatial compression,
leaving the temporal dimension uncompressed, so the cost stays linear in $N_t$.
\'Sroda \emph{et al.}~\cite{Sroda2025}, in independent work on tensor-train
nonequilibrium Green's functions, use DMD as a predictor with the same
complexity. Compressing time as well changes the scaling:
\begin{equation}
  \text{DMD: } \mathcal O\bigl(N_x N_t \min(N_x, N_t)\bigr), \qquad
  \text{MPS-DMD: } \mathcal O\bigl((n+m)\,\chi^3\bigr), \quad
  N_x = 2^n,\; N_t = 2^m .
  \label{eq:mps-dmd-cost}
\end{equation}%
For externally supplied data, where no space--time solve produced the MPS, stack
the snapshot MPSs sequentially and truncate after each; that one-off
construction is linear in the number of timesteps.

\paragraph{Results.}
\margintext{The headline is the scaling, not the compression ratio: runtime
logarithmic in \emph{both} $N_x$ and $N_t$, where prior tensor DMD variants are
linear in $N_t$.}
On the K\'arm\'an vortex street behind a cylinder~\cite{Peddinti2025a}, MPS-DMD
trained on the first $\sim\!1000$ steps reproduces the training data and
continues the oscillation of the $x$-velocity at a probe point into the
prediction window. The prediction error falls with rank $r$ at essentially the
same rate as exact DMD, while the wall-clock time stays several times below it
across ranks $r = 10$--$100$.
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.62\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Case} & \textsc{Setup and outcome} \\
    \midrule
    Burgers
      & $\chi = 10$ on $1024^2$; accurate $1000$ steps ahead, at $99\%$
        compression \\
    2D cylinder wake
      & $256\times128\times1024$ as a 25-bit MPS at $\chi = 200$; $97.42\%$
        compression, with 100 modes capturing the vortex street \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{remark}
  The truncation that makes MPS-DMD affordable is also, viewed differently, a
  regularizer: discarding small singular values discards low-amplitude
  structure, some of which is noise. Whether it denoises or faithfully
  reproduces the noise depends on where the noise sits in the singular-value
  spectrum. Broadband noise spreads across all singular values and is partly
  removed; structured or low-rank noise is retained exactly. Recall from
  \cref{subsec:finding-qtt} that cross interpolation fits noise faithfully if
  you let it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{SINDy, MANDy and More}
\label{subsec:sindy-mandy}
% Keywords: sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics, candidate
% dictionaries, curse of dimensionality, tensor form, MANDy, TT-based
% learning of dynamical laws, TT-WSINDy, applications.

DMD recovers an operator. The more ambitious task is to recover the
\emph{equation} --- and the difference matters, because an operator is tied to
the regime it was fitted in whereas an equation is not.

\paragraph{SINDy.}
Sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics~\cite{Brunton2016} starts from
$\dot{\mathbf x}(t) = \mathbf f(\mathbf x(t))$ and stacks the state and its time
derivative, one row per sample:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf X = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf x^T(t_1) \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf x^T(t_m) \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \dot{\mathbf X} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{\mathbf x}^T(t_1) \\ \vdots \\ \dot{\mathbf x}^T(t_m) \end{bmatrix}.
  \label{eq:sindy-data}
\end{equation}%
A library $\Theta(\mathbf X)$ evaluates candidate functions (constant, $x_i$,
$x_ix_j$, $\sin x_i$, \dots) column by column, and the recovered dynamics are
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathbf X} = \Theta(\mathbf X)\,\Xi ,
  \label{eq:sindy}
\end{equation}%
with $\Xi$ found by sparse regression (sequentially thresholded least squares).
The nonzero entries of $\Xi$ \emph{are} the equation: on Lorenz data, SINDy
returns the three Lorenz equations and nothing else.

\paragraph{SINDy: curse of dimensionality.}
From here on we use the transposed convention: data
$\mathbf X\in\mathbb R^{N\times M}$, $\mathbf X[i,m] \approx x_i(t_m)$, with $M$
the number of time samples and $N$ the state dimension. Choose $J$ univariate
functions $\psi_1,\dots,\psi_J:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ and take all their
products as candidates,
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{\mathbf j}(\mathbf x) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} \psi_{j_i}(x_i), \qquad
  \Theta[\mathbf j, m] = \phi_{\mathbf j}(\mathbf X[:,m]),
  \label{eq:product-dictionary}
\end{equation}%
and solve
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\Xi}\; \bigl\lVert \dot{\mathbf X} - \Xi\,\Theta_\flat(\mathbf X) \bigr\rVert_F^2
  + \mathcal R(\Xi),
  \label{eq:sindy-regularized}
\end{equation}%
where $\Theta_\flat$ is $\Theta$ with its $\mathbf j$ indices flattened. Such a
library needs no guessing about which cross terms matter, but the dictionary
$\Theta$ has size $J^N\times M$ and the coefficients $\Xi$ size $N\times J^N$:
one column per candidate, exponential in $N$. In matrix form,
$\Theta_\flat = \Psi_1 \odot \cdots \odot \Psi_N$ is a Khatri--Rao product
with $\Psi_i[j,m] = \psi_j(\mathbf X[i,m])$.

\paragraph{SINDy: tensor form.}
Drawn as tensors, $\dot{\mathbf X}$ (legs $i$, $m$) is approximated by
$\Xi\in\mathbb R^{J\times\cdots\times J\times N}$ contracted with
$\Theta(\mathbf X)\in\mathbb R^{J\times\cdots\times J\times M}$ over $N$ legs
$j_1,\dots,j_N$ of size $J$: a sum over $J^N$ terms. This is the same
exponential wall as a dense $2^n$ vector, and it invites the same response.

\paragraph{MANDy.}
\margintext{MANDy: multidimensional approximation of nonlinear dynamics.}
Gel\ss{} \emph{et al.}~\cite{Gelss2019} observe that the Khatri--Rao structure
makes $\Theta(\mathbf X)$ an \textbf{exact tensor train}: its cores are the
small matrices $\Psi_1,\dots,\Psi_N$, connected by bonds of size $M$ that carry
the (known) sample index. Orthonormalizing that train gives
$\Theta_\flat = U\Sigma V^\top$ with $U = U_1\cdots U_N$ in train form, and the
unknown, unstructured $\Xi$ is then solved in closed form,
\begin{equation}
  \Xi = \dot{\mathbf X}\,\Theta_\flat^{+}
      = \bigl(\dot{\mathbf X} V \Sigma^{-1}\bigr)\, U^\top ,
  \label{eq:mandy}
\end{equation}%
itself a tensor train with cores $U_1,\dots,U_N$ and a final $N\times r$ core
$\dot{\mathbf X}V\Sigma^{-1}$. No $J^N$ object is formed at any stage; the bonds
are at most $\min(J^k, M)$. The price is that \eqref{eq:mandy} is the
minimum-norm least-squares solution, not a sparse one.

\paragraph{And more.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Learned low-rank coefficients.} Goe\ss{}mann \emph{et
    al.}~\cite{Goessmann2020} represent $\Xi$ itself as a TT with ranks $r$
    plus a selection tensor $S$ that routes each output $i$ to its relevant
    cores, and learn it by alternating least squares --- the sweeping machinery
    of \cref{subsec:linear-solvers}, now as a regression.
  \item \textbf{Weak form in TT.} TT-WSINDy~\cite{Houser2026} combines MANDy
    with weak-form SINDy, performing the weak-form transformation, regression and
    sparsification in TT format, and searches an exponentially large space of
    candidates without the curse of dimensionality.
  \item \textbf{An application.} van't Hoff \emph{et al.}~\cite{vantHoff2026}
    chain the whole toolkit for the atmospheric effects of high-altitude
    (aviation) emissions: simulate atmospheric chemistry with and without
    emissions, subtract to isolate the perturbation, organize it in snapshot
    matrices, discover models by POD, SINDy and DMD, and use them as reduced-order
    models for ozone columns.
  \item \textbf{Data assimilation.} Combine a known but imperfect model with
    sparse observations. The tensor network carries the state, and the model is
    the Part~\partref{3} solver.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[The comparison that matters]
  Against neural-network surrogates the tensor-network case does not rest on raw
  accuracy. It rests on two things: modes, frequencies, growth rates and sparse
  equations come out of the method directly rather than from a post-hoc probe;
  and rank gives a single monitorable complexity knob with a known meaning ---
  the number of retained singular values.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Outlook}
\label{subsec:part4-outlook}
% Keywords: one stone, two birds; open challenges in data-driven TN
% methods, hybrid physics+data approaches, uncertainty quantification.

\paragraph{One stone, two birds.}
PDE solvers and data-driven models both live in the land of dynamical systems,
and the curse of dimensionality hits both: linear systems on the one hand, DMD
and SINDy on the other. Tensor networks restore hope for both at once, through
the same $\mathcal O(\log N)$ compression.

\paragraph{Open problems.}
\margintext{The $(1+1)$D restriction is the most immediate limitation of the
space--time work, and lifting it brings back the index-ordering questions of
\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Beyond $(1+1)$D.} The space--time analysis so far is
    one-dimensional in space. Two and three dimensions plus time --- and the
    index ordering that goes with them --- is open.
  \item \textbf{Nonlinear and Koopman extensions.} Extended DMD with
    tensor-train dictionaries~\cite{Williams2015,Klus2018}, and kernel DMD in
    compressed form.
  \item \textbf{Sparsity in TT format.} MANDy's closed form is not sparse;
    combining TT dictionaries with sparse or weak-form regression is active
    work~\cite{Goessmann2020,Houser2026}.
  \item \textbf{Noise and sparsity of measurements.} How TT truncation interacts
    with measurement noise is not settled; it can denoise, or it can fit the
    noise, and there is no theory saying which.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid physics and data.} Use the Part~\partref{3} solver as a
    prior and learn only the closure or the residual in TT form.
  \item \textbf{Uncertainty quantification.} Error bars on tensor-network
    predictions are essentially absent today, which compounds the missing
    a-posteriori bounds of \cref{subsec:part2-open-problems}.
  \item \textbf{Software and reproducibility.} Shared TT/MPS
    tooling~\cite{Nunez2025}, standard benchmarks, and open datasets.
\end{itemize}

      saturated back to 1. The bond measures complexity \emph{at the accuracy you
      asked for}, which is the only kind there is.
    \item Assemble the entire space--time system~\eqref{eq:space-time-system}
      for the heat equation as one MPO and solve it with a single DMRG call ---
      no time loop at all. Then swap the initial condition for a narrow Gaussian
      and watch the space/time bond grow. That bond is the price of removing the
      loop.
    \item Break MPS-DMD on purpose --- a standing wave, a frequency sweep, a
      saturating nonlinearity. Note that it fails silently in every case: the
      algorithm returns eigenvalues and modes regardless, and only the in-sample
      reconstruction gives it away.
    \item Use truncation as a denoiser and find the optimal rank as a function of
      noise amplitude. Then answer the question that matters: in a real problem
      you have no ground truth to optimize against, so what do you monitor?
    \item Implement MANDy~\eqref{eq:mandy} for a Fermi--Pasta--Ulam chain with
      the product dictionary $\{1, x, x^2, x^3\}^{\otimes N}$, check it against
      dense least squares at $N = 4$, and run $N = 8$ ($4^8$ candidate
      functions) without ever forming the dictionary.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
