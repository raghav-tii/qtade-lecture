\subsection{Why Fluid Dynamics?}
\label{subsec:why-fluid-dynamics}
% Keywords: which PDE to solve, rank as inter-scale correlation, Navier--Stokes,
% Reynolds number, turbulence and the energy cascade, number of scales, DNS cost,
% scale locality, Gourianov et al. (2022).

\paragraph{Which PDE should we solve?}
The question is not trivial, because QTT compresses information in a peculiar
way.
\begin{itemize}
  \item In an MPS of quantum degrees of freedom, rank (bond dimension) measures
    non-factorizability, i.e.\ entanglement; the area law is what makes ground
    states efficient MPSs (\cref{subsec:mps}).
  \item In a QTT the sites are the binary digits of a discretized domain, i.e.\
    \textbf{length scales} (\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}). Rank
    therefore measures \textbf{inter-scale correlations}.
  \item So the right question is: which physics has weak correlations between
    scales? For fluid flows that means talking about turbulence --- scale
    invariance and scale locality --- and turbulent regimes are precisely the
    computational bottleneck of CFD.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The Navier--Stokes equations.}
\label{subsec:navier-stokes}
The incompressible Navier--Stokes equations read
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}
    = -\frac{1}{\varrho}\nabla p + \nu \nabla^2 \mathbf{v},
  \qquad
  \nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0 .
  \label{eq:navier-stokes}
\end{equation}
Each term is a modelling choice:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{advection} $(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}$, the
    nonlinearity;
  \item the \textbf{density} $\varrho$: constant here (incompressible), as
    opposed to compressible flow;
  \item \textbf{dissipation} $\nu\nabla^2\mathbf{v}$: viscous flow, with
    $\nu = 0$ the inviscid Euler limit;
  \item the \textbf{divergence-free condition} $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$, which
    is incompressibility.
\end{itemize}
Nondimensionalize~\eqref{eq:navier-stokes} and exactly one control parameter
survives, the Reynolds number
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Re} \sim \frac{\text{advection}}{\text{dissipation}}
    \sim \frac{UL}{\nu}.
\end{equation}
At small $\mathrm{Re}$ the flow is \textbf{laminar}: flow past a cylinder is
attached, then forms a steady vortex pair, then sheds a K\'arm\'an vortex
street. At $\mathrm{Re} \gg 1$ it is \textbf{turbulent}.

From a tensor-network point of view, each ingredient is awkward in a
\emph{different} way:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{nonlinear advection} is a product of MPSs, so ranks multiply;
  \item \textbf{the pressure constraint} is nonlocal: a global elliptic solve
    at every step;
  \item \textbf{boundary conditions on complex geometry}: curved interfaces
    couple every length scale at once, hence high rank.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent
\Cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks} deals with each in turn.

\paragraph{Turbulence.}
\margintext{Richardson (1922): \enquote{Big whorls have little whorls that feed
on their velocity, and little whorls have lesser whorls and so on to
viscosity.}}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Richardson's cascade.} Energy is injected at the large scale
    $L$, handed down through a hierarchy of eddies, and dissipated to heat at
    the smallest scale.
  \item \textbf{Kolmogorov (1941).} In the inertial range between the
    injection and the dissipative scales the cascade is
    \textbf{self-similar}, and the energy spectrum is
    $E(k) \propto \epsilon^{2/3} k^{-5/3}$~\cite{Kolmogorov1941,Frisch1995}.
    Energy transfer is dominated by \textbf{neighbouring scales}: the coupling is
    strongest between eddies of similar size.
  \item The dissipative (Kolmogorov) scale is $\eta \sim L\,\mathrm{Re}^{-3/4}$,
    so a $D$-dimensional simulation needs
    $(L/\eta)^D \sim \mathrm{Re}^{3D/4}$ grid points to resolve both ends.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
In quantics the relevant count is not the number of points but the number of
binary \textbf{scales} per axis, $M = \log_2(L/\eta) \approx \tfrac34
\log_2\mathrm{Re}$:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccc@{}}
    \toprule
    $\mathrm{Re}$ & $10^4$ & $10^5$ & $10^6$ & $10^7$ & $10^8$ & $10^9$ \\
    \midrule
    \# scales $M$ & $10$ & $12$--$13$ & $15$ & $17$--$18$ & $20$ & $22$--$23$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Direct numerical simulation.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item DNS resolves every scale, so in three dimensions the number of
    \enquote{pixels} grows like $\mathrm{Re}^{9/4}$ --- the estimate quoted in
    \cref{subsec:numerical-methods-intro}.
  \item A typical airfoil simulation sits at
    $\mathrm{Re} \sim 2\times10^6$--$2\times10^7$, i.e.\ $10^{13}$--$10^{16}$
    pixels. Exascale still cannot simulate a full aircraft at flight Reynolds
    number.
  \item The CFD scenario --- flow prediction, shape optimization, forecasting
    --- therefore runs on hands-on expertise: mesh generation, boundary
    conditions, the choice of FD, FEM or FV, the time-evolution scheme, and
    ad-hoc phenomenological \textbf{models} (RANS, LES) with tuned closures and
    limited transfer between regimes.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Low-rank representation for turbulent fields?}
\margintext{Gourianov \emph{et al.} (2022) made this argument first and
quantitatively. It is the observation that turned a plausible analogy into a
research programme.}
The chain of reasoning on the slides is short:
\begin{center}
  QTT tensors represent scales
  $\;\Longrightarrow\;$ scale locality and invariance in turbulence
  $\;\Longrightarrow\;$ low rank?
\end{center}
If the correlations among scales are mostly \enquote{local}, we expect a
low-rank structure, hence a memory cost \textbf{logarithmic in the mesh size},
$\sim N\chi^2$ for $N$ binary sites. Note the contrast with the table above:
$10^{16}$ pixels, but only about twenty scales.

Gourianov \emph{et al.}~\cite{Gourianov2022} first laid this down. They measured
the bond dimension at every bipartition of the QTT of DNS snapshots (temporally
developing jets in 2D, Taylor--Green vortices in 3D), found it bounded and far
below the maximal rank, and ran the incompressible Navier--Stokes equations
directly in MPS form: with $\chi \approx 128$--$192$ the dynamics matched DNS,
while coarser classical grids with the same number of parameters did not.
The same encoding is also what a fault-tolerant quantum CFD algorithm would
consume and return.

\subsection{CFD Framework}
\label{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks}
% Keywords: five-step pipeline, QTT encoding of the flow (stacked/interleaved),
% TT-cross masks of immersed objects, Chorin projection, DMRG pressure solve,
% MPO boundary conditions, coarse-grained evaluation, pixel sampling, complexity.

CFD pipelines are very versatile. Can we replicate one inside the QTT
representation, with better scaling? This is a very active research field
(\cref{subsec:part3-literature}); we follow the framework of Peddinti
\emph{et al.}~\cite{Peddinti2024}, a loop of five steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{flow initialization} --- encode the velocity field as an MPS;
  \item \textbf{mask application} --- approximate MPS masks of arbitrary
    objects;
  \item \textbf{divergence-free projection} and
  \item \textbf{time integration} --- time evolution with open boundary
    conditions and a divergence-free projection, iterated $T$ times (steps 2--4);
  \item \textbf{MPS solution oracle} --- efficient retrieval from the MPS.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Flow initialization: the QTT encoding.}
\margintext{Every design decision here is inherited from Part~\partref{1}: the
encoding and low-rank MPOs from \cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}, rounding
from \cref{subsec:mps}.}
A velocity component is discretized and amplitude encoded,
\begin{equation}
  v(x,y) \xrightarrow{\ \text{discretize}\ } v(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})
  \xrightarrow{\ \text{amplitude encoding}\ }
  v_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}}\,\lvert \mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\rangle,
  \qquad
  v_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}}
    = A_1^{(i_1)} \cdots A_{n_x}^{(i_{n_x})}\,
      B_1^{(j_1)} \cdots B_{n_y}^{(j_{n_y})},
  \label{eq:stacked-encoding}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{i} = (i_1,\dots,i_{n_x})$ and $\mathbf{j}=(j_1,\dots,j_{n_y})$
the binary digits of the grid indices. The first bit $i_1$ selects the left or
right half of the domain, $i_2$ the quarter within it, and so on: on an
$8\times4$ grid the cell $(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}) = (110, 10)$ is the amplitude
of $\lvert 11010\rangle$. Hence the slogan
\begin{center}
  qubits $\Leftrightarrow$ scales, \qquad
  entanglement $\Leftrightarrow$ inter-scale correlations,
\end{center}
and the storage drops from $O(2^N)$ to $O(N\chi^2)$ for $N = n_x + n_y$ sites.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Equation~\eqref{eq:stacked-encoding} is the \textbf{2D stacked} order
    used in~\cite{Peddinti2024}: all $x$ bits, then all $y$ bits.
  \item The \textbf{interleaved} order $i_1 j_1 i_2 j_2\dots$ keeps the $x$ and
    $y$ bits of the same scale adjacent along the chain. The companion code
    uses it by default.
  \item Neither is better in general. A separable field $g(x)h(y)$ has rank one
    across the $x|y$ cut, so stacked order wins on axis-aligned structure; a
    feature along the diagonal couples $x$ and $y$ at every scale, and
    interleaving wins. On a $256\times256$ grid at tolerance $10^{-6}$ a
    Gaussian vortex needs $\chi = 7$ stacked against $33$ interleaved, a
    diagonal shear layer $143$ against $8$. Turbulence has no preferred axis,
    which is why the turbulence studies of \cref{subsec:expressivity} favour
    interleaving.
  \item A real initial condition is built with TT-cross rather than from a
    dense array, or the price the method was meant to avoid has already been
    paid.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Masking arbitrary objects.}
Immersed geometry enters as a mask that multiplies the velocity: $0$ inside the
body, $1$ in the fluid. The mask is available only as a function one can
evaluate, so its MPS is built by TT-cross~\cite{Oseledets2010}
(\cref{subsec:tt-cross-geometry}), at cost
\begin{equation}
  O(N\chi^2)\,(\text{cost of one evaluation}) + O(N\chi^3).
\end{equation}
The sharp indicator $\theta(x)$ is a poor target: a curved discontinuity
couples every scale, and at fixed rank TT-cross leaves blocky $O(1)$ errors
scattered over the body. The smoothed mask~\eqref{eq:smoothed-mask} of
Part~\partref{2}, with $q < 0$ inside the body, is exactly zero in the body and relaxes to one over a width $\sim 1/\alpha$
outside it; its approximation error is confined to a thin ring at the interface.
For a disc on a $512\times512$ grid at $\chi = 48$, the mean error against
$\theta$ is $0.026$ for the sharp mask and $0.010$ for the smoothed one, and
only the smoothed one is free of artefacts away from the interface. It is the
curvature, not the sharpness, that costs rank.

\begin{remark}[Sign convention]
  The level set $q$ must be \emph{negative} inside the solid, so that
  $q + |q| = 0$ there. Flip it and you will faithfully simulate a flow that
  exists only inside the cylinder.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Time integrator.}
The solver integrates~\eqref{eq:navier-stokes} entirely within the QTT
representation:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \textbf{finite-difference} scheme, with gradient, divergence and
    Laplacian as MPOs of rank $2$--$5$, built once in closed
    form~\cite{Kazeev2012};
  \item an \textbf{MPO encoding of open boundary conditions}: ghost-cell inlet
    and outlet conditions live inside the operators;
  \item \textbf{Chorin's projection method}~\cite{Chorin1968} --- predict and
    correct;
  \item a \textbf{DMRG-based divergence-free projection}: a linear system,
    solved as in \cref{subsec:linear-solvers}, at worst-case cost $O(N\chi^6)$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
One Chorin step reads
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{v}^{*}_{t+\Delta t} &= \mathbf{v}_t
    + \left(-(\mathbf{v}_t\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_t
    + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf{v}_t\right)\Delta t,
    \label{eq:chorin-predictor}\\
  \nabla^2 p_{t+\Delta t} &= \frac{\varrho}{\Delta t}\,
    \nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}^{*}_{t+\Delta t},
    \label{eq:chorin-poisson}\\
  \mathbf{v}_{t+\Delta t} &= \mathbf{v}^{*}_{t+\Delta t}
    - \frac{\Delta t}{\varrho}\nabla p_{t+\Delta t}.
    \label{eq:chorin-projection}
\end{align}
The \textbf{predictor}~\eqref{eq:chorin-predictor} ignores pressure and is
generally not divergence-free. The \textbf{pressure
Poisson}~\eqref{eq:chorin-poisson} is the DMRG solve and the expensive step.
The \textbf{projection}~\eqref{eq:chorin-projection} subtracts the pressure
gradient to land back on the divergence-free manifold. The mask is applied
elementwise (one Hadamard product) after each step.

\paragraph{Why pressure is the hard part.}
\margintext{This is not a tensor-network problem. The globally coupled elliptic
solve dominates runtime in classical incompressible CFD too, which is what makes
the comparison in \cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-results} a fair one.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pressure is \textbf{not a dynamical variable}. There is no evolution
    equation for it in~\eqref{eq:navier-stokes}: it is whatever instantaneously
    enforces $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$, a constraint rather than a dynamics.
  \item Taking the divergence of the momentum equation and using
    $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$ leaves an elliptic \textbf{Poisson problem},
    \begin{equation}
      \nabla^2 p = -\varrho\, \nabla\cdot\!\left[(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\right],
      \label{eq:pressure-poisson}
    \end{equation}%
    whose right-hand side is the only place the nonlinearity enters.
  \item The Green's function of $\nabla^2$ has infinite support, so
    information propagates across the whole mesh within a single step. Every
    timestep therefore contains a globally coupled linear solve, and that is
    where the runtime goes --- in classical CFD and in tensor networks alike.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}
  Steps~\eqref{eq:chorin-poisson}--\eqref{eq:chorin-projection} are a
  Helmholtz--Hodge decomposition: any vector field splits uniquely into a
  divergence-free part and a gradient, and the Poisson solve is how you find
  the gradient part in order to discard it.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Which Laplacian goes in the Poisson step]
  \margintext{Costs a day to find, ten seconds to fix, and looks exactly like
  \enquote{the solver is a bit inaccurate} the whole time.}
  The operator in~\eqref{eq:chorin-poisson} must be $\nabla\cdot\nabla$
  \emph{assembled from the same difference operators} used for the gradient
  in~\eqref{eq:chorin-projection} and the divergence on the right-hand side.
  Substitute the compact three-point Laplacian --- the obvious thing to reach
  for, since \cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators} built it and it has a lower
  MPO rank --- and the projection no longer projects.

  The algebra is one line. With $D$ the discrete gradient,
  $D\cdot\mathbf{v}_{t+\Delta t} = D\cdot\mathbf{v}^{*} - \Delta t\,(D\cdot
  D)p$, which vanishes identically only if the operator inverted was $D\cdot D$.
  With centred differences $D\cdot D \ne L$, and the residual divergence is
  $O(1)$ rather than $O(\text{solver tolerance})$. Measured on one step of our
  demonstrator: $\norm{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}}$ falls from $7.7$ to
  $4\times10^{-3}$ when the operators match, and by a factor of a few when they
  do not.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The nonlinearity multiplies ranks]
  The advection term is the only place where ranks multiply rather than add: a
  Hadamard product of trains of ranks $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$ has rank
  $\chi_1\chi_2$ before rounding (in the demonstrator, $u$ of rank $6$ times
  $\partial_x u$ of rank $9$ gives $54$, rounded back to $8$). The advective
  form $u\,\partial_x u$ and the conservative form
  $\tfrac12\partial_x(u^2)$ cost the same, agree in exact arithmetic, and
  differ under truncation, because one rounds before differentiating and the
  other after.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Recover the solution.}
The fifth step is what makes the method usable rather than merely fast. The
dense $2^N$ vector is \textbf{never reconstructed}; instead the MPS is queried
as an oracle:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{coarse-grained evaluation}, $O(N\chi^3)$: contract the finest
    legs against $(1/2, 1/2)^T$, which is exactly average pooling, and look at a
    coarser picture of the field;
  \item \textbf{pixel sampling}, $O(N_{\text{samples}}\chi^3)$: draw grid points
    $\mathbf{i} \sim P(\mathbf{i}) \propto |v_{\mathbf{i}}|^2$ bit by bit from
    conditional marginals, exactly as one samples a quantum state. The samples
    concentrate where the field (for instance the vorticity) lives;
  \item a \textbf{single grid value} costs $O(N\chi^2)$, by contracting the
    chain against the binary digits of its index.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}
  Without a compressed readout the whole advantage evaporates: you would
  compress the computation and then decompress it in order to look at it. This
  is a general lesson about compressed solvers, not a detail of this one.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Complexity of the algorithms.}
Per step and per task~\cite{Peddinti2024}:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Algorithmic task} & \textsc{Complexity} & \textsc{Runtime \%} \\
    \midrule
    Mask generation             & $O(N\chi^3)$ & offline \\
    Mask application            & $O(\chi^6)$  & $2.0$ \\
    Divergence-free projection  & $O(N\chi^6)$ & $\mathbf{80.1}$ \\
    Euler time stepping         & $O(\chi^6)$  & $17.9$ \\
    Coarse-grained evaluation   & $O(N\chi^3)$ & offline \\
    Pixel sampling (per pixel)  & $O(\chi^3)$  & offline \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent
The numerically observed total runtime is $\approx N\chi^{4.1}$: linear in $N$,
hence \textbf{poly-logarithmic in the mesh size} $2^N$. The projection dominates,
and approximate solutions of it at $\sim O(\chi^3)$ are possible.

\begin{remark}[What the teaching demonstrator reproduces, and what it does not]
  The companion solver in \texttt{notebooks/qtade\_cfd.py} is a few hundred
  lines of NumPy with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions on every wall, no
  ghost-cell inlet or outlet, and interleaved ordering; immersed geometry is
  added in the notebook by the mask~\eqref{eq:smoothed-mask}. It is not the
  code that produced the results of \cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-results}, and it is
  not validated against anything.

  What it does reproduce is the \emph{cost structure}, which is the part worth
  carrying away. On a $64\times64$ grid the pressure solve takes $80$--$85\%$ of
  the runtime, against the $80.1\%$ reported for the full solver. Scaling from
  $n=5$ to $n=8$ bits per axis multiplies the cell count by 64 and the runtime
  by about 20 --- sublinear in cells, and well above the ideal $O(N)$, the
  excess being interpreter overhead and local conjugate-gradient iterations
  rather than anything about tensor networks. Take the shape of that curve from
  the demonstrator and the constant from the paper.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Truncation does not respect the constraint]
  SVD truncation is optimal in the Frobenius norm, and the Frobenius norm knows
  nothing about $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$. Every rounding step puts a little
  divergence back and the next projection removes it again; the balance is set
  by the rank cap. Measured after 20 steps of the demonstrator:

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
      \toprule
      $\chi_{\max}$ & $\norm{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}}$ \\
      \midrule
      8  & $1.2\times10^{1}$ \\
      16 & $1.6\times10^{0}$ \\
      32 & $4.1\times10^{-2}$ \\
      48 & $5.9\times10^{-4}$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \noindent
  Structure-preserving rounding --- truncation that conserves mass, energy or
  the constraint --- is open (\cref{subsec:part2-open-problems}). Until it
  exists, $\chi_{\max}$ is the knob and the divergence is the diagnostic you
  watch. Note also that on a $64\times64$ grid a train with $\chi=32$ already
  stores more numbers than the dense field: compression is an asymptotic
  statement.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Numerical Results}
\label{subsec:qtt-cfd-results}
% Keywords: flow around a cylinder, squares, NACA airfoil, Karman street,

The framework of \cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks} was validated on laminar
benchmarks~\cite{Peddinti2024}:

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.22\textwidth}p{0.18\textwidth}p{0.48\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Case} & \textsc{Encoding} & \textsc{What it shows} \\
    \midrule
    Flow around a cylinder,
    $\mathrm{Re} = 1.7, 25, 125$
      & 15-bit MPS, $\chi = 30$
      & laminar regime: attached flow, steady vortex pair, and the K\'arm\'an
        vortex street \\
    Flow around squares,
    $\mathrm{Re} = 127, 141$
      & 15-bit MPS, $\chi = 30$
      & a collection of squares; qualitative agreement with Ansys Fluent, a
        commercial CFD solver \\
    NACA 0040 airfoil at $22^\circ$,
    $\mathrm{Re} = 750$
      & 19-bit MPS, $\chi = 45$
      & $T = 5000$ steps at $28\times$ compression; vorticity read out at full
        resolution, by coarse-grained evaluation and by pixel sampling \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Further geometries in the range $100 < \mathrm{Re} < 200$ (single and multiple
obstacles) show the same qualitative behaviour. These are laminar flows: they
test the \emph{framework} --- geometry, boundary conditions, projection,
readout --- but not yet the scale-locality argument of
\cref{subsec:why-fluid-dynamics}, which is about turbulence.

\subsection{Recent Literature}
\label{subsec:part3-literature}
% Keywords: compressible flows, TT finite elements, reacting flows, reduced-order
% models, Rayleigh--Benard, curvilinear coordinates, Vlasov, neutron transport,
% isogeometric Poisson, finance.

Since the framework is flexible, it has been carried to many other problems.
A non-exhaustive list:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Compressible flows}, with shocks and a nonlinear
    flux~\cite{Peddinti2025}.
  \item \textbf{Incompressible flow with TT finite elements}, applied to
    chemical mixers~\cite{Kornev2023}.
  \item \textbf{Reacting shear flows} in MPS form~\cite{Pinkston2025}.
  \item \textbf{Reduced-order models} for wall-bounded flows, at a few percent
    of the DNS variable count~\cite{Kiffner2023}.
  \item \textbf{2D turbulent Rayleigh--B\'enard convection}~\cite{Huelst2026},
    and a \textbf{fractional-step method in curvilinear coordinates} for
    body-fitted geometry~\cite{Huelst2025}.
  \item \textbf{Kinetic plasmas}: Vlasov--Poisson~\cite{Ye2022} and
    Vlasov--Maxwell~\cite{Ye2024} in quantized tensor networks, a
    discontinuous-Galerkin TT method for Vlasov--Maxwell~\cite{Chinomona2026},
    and a quantum algorithm for the collisional linear Vlasov
    equation~\cite{Ameri2023}.
  \item \textbf{Large-scale 3D Poisson} problems with a TT isogeometric
    solver~\cite{Tran2026}, and \textbf{neutron transport} in nuclear reactor
    problems~\cite{Ortega2026}.
  \item The quantum-resource (\enquote{magic}) content of fluid-dynamics
    data~\cite{Mello2025}.
  \item And beyond physics: full-grid multi-asset \textbf{option pricing} with
    tensor networks~\cite{Arenstein2025a}.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The common thread is that the ansatz is portable across discretizations, and
that the global solve is always the hard part.

\subsection{Toward Turbulence}
\label{subsec:expressivity}
% Keywords: statistical metrics (structure functions, energy spectrum,
% flatness, increment PDFs), intermittency, compression/evolution/synthesis of
% isotropic turbulence, stacked vs interleaved, small-scale artefacts, block
% boundaries, Hybrid-TT, open questions.

\paragraph{Yes, but.}
We started from turbulence, and the results of \cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-results}
are laminar.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It is in the \textbf{turbulent} regime that we expect a memory
    advantage, because that is where scale locality lives.
  \item The Navier--Stokes equations are dissipative, not norm preserving, and
    turbulent fields are chaotic: pointwise fidelity is the wrong yardstick.
    We need the mathematical tools that benchmark and characterize
    turbulence --- \textbf{statistical metrics}.
  \item We will still compress with the standard, $\ell_2$-optimal SVD
    truncation. Constructions tailored to the problem are an open question,
    and one answer appears at the end of this subsection.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Two sub-questions should be kept apart. How $\chi$ grows with
\textbf{resolution} at fixed physics is benign --- that is the quantics promise
of \cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}. How $\chi$ grows with
\textbf{Reynolds number} is the real question; it has no theory behind it
(\cref{subsec:part3-outlook}), and the answer is empirical.

\paragraph{Statistical metrics.}
With $\delta v(r)$ the velocity increment over a separation $r$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scale invariance} (Kolmogorov 1941) predicts the structure
    functions
    \begin{equation}
      S_p(r) = \langle \delta v(r)^p \rangle \sim \epsilon^{p/3} r^{p/3},
      \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
      E(k) \propto \epsilon^{2/3} k^{-5/3}.
      \label{eq:structure-functions}
    \end{equation}
  \item Deviations from scale invariance are \textbf{intermittency}. They
    appear as a non-constant \textbf{flatness}
    \begin{equation}
      \Gamma_p(r) \equiv \frac{S_p(r)}{S_2(r)^{p/2}},
      \label{eq:flatness}
    \end{equation}
    ($\Gamma_4 = 3$ for a Gaussian field, growing at small $r$ in turbulence),
    and as peaked, heavy-tailed \textbf{PDFs of velocity increments}
    $\delta u(r)/\sigma_{\delta u(r)}$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Compression of 3D turbulence.}
\margintext{Pisoni \emph{et al.} (2026): a forced, isotropic, periodic DNS
cube of $1024^3 = 2^{30}$ points at $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda = 315$, encoded as
30-site tensor trains. The largest such study to date, and the source of the
numbers below.}
Pisoni \emph{et al.}~\cite{Pisoni2026} take homogeneous isotropic turbulence
through compression, evolution and synthesis in QTT, and judge each by $E(k)$,
the flatness and the increment PDFs.

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llp{0.46\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    $\chi$ & \textsc{Retained} & \textsc{What survives} \\
    \midrule
    $1000$ & $2.2\%$  & the full flat inertial range of $E(k)$ \\
    $100$  & $0.03\%$ & enough to run the solver stably for 9--10 Kolmogorov
                        time units \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
That is good compression, with the energy spectrum essentially intact. But:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The spectrum is the easy diagnostic.} $E(k)$ is a second-order
    two-point statistic, and second-order statistics are exactly what an
    $\ell_2$-optimal truncation protects. It still looks fine long after the
    flow does not, so reporting only $E(k)$ overstates the quality of a
    compression.
  \item The demanding diagnostics are the \textbf{flatness} and the
    \textbf{increment PDFs}: high-order, living in the tails, where truncation
    error concentrates. There the compressed fields show an \textbf{issue with
    small-scale physics}: the flatness overshoots at small separations.
  \item \textbf{Bit ordering matters.} The two QTT orderings, stacked
    ($i_1\dots i_{N_x} j_1\dots j_{N_y} k_1\dots k_{N_z}$) and interleaved
    ($i_1 j_1 k_1 \dots i_{N_x} j_{N_y} k_{N_z}$), behave differently; the
    interleaved encoding is the one to use for turbulence, but below
    $\chi \approx 500$ it too injects spurious small-scale fluctuations.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[The ordering in which diagnostics recover]
  The claim is easy to check in two dimensions, and worth checking because the
  ordering is so lopsided. Compressing a synthetic intermittent field (a
  $k^{-5/3}$ spectrum modulated by a lognormal multiplicative cascade, true
  flatness $\Gamma_4(r{=}1) = 37.6$):

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
      \toprule
      $\chi$ & $\ell_2$ error & $E(k)$ error, $k<40$ & $\Gamma_4(r{=}1)$ \\
      \midrule
      16 & $0.61$ & $33\%$   & $119$ \\
      32 & $0.42$ & $12\%$   & $64$ \\
      64 & $0.21$ & $1.8\%$  & $44$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \noindent
  At $\chi = 64$ the spectrum is right to $2\%$ while the pointwise error is
  still $21\%$ and the flatness is $16\%$ high --- overshooting, as in 3D. The
  diagnostic that survives compression longest is therefore the one least able
  to tell you that the compression was safe. State this to anyone who shows you
  a compressed turbulent field and an $E(k)$ plot.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Evolution and synthesis.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Evolution.} A 3D TT-based solver --- initial TT snapshot,
    divergence-free projection, Euler time stepping: the loop of
    \cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks} --- runs at $\chi = 100$ for 9--10
    Kolmogorov time units, with $E(k)$ checked against the expected spectrum
    and $\norm{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}}_\infty$ monitored along the way.
  \item \textbf{Synthesis.} Turbulent snapshots can be generated
    \emph{natively} in QTT. For each split point $m \in [2, M-1]$ a train
    $G_m$ carries low-rank noise on the coarse scales $1,\dots,m$ and QTT
    interpolation (upsampling~\cite{Guzman2026}) on the fine scales
    $m+1,\dots,M$; the snapshot is $\sum_m \omega_m G_m$. The result has the
    right spectrum and flatness, with a bond dimension growing linearly in the
    number of scales $M$ --- logarithmic cost in the grid. Whatever the ansatz
    is missing, it is not the gross statistical structure of turbulence.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Open questions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Truncation versus projection}: rounding the three velocity
    components separately breaks the constraint coupling them.
  \item \textbf{Small-scale overshooting} for strong compressions.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What actually happens when we compress.}
Turbulence statistics are about the standard deviation of the increments,
i.e.\ about the small scales. SVD truncation of a QTT smooths the field --- but
not uniformly. Within each dyadic block fine-scale information is discarded;
at the \textbf{block boundaries} the compressed field jumps. A 1D experiment
makes it visible: a rough signal on $2^{12}$ points compressed to $\chi = 8$
loses more than half of the standard deviation of its derivative, and the
derivative error on the boundaries of blocks of $256$ points is $2.5$ times its
value elsewhere. Smoothing in the bulk plus spikes at the boundaries is exactly
the wrong small-scale statistics measured above.

\paragraph{A fix: Hybrid-TT.}
\margintext{arXiv:2608.00194 (2026), \enquote{Multiscale passive scalar
turbulence in a compressed subspace via tensor trains}.}
For a 2D passive scalar advected by a forced 2D Navier--Stokes flow,
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t\theta + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\theta = \kappa\Delta\theta + f_\theta,
  \qquad
  \partial_t\mathbf{v} + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}
    = -\nabla p + \nu\Delta\mathbf{v} - \beta\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{f},
\end{equation}
(DNS at $4096^2$), the \textbf{Hybrid-TT} compression keeps a \textbf{dense head
tensor} of dimension $2^{2L_h}$ that retains the large scales exactly, followed
by a tensor train for the small ones, and uses \textbf{native QTT interpolation
at the block boundaries}. At $1.3\%$ of the original size it reproduces $E(k)$,
$S_4(r)$, the flatness $S_4/S_2^2$ and the increment PDFs at small $r$ ---
better than Fourier (Galerkin) or wavelet truncation at the same size, where the
standard TT overshoots.

\begin{remark}[The honest claim]
  What the evidence supports is that turbulence compresses \emph{well at
  moderate} $\mathrm{Re}$, provided the compression is judged on the right
  statistics. What it does not support is any claim that turbulence is low-rank
  at arbitrary $\mathrm{Re}$ and at every scale. The $\chi = 100$ ceiling in the
  current 3D solver is a memory-bound implementation limit, not a physical
  statement.

  A cheap measurement makes the shape of the dependence visible without a DNS:
  synthesize fields with an inertial range of increasing width and compress
  them to a fixed tolerance. With $k_{\max} = 4, 8, 16, 32, 64$ the bond
  dimension needed at $10^{-2}$ goes $14, 26, 41, 68, 128$. The rank tracks the
  \emph{width of the inertial range}, which is the thing $\mathrm{Re}$ controls
  --- not the grid, which is the thing quantics already handles.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{More on turbulence.}
Two-dimensional turbulence on GPUs~\cite{Hoelscher2025}; turbulence probability
distributions computed directly in tensor-network form, rather than by sampling
a reconstructed field~\cite{Gourianov2025}; and an a priori assessment of
tensor-network encodings for isotropic turbulence~\cite{Esmaeili2026}.

\subsection{Outlook}
\label{subsec:part3-outlook}
% Keywords: scaling to 3D/turbulent regimes, solvers, adaptivity,
% structure-preserving truncation, theory gap, quantum hand-off.

\paragraph{Solvers and scale.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Higher $\mathrm{Re}$, full 3D.} Lifting the $\chi = 100$ ceiling
    is an engineering problem before it is a physics one.
  \item \textbf{Better solvers.} TT preconditioning, distributed and GPU sweeps,
    mixed precision. The Poisson solve is $80\%$ of runtime, so nothing else is
    worth optimizing first.
  \item \textbf{Adaptivity.} Choose $\chi(t)$ and $\Delta t$ from a live error
    estimate rather than a fixed cap; tangent-space integrators supply the
    machinery~\cite{Lubich2015}.
  \item \textbf{Structure-preserving and physics-aware truncation.} Maintain
    $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$ and the conserved quantities under rounding,
    and compress with respect to the statistics that matter rather than the
    $\ell_2$ norm.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Physics and theory.}
\margintext{The theory gap is the takeaway for an applied-mathematics audience:
there is at present no sharp rank bound for Navier--Stokes solutions as a
function of $\mathrm{Re}$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Compressible and multiphysics.} Shocks, reacting flows, MHD ---
    regimes where rank behaviour is essentially unmapped.
  \item \textbf{The theory gap.} No sharp rank bounds for Navier--Stokes
    solutions as a function of $\mathrm{Re}$. Contrast this with
    \cref{subsec:mps}, where the one-dimensional area law~\cite{Hastings2007}
    gives exactly such a bound for quantum ground states.
  \item \textbf{Data-driven methods.} Learned closures, learned
    preconditioners, tensor-network surrogates --- which is Part~\partref{4}.
  \item \textbf{Quantum hand-off.} The same MPS encoding is the natural
    interface to future quantum CFD algorithms.
\end{itemize}
