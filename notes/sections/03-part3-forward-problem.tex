Part~\partref{2} ended on nonlinearity. A product of two tensor trains has the
product of their bond dimensions, every Picard or Newton iteration rounds again,
and nobody yet understands how iteration error and truncation error interact
(\cref{subsec:part2-open-problems}). Part~\partref{3} meets that problem where
it matters most: the incompressible Navier--Stokes equations. Fluid dynamics
provokes every weakness of the toolkit at once --- a nonlinear advection term, a
global constraint enforced by the pressure, complicated geometry and, above all,
turbulence --- and it is also the problem for which the course's single idea
makes its sharpest prediction. Low rank means weak coupling between scales, and
turbulence is the regime in which the coupling between scales \emph{is} the
physics.

\paragraph{Pipeline stages covered.}
All five. The equation is Navier--Stokes; the formulation is finite
differences on a uniform grid with Chorin's projection method; the
tensor-network encoding covers the velocity field, the immersed-body mask and the
operators with their boundary conditions; time evolution combines an explicit
predictor with an implicit pressure solve; and retrieval reads the flow out of
the compressed state without decompressing it.

The argument runs as follows. \Cref{subsec:why-fluid-dynamics} states why fluids
are the right test of the ansatz. \Cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks} builds the
solver one pipeline stage at a time, and \cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-results} validates
it on laminar flows. \Cref{subsec:expressivity} then asks the question the
course hinges on: does the rank stay small for turbulent flows, and how would we
tell? \Cref{subsec:part3-outlook} collects the open problems and related work.

\subsection{Why Fluid Dynamics?}
\label{subsec:why-fluid-dynamics}
% Keywords: which PDE to solve, rank as inter-scale correlation, Navier--Stokes,
% Reynolds number, turbulence and the energy cascade, number of scales, DNS cost,
% scale locality, Gourianov et al. (2022).

\paragraph{Which PDE to solve.}
\margintext{In a chain of quantum spins the bond dimension measures
entanglement, and the area law is why ground states are cheap to store. The
quantics chain has the same mathematics with a different meaning: its sites are
length scales, not particles.}
The choice is not arbitrary, because a quantics tensor train compresses
information in a particular way. Its cores are the binary digits of the grid
index, that is, \textbf{length scales} (\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}),
so its bond dimension measures \textbf{inter-scale correlation}.
The right question is therefore which physics has weak
correlations between scales. For fluid flows that leads straight to turbulence,
with its scale invariance and scale locality --- and turbulent regimes are
precisely the computational bottleneck of classical CFD.

\paragraph{The Navier--Stokes equations.}
\label{subsec:navier-stokes}
The incompressible Navier--Stokes equations read
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}
    = -\frac{1}{\varrho}\nabla p + \nu \nabla^2 \mathbf{v},
  \qquad
  \nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0 .
  \label{eq:navier-stokes}
\end{equation}
Each term is a modelling choice. The \textbf{advection} term
$(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}$ is the nonlinearity. The \textbf{density}
$\varrho$ is constant, which is what incompressible means here, as opposed to
compressible flow. The \textbf{dissipation} term $\nu\nabla^2\mathbf{v}$ makes the
flow viscous; $\nu = 0$ is the inviscid Euler limit. The
\textbf{divergence-free condition} $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$ is
incompressibility itself. Once~\eqref{eq:navier-stokes} is nondimensionalized,
exactly one control parameter survives, the Reynolds number
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Re} \sim \frac{\text{advection}}{\text{dissipation}}
    \sim \frac{UL}{\nu},
\end{equation}
with $U$ and $L$ a characteristic velocity and length. At small $\mathrm{Re}$
the flow is \textbf{laminar}: flow past a cylinder is attached, then forms a
steady vortex pair, then sheds a K\'arm\'an vortex street. At $\mathrm{Re} \gg 1$
it is \textbf{turbulent}.

From a tensor-network point of view, each ingredient is awkward in a
\emph{different} way:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{nonlinear advection} is a product of tensor trains, so ranks
    multiply;
  \item \textbf{the pressure constraint} is nonlocal: a global elliptic solve
    at every step;
  \item \textbf{boundary conditions on complex geometry}: curved interfaces
    couple every length scale at once, hence high rank.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent
\Cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks} deals with each in turn.

\paragraph{Turbulence.}
\margintext{Richardson (1922): \enquote{Big whorls have little whorls that feed
on their velocity, and little whorls have lesser whorls and so on to
viscosity.}}
In Richardson's picture of the \textbf{cascade}, energy is injected at the large
scale $L$, handed down through a hierarchy of eddies, and dissipated into heat at
the smallest scale. Kolmogorov (1941) added that in the \textbf{inertial range}
between the injection and the dissipative scales the cascade is
\textbf{self-similar}. With $\mathcal{E}$ the mean rate of energy dissipation per
unit mass, the energy spectrum is then
$E(k) \propto \mathcal{E}^{2/3} k^{-5/3}$~\cite{Kolmogorov1941,Frisch1995}, and
energy transfer is dominated by \textbf{neighbouring scales}: the coupling is
strongest between eddies of similar size. The dissipative (Kolmogorov) scale is
$\eta \sim L\,\mathrm{Re}^{-3/4}$, so a $D$-dimensional simulation needs
$(L/\eta)^D \sim \mathrm{Re}^{3D/4}$ grid points to resolve both ends of the
cascade.

In quantics the relevant count is not the number of grid points but the number
of binary \textbf{scales} per axis, $n = \log_2(L/\eta) \approx \tfrac34
\log_2\mathrm{Re}$:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccc@{}}
    \toprule
    $\mathrm{Re}$ & $10^4$ & $10^5$ & $10^6$ & $10^7$ & $10^8$ & $10^9$ \\
    \midrule
    \# scales $n$ & $10$ & $12$--$13$ & $15$ & $17$--$18$ & $20$ & $22$--$23$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Direct numerical simulation.}
Direct numerical simulation (DNS) resolves every scale, so in three dimensions
the number of grid points grows like $\mathrm{Re}^{9/4}$, the exponential wall
of \cref{subsec:numerical-methods-intro} in its fluid-dynamical form. A typical
airfoil simulation sits at $\mathrm{Re} \sim 2\times10^6$--$2\times10^7$, that is
$10^{13}$--$10^{16}$ grid points, and exascale machines still cannot simulate a
full aircraft at flight Reynolds number. Practical CFD --- flow prediction, shape
optimization, forecasting --- therefore runs on expertise: mesh generation,
boundary conditions, the choice of finite differences, elements or volumes, the
time-evolution scheme, and phenomenological \textbf{models} (RANS, LES) whose
tuned closures transfer poorly between regimes.

\paragraph{The bet.}
The argument for tensor trains is short. Quantics cores represent scales;
turbulence is scale-local and scale-invariant; so its fields may have low rank.
If the correlations among scales are mostly local, the memory cost is
$O(n\chi^2)$, \textbf{logarithmic in the number of grid points}. The table above
makes the contrast concrete: $10^{16}$ grid points, but only about twenty scales
per axis.

Gourianov \emph{et al.}~\cite{Gourianov2022} were the first to test this
quantitatively. They measured the bond dimension at every bipartition of the QTT
of DNS snapshots (temporally developing jets in 2D, Taylor--Green vortices in
3D), found it bounded and far below the maximal rank, and ran the incompressible
Navier--Stokes equations directly in tensor-train form: with $\chi \approx
128$--$192$ the dynamics matched DNS, while coarser classical grids with the same
number of parameters did not.

\subsection{The Solver, Stage by Stage}
\label{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks}
% Keywords: course pipeline, QTT encoding of the flow (stacked/interleaved),
% TT-cross masks of immersed objects, MPO boundary conditions, Chorin
% projection, pressure Poisson solve and its cost, coarse-grained evaluation,
% pixel sampling.

Classical CFD codes are built from interchangeable parts. The question is
whether the same pipeline can be assembled inside the tensor-train format with
better scaling. Many groups are working on it (\cref{subsec:part3-outlook}); we
follow the framework of Peddinti \emph{et al.}~\cite{Peddinti2024} and walk
through it in the order of the course pipeline.

\paragraph{Equation and formulation.}
The equation is~\eqref{eq:navier-stokes} in two dimensions. It is discretized by
finite differences on a uniform grid of $N \times N$ points, $N = 2^n$, and
advanced in time by Chorin's projection method~\cite{Chorin1968}, which splits
each step into an explicit velocity update and a pressure correction. Inlet and
outlet are open boundaries; immersed bodies impose no-slip.

\paragraph{Encoding the velocity field.}
\margintext{In quantum language each core is a qubit, the encoding is
\enquote{amplitude encoding}, and the bond dimension measures entanglement
between scales.}
Each velocity component is sampled on the grid and its values are arranged as a
tensor train over the binary digits of the grid indices,
\begin{equation}
  v(x,y) \xrightarrow{\ \text{discretize}\ } v_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}}
    = A_1^{(i_1)} \cdots A_{n_x}^{(i_{n_x})}\,
      B_1^{(j_1)} \cdots B_{n_y}^{(j_{n_y})},
  \label{eq:stacked-encoding}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{i} = (i_1,\dots,i_{n_x})$ and $\mathbf{j}=(j_1,\dots,j_{n_y})$ the
binary digits of the two grid indices. The first bit $i_1$ selects the left or
right half of the domain, $i_2$ the quarter within it, and so on: on an
$8\times4$ grid the grid point $(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}) = (110, 10)$ is the entry
with multi-index $11010$. Cores correspond to scales, and the bond dimension
measures how strongly those scales are coupled. Storage drops from
$O(2^{n_x+n_y})$ to $O((n_x+n_y)\chi^2)$; with $n_x = n_y = n$ that is
$O(n\chi^2)$, and costs below are written in $n$ with constant factors dropped.

Equation~\eqref{eq:stacked-encoding} is the \textbf{stacked} order used
in~\cite{Peddinti2024}: all $x$ bits, then all $y$ bits. The
\textbf{interleaved} order $i_1 j_1 i_2 j_2\dots$ instead keeps the $x$ and $y$
bits of the same scale adjacent along the chain, and the companion code uses it
by default. Neither is better in general. A separable field $g(x)h(y)$ has rank
one across the $x|y$ cut, so stacked order wins on axis-aligned structure; a
feature along the diagonal couples $x$ and $y$ at every scale, and interleaving
wins. On a $256\times256$ grid at tolerance $10^{-6}$ a Gaussian vortex needs
$\chi = 7$ stacked against $33$ interleaved, and a diagonal shear layer $143$
against $8$. Turbulence has no preferred axis, which is why the turbulence
studies of \cref{subsec:expressivity} favour interleaving. A realistic initial
condition is built by TT-cross rather than from a dense array; otherwise the
price the method was meant to avoid has already been paid.

\paragraph{Encoding the geometry.}
Immersed geometry enters as a mask that multiplies the velocity: $0$ inside the
body, $1$ in the fluid. The mask is available only as a function that can be
evaluated, so its tensor train is built by TT-cross~\cite{Oseledets2010}
(\cref{subsec:tt-cross-geometry}), at cost
\begin{equation}
  O(n\chi^2)\,(\text{cost of one evaluation}) + O(n\chi^3).
\end{equation}
The solver uses the smoothed mask~\eqref{eq:smoothed-mask} of
Part~\partref{2} rather than the sharp indicator $\theta(x)$. At fixed rank,
TT-cross of the sharp indicator leaves blocky $O(1)$ errors scattered over the
body, whereas the error of the smoothed mask is confined to a thin ring at the
interface. For a disc on a $512\times512$ grid at $\chi = 48$, the mean error
against $\theta$ is $0.026$ for the sharp mask and $0.010$ for the smoothed one,
and only the smoothed one is free of artefacts away from the interface. It is
the curvature, not the sharpness, that costs rank.

Applying the mask is one \textbf{elementwise (Hadamard) product} with the
velocity field per step. No-slip is thus enforced without meshing the body and
without an immersed-boundary force term.

\begin{remark}[Sign convention]
  The level set $q$ in~\eqref{eq:smoothed-mask} must be \emph{negative} inside
  the solid, so that $q + |q| = 0$ there. Flip it and you will faithfully
  simulate a flow that exists only inside the cylinder.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Encoding the operators and boundary conditions.}
Gradient, divergence and Laplacian are finite-difference MPOs of rank $2$--$5$,
built once in closed form~\cite{Kazeev2012}. The open inlet and outlet
conditions are folded into these MPOs through \textbf{ghost cells}, so the
boundary conditions live inside the operators and the open boundaries keep the
computational domain small.

\paragraph{Time evolution.}
One Chorin step reads
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{v}^{*} &= \mathbf{v}^{k}
    + \left(-(\mathbf{v}^{k}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}^{k}
    + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf{v}^{k}\right)\Delta t,
    \label{eq:chorin-predictor}\\
  \nabla^2 p^{k+1} &= \frac{\varrho}{\Delta t}\,
    \nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}^{*},
    \label{eq:chorin-poisson}\\
  \mathbf{v}^{k+1} &= \mathbf{v}^{*}
    - \frac{\Delta t}{\varrho}\nabla p^{k+1}.
    \label{eq:chorin-projection}
\end{align}
The \textbf{predictor}~\eqref{eq:chorin-predictor} ignores pressure, and its
result is in general not divergence-free. The \textbf{pressure
Poisson equation}~\eqref{eq:chorin-poisson} is a linear solve. The
\textbf{projection}~\eqref{eq:chorin-projection} subtracts the pressure gradient
to return to the divergence-free fields. The mask is applied after each step,
and the loop is repeated for $K$ steps.

The predictor is explicit, which needs a word after Part~\partref{2}'s case for
implicit time stepping (\cref{subsec:heat-implicit-euler}). That case rested on
the diffusive stability bound $\Delta t \lesssim \Delta x^2$, which with
$\Delta x = 2^{-n}$ demands $4^n$ steps. In the predictor the viscous term
imposes $\Delta t \lesssim \Delta x^2/\nu$ and the advective term
$\Delta t \lesssim \Delta x/\abs{\mathbf{v}}$. While the grid spacing stays above
$\nu/\abs{\mathbf{v}}$, the advective bound is the tighter of the two, and it
requires only $2^n$ steps, not $4^n$. On finer grids the viscous bound takes
over again, and with it the case for treating the viscous term implicitly. The stiffest part of the
problem, the pressure constraint, which acts instantaneously across the whole
domain, is not stepped explicitly at all: it is handled by the implicit Poisson
solve.

\begin{remark}[The nonlinearity multiplies ranks]
  The advection term is the only place where ranks multiply rather than add: a
  Hadamard product of trains of ranks $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$ has rank
  $\chi_1\chi_2$ before rounding (in the demonstrator, $u$ of rank $6$ times
  $\partial_x u$ of rank $9$ gives $54$, rounded back to $8$). The advective
  form $u\,\partial_x u$ and the conservative form
  $\tfrac12\partial_x(u^2)$ cost the same, agree in exact arithmetic, and
  differ under truncation, because one rounds before differentiating and the
  other after.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{The pressure step.}
Pressure is \textbf{not a dynamical variable}. There is no evolution equation
for it in~\eqref{eq:navier-stokes}; it is whatever instantaneously enforces
$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$, a constraint rather than a dynamics. Taking the
divergence of the momentum equation and using $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$
leaves an elliptic \textbf{Poisson problem},
\begin{equation}
  \nabla^2 p = -\varrho\, \nabla\cdot\!\left[(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\right],
  \label{eq:pressure-poisson}
\end{equation}
whose right-hand side is the only place the nonlinearity enters. The Green's
function of $\nabla^2$ has infinite support, so information crosses the whole
grid within a single step, and every time step therefore contains a globally
coupled linear solve. In the tensor-train solver that solve,
\eqref{eq:chorin-poisson}, is done by a DMRG-type sweeping solver
(\cref{subsec:linear-solvers}). This is not a peculiarity of tensor networks:
the globally coupled elliptic solve dominates the runtime of classical
incompressible CFD as well.

Steps~\eqref{eq:chorin-poisson}--\eqref{eq:chorin-projection} together are a
Helmholtz--Hodge decomposition. Any vector field splits uniquely into a
divergence-free part and a gradient, and the Poisson solve is how the gradient
part is found in order to discard it.

\begin{remark}[Which Laplacian goes in the Poisson step]
  The operator in~\eqref{eq:chorin-poisson} must be $\nabla\cdot\nabla$
  \emph{assembled from the same difference operators} used for the gradient
  in~\eqref{eq:chorin-projection} and the divergence on the right-hand side.
  Substitute the compact three-point Laplacian --- the obvious choice, since
  \cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators} built it and it has a lower MPO rank ---
  and the projection no longer projects.

  The algebra is one line. With $D$ the discrete gradient,
  $D\cdot\mathbf{v}^{k+1} = D\cdot\mathbf{v}^{*} - \Delta t\,(D\cdot D)p$ (with
  $\varrho = 1$), which vanishes identically only if the operator inverted was
  $D\cdot D$. With centred differences $D\cdot D \ne L$, and the residual
  divergence is $O(1)$ rather than $O(\text{solver tolerance})$. Measured on one
  step of the demonstrator: $\norm{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}}$ falls from $7.7$ to
  $4\times10^{-3}$ when the operators match, and only by a factor of a few when
  they do not. The symptom looks like a slightly inaccurate solver, which is
  what makes the error hard to find.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{What the solve costs.}
The cost of each task per time step, and its share of the measured runtime,
are~\cite{Peddinti2024}:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Algorithmic task} & \textsc{Complexity} & \textsc{Runtime \%} \\
    \midrule
    Mask generation             & $O(n\chi^3)$ & offline \\
    Mask application            & $O(\chi^6)$  & $2.0$ \\
    Divergence-free projection  & $O(n\chi^6)$ & $\mathbf{80.1}$ \\
    Euler time stepping         & $O(\chi^6)$  & $17.9$ \\
    Coarse-grained evaluation   & $O(n\chi^3)$ & offline \\
    Sampling (per grid point)   & $O(\chi^3)$  & offline \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent
The pressure projection, that is, the Poisson solve, takes $80.1\%$ of the
runtime. Its worst-case cost $O(n\chi^6)$ assumes that the local problem is
solved to full accuracy at maximum rank on every core of every sweep. The
observed total runtime is $\approx n\chi^{4.1}$: linear in $n$, hence
\textbf{polylogarithmic in the number of grid points}. The gap between the
exponents $6$ and $4.1$ is where the practical settings matter: the number of
sweeps, the local tolerance, the rank cap and, above all, \textbf{warm-starting
from the previous time step}, which means the worst case is almost never
reached. Approximate solutions of the projection at $\sim O(\chi^3)$ are also
possible.

\paragraph{Solution retrieval.}
The last stage is what makes the method usable rather than merely fast. The
dense vector of $2^{2n}$ entries is \textbf{never reconstructed}; instead the
tensor train is queried for what is needed:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{coarse-grained evaluation}, $O(n\chi^3)$: contract the finest
    cores against $(1/2, 1/2)^T$, which is exactly average pooling, and look at a
    coarser picture of the field;
  \item \textbf{sampling}, $O(\chi^3)$ per sample: draw grid points
    $\mathbf{i} \sim P(\mathbf{i}) \propto |v_{\mathbf{i}}|^2$ bit by bit from
    conditional marginals, so that the samples concentrate where the field (for
    instance the vorticity) is large;
  \item a \textbf{single grid value}, $O(n\chi^2)$: contract the chain against
    the binary digits of its index.
\end{itemize}
\margintext{Sampling bit by bit from conditional marginals is how one measures a
quantum state, one qubit at a time.}
Without a compressed readout the whole advantage disappears: the computation
would be compressed only to be decompressed for inspection. This is a general
lesson about compressed solvers, not a detail of this one.

\paragraph{The published solver.}
The framework of \cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks} was validated on laminar

  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.22\textwidth}p{0.18\textwidth}p{0.48\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textsc{Case} & \textsc{Encoding} & \textsc{What it shows} \\
    \midrule

    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Further geometries in the range $100 < \mathrm{Re} < 200$ (single and multiple
obstacles) show the same qualitative behaviour.

\paragraph{The course demonstrator.}
The companion solver in \texttt{notebooks/qtade\_cfd.py} is a few hundred lines
of NumPy with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions on every wall, no ghost-cell inlet
or outlet, and interleaved ordering; immersed geometry is added in the notebook
through the mask~\eqref{eq:smoothed-mask}. It is not the code that produced the
results above, and it is not validated against anything. What it does reproduce
is the \emph{cost structure}. On a $64\times64$ grid the pressure solve takes
$80$--$85\%$ of the runtime, in line with the published share. Going from $n=5$
to $n=8$ multiplies the number of grid points by $64$ and the runtime by about
$20$. That is sublinear in the number of grid points, as it should be, but far
above the factor $8/5$ that an ideal $O(n)$ cost would give; the excess is
interpreter overhead and local conjugate-gradient iterations, not anything
intrinsic to tensor networks. The shape of the scaling curve can be taken from
the demonstrator, the constant from the paper.

\paragraph{Truncation and the constraint.}
SVD truncation is optimal in the Frobenius norm, and the Frobenius norm knows
nothing about $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$. Every rounding step puts a little
divergence back, and the next projection removes it again; the balance is set by
the rank cap. After 20 steps of the demonstrator:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
    \toprule
    $\chi_{\max}$ & $\norm{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}}$ \\
    \midrule
    8  & $1.2\times10^{1}$ \\
    16 & $1.6\times10^{0}$ \\
    32 & $4.1\times10^{-2}$ \\
    48 & $5.9\times10^{-4}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent
Until structure-preserving rounding exists (\cref{subsec:part3-outlook}),
$\chi_{\max}$ is the control and the divergence is the diagnostic to watch.
Note also that on a $64\times64$ grid a train with $\chi=32$ already stores more
numbers than the dense field: compression is an asymptotic statement.

\paragraph{What validation does and does not show.}
These are laminar flows. They test the \emph{framework} --- geometry, boundary
conditions, projection, readout --- but not yet the scale-locality argument of
\cref{subsec:why-fluid-dynamics}, which is about turbulence.

\subsection{Turbulence: How Far the Ranks Stretch}
\label{subsec:expressivity}
% Keywords: statistical metrics (structure functions, energy spectrum,
% flatness, increment PDFs), intermittency, compression/evolution/synthesis of
% isotropic turbulence, stacked vs interleaved, small-scale artefacts, block
% boundaries, Hybrid-TT, rank vs inertial range, open questions.

\paragraph{From laminar to turbulent flow.}
The argument started from turbulence, but the results of
\cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-results} are laminar. A memory advantage is expected in the
\textbf{turbulent} regime, because that is where scale locality lives. Turbulent
fields are chaotic, however, and the Navier--Stokes equations are dissipative
rather than norm preserving, so pointwise fidelity is the wrong yardstick. The
tools that characterize turbulence are \textbf{statistical metrics}, and they are
what a compressed turbulent field must be judged by. Compression itself still
uses the standard, $\ell_2$-optimal SVD truncation; one construction tailored to
the problem appears later in this subsection.

Two questions should be kept apart. How $\chi$ grows with \textbf{resolution} at
fixed physics is benign: that is the quantics promise of
\cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}. How $\chi$ grows with \textbf{Reynolds
number} is the real question. It has no theory behind it
(\cref{subsec:part3-outlook}), so the answer is empirical.

\paragraph{Statistical metrics.}
Let $\delta v(r)$ be the velocity increment over a separation $r$.
Kolmogorov's scale invariance predicts the structure functions
\begin{equation}
  S_p(r) = \langle \delta v(r)^p \rangle \sim \mathcal{E}^{p/3} r^{p/3},
  \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
  E(k) \propto \mathcal{E}^{2/3} k^{-5/3}.
  \label{eq:structure-functions}
\end{equation}
Deviations from scale invariance are called \textbf{intermittency}. They appear
as a non-constant \textbf{flatness}
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma_p(r) \equiv \frac{S_p(r)}{S_2(r)^{p/2}},
  \label{eq:flatness}
\end{equation}
with $\Gamma_4 = 3$ for a Gaussian field and growing at small $r$ in
turbulence, and as peaked, heavy-tailed \textbf{probability density functions
(PDFs) of velocity increments} $\delta v(r)/\sigma_{\delta v(r)}$.

\paragraph{Compression of 3D turbulence.}
Pisoni \emph{et al.}~\cite{Pisoni2026} take homogeneous isotropic turbulence
through compression, evolution and synthesis in tensor-train form, the largest
such study to date. Their data is a forced, isotropic, periodic DNS on a
$1024^3 = 2^{30}$ grid, encoded as 30-core tensor trains, at
$\mathrm{Re}_\lambda = 315$. Here $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda = u'\lambda/\nu$ is the
Reynolds number built on the root-mean-square velocity $u'$ and the Taylor
microscale $\lambda$, the standard measure for isotropic turbulence. Time is
measured in Kolmogorov time units $\tau_\eta = (\nu/\mathcal{E})^{1/2}$, the
turnover time of the smallest eddies. Each result is judged by $E(k)$, the
flatness and the increment PDFs.

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llp{0.46\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    $\chi$ & \textsc{Retained} & \textsc{What survives} \\
    \midrule
    $1000$ & $2.2\%$  & the full flat inertial range of $E(k)$ \\
    $100$  & $0.03\%$ & enough to run the solver stably for $9$--$10\,\tau_\eta$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
That is good compression, with the energy spectrum essentially intact. The
spectrum, however, is the easy diagnostic. $E(k)$ is a second-order two-point
statistic, and second-order statistics are exactly what an $\ell_2$-optimal
truncation protects, so it still looks right long after the flow does not;
reporting only $E(k)$ overstates the quality of a compression. The demanding
diagnostics are the \textbf{flatness} and the \textbf{increment PDFs}, which are
high-order and live in the tails, where truncation error concentrates. There
the compressed fields show a defect in the \textbf{small-scale physics}: the
flatness overshoots at small separations. Bit ordering matters as well. The
stacked order ($i_1\dots i_n\, j_1\dots j_n\, k_1\dots k_n$) and the interleaved
order ($i_1 j_1 k_1 \dots i_n j_n k_n$) behave differently; the interleaved
encoding is the one to use for turbulence, but below $\chi \approx 500$ it too
injects spurious small-scale fluctuations.

\begin{remark}[The ordering in which diagnostics recover]
  The claim is easy to check in two dimensions, and worth checking because the
  ordering is so lopsided. Compressing a synthetic intermittent field (a
  $k^{-5/3}$ spectrum modulated by a lognormal multiplicative cascade, true
  flatness $\Gamma_4(r{=}1) = 37.6$):

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
      \toprule
      $\chi$ & $\ell_2$ error & $E(k)$ error, $k<40$ & $\Gamma_4(r{=}1)$ \\
      \midrule
      16 & $0.61$ & $33\%$   & $119$ \\
      32 & $0.42$ & $12\%$   & $64$ \\
      64 & $0.21$ & $1.8\%$  & $44$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \noindent
  At $\chi = 64$ the spectrum is right to $2\%$ while the pointwise error is
  still $21\%$ and the flatness is $16\%$ high --- overshooting, as in 3D. The
  diagnostic that survives compression longest is therefore the one least able
  to tell you that the compression was safe. Anyone shown a compressed
  turbulent field together with an $E(k)$ plot should ask for the flatness.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Evolution and synthesis.}
The same study evolves the compressed field. A 3D tensor-train solver --- an
initial snapshot, divergence-free projection and Euler time stepping, the loop
of \cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks} --- runs at $\chi = 100$ for
$9$--$10\,\tau_\eta$, with $E(k)$ checked against the expected spectrum and
$\norm{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}}_\infty$ monitored along the way.

Turbulent snapshots can also be generated \emph{natively} in tensor-train form.
For each split point $s \in [2, n-1]$ a train $G_s$ carries low-rank noise on the
coarse scales $1,\dots,s$ and QTT interpolation (upsampling~\cite{Guzman2026})
on the fine scales $s+1,\dots,n$; the snapshot is $\sum_s \omega_s G_s$. The
result has the right spectrum and flatness, with a bond dimension growing
linearly in the number of scales $n$, which is a cost logarithmic in the number
of grid points. Whatever the ansatz is missing, it is not the gross statistical
structure of turbulence.

\paragraph{What truncation does to the small scales.}
Turbulence statistics concern the standard deviation of the increments, that is,
the small scales. SVD truncation of a QTT smooths the field, but not uniformly.
Within each dyadic block fine-scale information is discarded; at the
\textbf{block boundaries} the compressed field jumps. A 1D experiment makes this
visible: a rough signal on $2^{12}$ points compressed to $\chi = 8$ loses more
than half of the standard deviation of its derivative, and the derivative error
on the boundaries of blocks of $256$ points is $2.5$ times its value elsewhere.
Smoothing in the bulk plus spikes at the boundaries is exactly the wrong
small-scale statistics measured above.

\paragraph{A remedy: Hybrid-TT.}
The \textbf{Hybrid-TT} format addresses both defects. It keeps the $L_h$
coarsest scales of each axis in a \textbf{dense head tensor}, of dimension
$2^{2L_h}$ in two dimensions, so that the large scales are retained exactly; it
represents the remaining fine scales by a tensor train; and it uses
\textbf{native QTT interpolation at the block boundaries} instead of letting the
truncated field jump there. It was tested (\enquote{Multiscale passive scalar
turbulence in a compressed subspace via tensor trains}, arXiv:2608.00194, 2026)
% TODO: replace the arXiv identifier by \cite once the entry is in bibliography.bib.
on a 2D passive scalar $\theta$ advected by a forced 2D Navier--Stokes flow,
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t\theta + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\theta = \kappa\Delta\theta + f_\theta,
  \qquad
  \partial_t\mathbf{v} + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}
    = -\nabla p + \nu\Delta\mathbf{v} - \mu\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{f},
\end{equation}
with scalar diffusivity $\kappa$, linear friction coefficient $\mu$ and forcings
$f_\theta$, $\mathbf{f}$, against a DNS at $4096^2$. At $1.3\%$ of the original
size it reproduces $E(k)$, $S_4(r)$, the flatness $S_4/S_2^2$ and the increment
PDFs at small $r$, where the standard tensor train overshoots, and it does so
better than Fourier (Galerkin) or wavelet truncation at the same size.

\paragraph{Rank versus the width of the inertial range.}
None of this yet answers how $\chi$ grows with $\mathrm{Re}$. A cheap
measurement shows the shape of the dependence without a DNS: synthesize fields
with an inertial range of increasing width and compress them to a fixed
tolerance. With spectral cut-offs $k_{\max} = 4, 8, 16, 32, 64$, the bond
dimension needed at tolerance $10^{-2}$ is $14, 26, 41, 68, 128$. The rank tracks
the \emph{width of the inertial range}, which is what $\mathrm{Re}$ controls ---
not the grid, which quantics already handles.

What the evidence supports, then, is that turbulence compresses \emph{well at
moderate} $\mathrm{Re}$, provided the compression is judged on the right
statistics. It does not support any claim that turbulence is low-rank at
arbitrary $\mathrm{Re}$ and at every scale. The $\chi = 100$ ceiling of the
current 3D solver is a memory-bound implementation limit, not a physical
statement.

\paragraph{Open questions.}
Two problems specific to turbulence remain.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Truncation versus projection.} Rounding the three velocity
    components separately breaks the constraint that couples them.
  \item \textbf{Small-scale overshoot under strong compression.} Hybrid-TT
    removes it for a 2D passive scalar; whether it does so for 3D
    Navier--Stokes at higher $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda$ is untested.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Outlook and Related Work}
\label{subsec:part3-outlook}
% Keywords: scaling to 3D/turbulent regimes, rank blow-up, solvers, adaptivity,
% structure-preserving truncation, theory gap, fair benchmarking, related work,
% quantum hand-off.

\paragraph{Open problems.}
The problems below concern the solver as a whole rather than turbulence alone.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rank blow-up.} Shocks, sharp interfaces, non-smooth data and
    high-$\mathrm{Re}$ turbulence all push $\chi$ up. Where exactly does the
    method stop paying for itself? For turbulence, lifting the $\chi = 100$
    ceiling in full 3D is an engineering problem before it is a physical one.
  \item \textbf{Better solvers.} TT preconditioning, distributed and GPU sweeps,
    mixed precision. The Poisson solve dominates the runtime
    (\cref{subsec:qtt-cfd-frameworks}), so nothing else is worth optimizing
    first.
  \item \textbf{Adaptivity.} Choose $\chi$ and $\Delta t$ at each step from a
    live error estimate rather than a fixed cap; tangent-space integrators
    supply the machinery~\cite{Lubich2015}.
  \item \textbf{Structure-preserving and physics-aware truncation.} SVD
    truncation preserves neither mass nor energy nor $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} =
    0$, because the Frobenius norm knows nothing about the physics. Rounding
    that maintains the constraint and the conserved quantities, and compresses
    with respect to the statistics that matter rather than the $\ell_2$ norm, is
    wide open.
  \item \textbf{Compressible flow and multiphysics.} Shocks, reacting flows,
    MHD: regimes where rank behaviour is essentially unmapped.
  \item \textbf{The theory gap.} There is no sharp rank bound for Navier--Stokes
    solutions as a function of $\mathrm{Re}$. Contrast \cref{subsec:qtt-functions-operators}, where
    the one-dimensional area law~\cite{Hastings2007} gives exactly such a bound
    for quantum ground states.
  \item \textbf{Fair benchmarking.} An honest comparison against tuned classical
    CFD --- multigrid, spectral methods --- on equal hardware has yet to be made.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Related work.}
The framework is flexible, and it has been carried well beyond the problems of
this part; the earliest of these works are surveyed in
\cref{subsec:literature-survey}. In fluid dynamics it now covers compressible
flows with shocks and a nonlinear flux~\cite{Peddinti2025}, incompressible flow
with TT finite elements applied to chemical mixers~\cite{Kornev2023}, reacting
shear flows~\cite{Pinkston2025}, 2D turbulent Rayleigh--B\'enard
convection~\cite{Huelst2026}, and a fractional-step method in curvilinear
coordinates for body-fitted geometry~\cite{Huelst2025}. For turbulence there are
GPU solvers for two-dimensional turbulence~\cite{Hoelscher2025} and an a priori
assessment of tensor-network encodings for isotropic
turbulence~\cite{Esmaeili2026}. Beyond fluids, kinetic plasmas have been treated
in quantized tensor networks for Vlasov--Maxwell~\cite{Ye2024}, by a
discontinuous-Galerkin TT method for Vlasov--Maxwell~\cite{Chinomona2026}, and
by a quantum algorithm for the collisional linear Vlasov
equation~\cite{Ameri2023}. Large-scale 3D Poisson problems have been solved with
a TT isogeometric solver~\cite{Tran2026}, and neutron transport in nuclear
reactor problems~\cite{Ortega2026}. One study measures how hard
fluid-dynamics data would be to prepare on a quantum computer, through its
non-stabilizer (\enquote{magic}) content~\cite{Mello2025}, and outside physics
the same techniques price multi-asset options on the full
grid~\cite{Arenstein2025a}. The common thread is that the ansatz is portable
across discretizations, and that the global solve is always the hard part.

\paragraph{Summary and hand-off.}
This part built an incompressible Navier--Stokes solver entirely in tensor-train
format, one pipeline stage at a time: a quantics encoding of the velocity, an
immersed-body mask built by TT-cross, finite-difference MPOs with the boundary
conditions inside them, an explicit predictor followed by an implicit pressure
projection, and a readout that never decompresses the state. On laminar flows
it reproduces the expected physics at modest bond dimension, and the Poisson
solve dominates its cost. On turbulence the bond dimension stays affordable at
moderate $\mathrm{Re}$ when compression is judged on the right statistics, but it
grows with the width of the inertial range, and no theory yet says how fast.
The same tensor-train encoding is also the natural interface to future quantum
CFD algorithms. Part~\partref{4} turns from equations to data: it asks how to
recover dynamics from snapshots when no equation is given, and it reuses the
solver of this part as a source of such snapshots.
